Abstract. Learning from trajectory demonstrations offers a route to active-inference control of complex systems whose dynamics are diffi-cult to model explicitly. We introduce generative active-inference control (GenAIF), in which one generative trajectory model learns from demon-strations and measured action interventions to supply a goal-conditioned policy distribution and a state-to-observation likelihood mapping. From this control design, we derive three model requirements: (i) useful ac-tion proposals, (ii) accurate prediction under imposed actions, and (iii) probabilistic observation evidence for belief updating and expected in-formation gain. We benchmark diffusion, autoregressive Transformers, conditional variational autoencoders (CVAEs), and flow matching in a MuJoCo manipulation task with multiple physical conditions. Diffusion delivers the strongest control across the tested dynamics, while CVAE combines comparable short-horizon prediction with much faster infer-ence. Correct conditioning is decisive, and trajectory reuse offers further computational savings. With the same frozen models, a hidden-dynamics experiment demonstrates prompt belief adaptation after an unannounced tilt change; subsequent instability identifies sustained inference as a re-maining challenge. These findings support the use of shared generative trajectory models to connect action proposal, controlled prediction, and observation evidence within GenAIF. 

Keywords: Active inference · Generative trajectory models · Capability benchmark · Belief adaptation 

## 1 Introduction 

People learn how to act by trying, observing, and adjusting. Watching someone learn a movement can help us adapt to the same unfamiliar physical dynamics [1]. Active Inference (AIF) offers an elegant account of how organisms bring perception and action together: an internal generative model connects hidden causes to sensory observations, and perception updates beliefs by minimizing variational free energy (VFE), an upper bound on sensory surprise [2,3]. Action selection looks ahead through expected free energy (EFE), combining preferences

for useful, safe outcomes with the information that an action is expected to reveal [5,6]. Our goal is a general framework that learns how a complex system behaves and how to control it from trajectory demonstrations. It should accommodate prior knowledge of system structure and hidden dynamics when available, and eventually learn their structure, form, and range when such knowledge is limited or absent. Realizing this goal is computationally demanding: the controller must predict uncertain consequences while searching continuous action sequences [7,8]. Learned proposals can concentrate a finite EFE-evaluation budget on useful futures. We first explored this route in autonomous parking, combining diffusion-generated strategies with AIF evaluation and observation-driven adjustment [21]. Although the approach generalized to new parking settings, its design was tailored to that task. This experience led us to ask how much of the active-inference loop a single learned trajectory model could support. 

Can a single learned generative trajectory model provide both a proposal distribution over policies and action-conditioned predictions of future observa-tions sufficient for belief updating and expected-free-energy evaluation in active inference? 

We propose generative active-inference control (GenAIF) around this shared model of action–observation trajectories. The same learned parameters support candidate generation and prediction under supplied actions; predictive evidence then supports belief updates about hidden operating conditions. The controller uses these operations to alternate between observing, updating its belief, and selecting actions according to task preferences and expected information gain. We learn the model offline from demonstrations supplemented with measured action interventions. At this stage, the hidden variables and their possible values are specified. This design leaves a concrete model-selection problem. Which capabilities must a trajectory generator provide, and which generative families can provide them together at a useful computational cost? We identify three requirements: actionable trajectory proposals, accurate prediction of observations under ex-ternally imposed actions, and predictive evidence that can distinguish hidden conditions using actual observations. A plausible generated trajectory does not by itself establish the latter two capabilities. These requirements give us a way to test our framework’s learned component and offer other researchers practical criteria for combining AIF with generative trajectory models. We design two groups of experiments in a custom MuJoCo ball-pushing environment to evaluate diffusion, autoregressive Transformers, conditional VAEs, and flow matching. The first benchmarks the required model capabilities and computational cost across different physical conditions. The second uses the same trained models to track a hidden dynamics change from replayed actions and observations, testing whether their predictive evidence remains reliable over time. At the tested settings, diffusion gives the strongest direct control, while CVAE provides similar short-horizon position prediction at lower cost. ## 3 Required Capabilities and Candidate Models 

Our finite-candidate GenAIF controller imposes three evaluation requirements. The two queries in Eq. (3) supply candidates and their predicted consequences, but using predictions for belief updating and expected information gain also requires reliable observation evidence. We therefore assess proposal usefulness, controlled prediction, and evidence quality separately; the latter two are different demands on the same predictive model. 

Action proposals. With a finite action-search budget, the proposal distribution determines which action sequences are evaluated using G. Even perfect scoring cannot select an action that was never proposed. We therefore require useful, executable action sequences. 

Controlled prediction. A proposal generated assuming a flat board must also be evaluated under tilted-board hypotheses in the current belief. This requires predicting the consequences of the same imposed actions across that belief, as in Eq. (6). Observations sampled alongside the original proposal represent only one possible future. Controlled predictions supply the outcome distribution needed to evaluate task cost, safety, and possible future observations. 

Observation evidence. A forecast must also say how strongly an observed outcome supports each hypothesis. Predictors with identical means can produce very different belief updates if their uncertainty differs. The predictive law must 6 Y. Li et al. 

provide normalized likelihoods for the VFE update after execution and expected information gain in G before execution [4]. Forecast accuracy and evidence quality therefore need separate evaluation. If hypotheses predict indistinguishable observations, the observation should not change their relative belief. These requirements motivate four candidate families whose literature supports relevant proposal, prediction, or density-evaluation operations. 

Diffusion. Diffusion models [13,17] offer precedents for trajectory planning in Diffuser and Decision Diffuser, and action generation in Diffusion Policy [16,22,15]. CSDI demonstrates completion from observed time-series coordinates [23], mo-tivating conditional prediction; score-based formulations also offer a route to likelihood evaluation [14]. 

Autoregressive Transformer. Transformers model sequential dependencies [20]. Trajectory Transformer demonstrates joint state–action modeling and plan-ning [18], while Decision Transformer supports return-conditioned action genera-tion [24]. With probabilistic outputs, an autoregressive factorization offers both forecasts conditioned on supplied actions and evaluable conditional densities, motivating prediction and observation evidence alongside proposals. 

Conditional variational autoencoder. Conditional VAEs learn context-dependent distributions through latent variables [25,12]. Play-LMP provides evidence for goal-directed action generation [26], and Trajectron++ for proba-bilistic forecasts conditioned on an ego-agent’s planned motion [27]. These support the proposal and prediction hypotheses; using such predictions as observation evidence additionally requires accounting for latent-variable uncertainty. 

Flow matching. Flow matching provides conditional sampling and, through its continuous-flow formulation, a route to density evaluation [19]. Trajectory Con-ditional Flow Matching demonstrates forecasting and goal-conditioned planning [28], while π0 generates robot action sequences [29]. These precedents motivate both prediction and proposal use. The cited studies motivate the candidates without establishing all three capabilities in one checkpoint. Conditional completion alone does not establish accurate prediction under imposed actions or reliable observation evidence. Our benchmark tests shared proposal and prediction interfaces, then evaluates their evidence for inference, alongside supplied-condition sensitivity, trajectory reuse, and computation. 

## 4 Experimental Setup 

Task. Our custom MuJoCo environment [30] requires a rectangular pusher to move a ball uphill, around an obstacle, and settle it safely in a goal region (Fig. 1). We vary lateral tilt across {− 15 , 0, +15 }◦ and use three obstacle positions, giving nine physical conditions. Longitudinal tilt stays at +20 ◦. This tests whether one model can adjust its control to different dynamics while solving the same task. An episode ends on success, ball–obstacle collision, falling off the board, or the control-step limit. Generative Trajectory Models for AIF Control 7

Actions specify planar pusher velocity and yaw rate. The observation records ball position and velocity, pusher position, and pusher yaw. Each query receives five observations and four preceding actions, together with geometry, supplied tilt, and elapsed time; prediction horizons are H ∈ { 1, 3, 6} steps. The simulator is deterministic, so repeated trials measure variation in generative sampling. 

Dataset. A feedback controller supplies expert demonstrations in every physical condition. From 216 successful reference episodes, we collect 65,195 short action interventions by restoring recorded simulator states and executing reference variants, perturbations, and exploratory actions. Demonstrations supply useful behaviour, while these interventions reveal what happens when actions depart from it. Prediction training retains unsafe outcomes; proposal training uses references and safe intentional continuations. Windows are truncated at physical termination, and padding is excluded from the loss. Training, development, calibration, and test sets are separated by source rollout: each intervention stays with its reference episode. We remove exact duplicates and compute normalization from training data only. All sets cover the same physical conditions, so evaluation asks whether the models can handle new rollouts within this task. Private simulator state is never an input. 

Training and selection. All models complete the same training-data exposure budget. We select checkpoints on development performance in proposal usefulness, controlled prediction, and observation likelihood. Each selected checkpoint serves both proposal and prediction queries and remains fixed during evaluation. Test data influence neither selection nor calibration. Model sizes share a fixed maximum parameter budget. We retain the smallest tested model when additional capacity no longer improves development perfor-mance. Diffusion continued to benefit from scaling, motivating its larger selected configuration. Training recipes also differ: diffusion’s task-directed proposal and completion examples come from successful references, while the other families additionally use safe intervention continuations. ## 5 Experiments and Results 

We evaluate the shared model in two experiment groups: the capability benchmark (E1) and the belief-tracking experiment (E2). In E1, direct execution tests proposal usefulness, imposed-action queries test prediction, and single-step condition identification tests observation evidence. We also evaluate condition sensitivity, trajectory reuse, and computation. E2 uses the same frozen checkpoints and calibration to track a hidden tilt change, with every model receiving the same executed actions and observations for each trial. 

Action generation across dynamics. We first test whether one learned model can control all nine physical conditions, using ten sampling seeds per condition for 90 episodes per setting. At each decision, it proposes one six-step trajectory, executes the first action, and replans. Tilt stays fixed within an episode. We compare correct and incorrect tilt inputs on matched scenes and seeds. This arm uses no online belief update or EFE-based candidate selection, so its success rate measures proposal usefulness under supplied conditions. Diffusion provides the most reliable control across the tested dynamics: 90% success, compared with 54% for CVAE and 48% for flow (Fig. 2a). ## 6 Discussion 

Our vision for GenAIF is to learn a system’s behaviour from rich trajectory demonstrations and use that understanding to connect inference with action. This paper takes a first step toward that vision: it derives the required model capabilities and benchmarks candidate families against them. The central conclusion is that one shared trajectory model can provide a goal-conditioned policy distribution and an approximate state-to-observation likelihood mapping, supporting action proposals, controlled prediction, and initial belief revision. These capabilities must be evaluated together: plausible trajectories and accurate forecasts alone do not establish effective control. Diffusion offers more reliable proposals in the tested setting, while CVAE provides comparable short-horizon prediction at substantially lower query cost. Their suitability for GenAIF depends on the balance between proposal quality, prediction, evidence reliability, and computation. Accurate conditioning is fundamental to using either model effectively. The earlier proposal-only experiments in Appendix A provide complementary evidence for the action-search component. The next step is to demonstrate sustained inference and the complete belief-guided control loop across a wider range of systems. We will test whether richer recovery demonstrations and better calibrated observation evidence stabilize tracking, and whether expected information gain changes actions in ways that improve control. This extends the current benchmark from component capabilities to their interaction during execution. Looking ahead, we want GenAIF to control complex industrial systems whose interacting dynamics make detailed models impractical to construct or maintain with traditional methods. Rich trajectory demonstrations would supply the main learning signal, complemented by limited prior knowledge of system structure and the form and range of hidden dynamics. A graph would encode subsystem relationships and organize learned transition models into a model of the whole system. Our aim is active-inference control that infers hidden operating conditions, anticipates consequences, and pursues specified goals while seeking information when needed. ## A Proposal Models with Simulator-Based Planning 

The main paper evaluates learned models as world models. This appendix instead tests proposal distributions with MuJoCo transitions, measuring candidate quality and finite-budget control when an accurate simulator is available. It provides practical guidance for model-based planners, but does not demonstrate learned dynamics or the complete GenAIF loop. All controllers use simulator rollouts, common preference and safety scoring, an eight-step horizon, and receding-horizon execution. The learned proposals come from a separate archive of successful RL-specialist demonstrations; their data and checkpoints differ from E1 and E2. We compare them with the cross-entropy method (CEM) [9,10]. Six scenes and three sampling seeds give eighteen episodes per family and budget. “Proposal model” pools five separately evaluated controllers with equal family weight: BC-MDN [11], CVAE, diffusion, flow, and autoregressive. Gated controllers mix learned and fallback candidates. 

Learned proposals improve control with limited search. We vary the can-didate budget while keeping rollout and scoring shared. At just eight candidates, learned proposals succeed in most trials, while CEM fails throughout (Fig. A1A). Gating raises reliability further and solves nearly every trial at the largest com-mon budget. The improvement comes from supplying useful alternatives to the planner: a good transition model alone cannot compensate for a poor candidate set. More extensive CEM search closes much of the success gap at greater planning cost (Fig. A1B). Because this configuration also doubles refinement iterations and changes the elite rule, it represents a stronger search rather than a candidate-count change alone. Learned proposals use small search budgets effectively; extensive simulator search remains a viable alternative.

The gap in total planning time is much smaller because simulator rollouts and scoring dominate the fast generators. Model selection should therefore use complete planning cost as well as proposal latency.
