# Reward-DAgger: Robot-Gated Interactive Imitation Learning with General-Purpose Progress-Based Reward Models 

Ryan Li ∗1, Yigit Korkmaz ∗2, Erdem Bıyık 21Department of Mathematics, University of Southern California

Abstract — Recent advances in robot learning have enabled generalist control policies capable of completing a wide range of tasks. However, their performance degrades when deployed in unseen environments, making it critical to detect failures and teach recovery behaviors. Existing runtime monitoring methods often require task- and policy-specific training or hyperparameter tuning, limiting cross-task deployment and introducing additional overhead during iterative policy updates. We present Reward-DAgger, a robot-gated interactive imitation learning framework that uses dense progress signals from a general-purpose reward model to determine when human intervention is needed. Our approach is agnostic to the under-lying policy architecture, requires no access to policy internals, and can be applied across tasks without retuning the gating mechanism. Our results show that Reward-DAgger achieves a better failure-detection accuracy–latency tradeoff than ex-isting runtime monitoring baselines. Across eight simulated and real-world tasks, Reward-DAgger consistently improves the downstream policy’s autonomous success rate throughout interactive learning and achieves strong return on human effort, outperforming the baselines in most settings. Importantly, the same gating configuration is used across tasks without task-specific hyperparameter tuning, demonstrating transfer across tasks, environments, and policy architectures. Code and videos are available at https://liralab.usc.edu/reward-dagger. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Modern learning-based robot policies, ranging from task-specific models [1, 2] to generalist vision-language-action (VLA) models [3, 4, 5], are increasingly capable of com-pleting challenging manipulation tasks. Yet, these policies remain brittle during deployment: a missed grasp, a dropped object, or a misaligned insertion might cause an otherwise successful rollout to fail. Interactive imitation learning meth-ods, such as DAgger [6] and its human-gated variants [7, 8, 9, 10, 11], address this problem by collecting expert inter-ventions from states where the policy struggles and using these corrections to iteratively improve the policy. However, continuously monitoring robot execution and deciding when to intervene places a substantial burden on the human expert, making such approaches difficult to scale to robot fleets. Robot-gated interactive imitation learning instead seeks to automate this decision by requesting human assistance when the policy is likely to fail, thereby making more efficient use of costly human supervision. Existing robot-gating methods, however, commonly rely on either learned failure-prediction

functions trained from explicit failure data [12] or policy-dependent uncertainty scores that require task- and policy-specific calibration [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. The former requires collecting failures that are undesirable or infeasible during safety-critical deployment, while the latter couples the gating mechanism to a particular policy checkpoint. As the policy changes throughout interactive learning, the gate needs to be retrained or recalibrated, limiting its scalability. Ideally, a scalable gating mechanism for interactive im-itation learning should satisfy four properties: (1) detect failures early while avoiding unnecessary interruptions of successful behavior; (2) remain effective across iterations of interactive learning without repeated retraining or recalibra-tion; (3) operate with different, potentially black-box, policy architectures; and (4) transfer across tasks without requiring a separate training or calibration procedure for each task. Our key insight is that general-purpose progress-based reward models, which estimate task progress directly from visual observations and natural-language instructions [18, 19, 20], provide a natural signal for such a gate. Unlike policy-dependent uncertainty measures, task progress de-scribes what the robot has accomplished rather than how the policy generates its actions. Consequently, progress can be evaluated independently of the underlying policy architecture or checkpoint. This decoupling allows the gating mechanism to be fixed as the policy is updated and to be applied to different policies without access to their internal represen-tations. Moreover, by leveraging reward models pretrained across diverse tasks, progress provides a common signal that can support gating across tasks without task-specific gate training. Importantly, even under task distribution shift, the reward model only determines when to request assistance; the policy is still trained from human corrections, which provide valid task-specific supervision. Based on this insight, we introduce Reward-DAgger ,a robot-gated interactive imitation learning framework that uses a simple but effective two-timescale rule over progress estimates from a general-purpose progress-based reward model to trigger human takeover. A short-window gate detects rapid decreases in progress, capturing immediate fail-ures such as dropping an object, while a long-window gate detects progress plateaus corresponding to stalled behavior, such as repeatedly failed grasps. When either gate fires, control is transferred to a human expert, and the resulting corrections are used in the next policy update. Importantly, arXiv:2610.04054v1 [cs.RO] 2 Oct 2026 both the reward model and the gating configuration remain fixed throughout interactive learning, requiring no per-round retraining or recalibration. We evaluate Reward-DAgger along three dimensions: failure-detection performance, cross-task transfer of the gat-ing parameters, and downstream policy improvement. On four simulation tasks, Reward-DAgger achieves a strong accuracy–latency trade-off compared to existing robot-gating and runtime-monitoring baselines. We further find that a single gate configuration selected jointly on multiple source tasks transfers to held-out target tasks without reducing detection accuracy and with only a minor increase in de-tection latency. In simulation, Reward-DAgger consistently improves autonomous success rate across three rounds of interactive learning while achieving the highest return on hu-man effort. Finally, we deploy the same simulation-selected gate configuration on four real-world manipulation tasks with large pretrained VLA policies. Without any task-specific hy-perparameter tuning, Reward-DAgger achieves competitive performance across all tasks and outperforms the baselines in most settings in terms of autonomous success rate and return on human effort, demonstrating that the gating mechanism transfers across environments and policy architectures. In summary, our main contributions are:  

> •

We propose a two-timescale, progress-based gating mech-anism that detects both abrupt failures and stalled behavior during robot execution. The gate is independent of the underlying policy, remains fixed across policy updates, and supports cross-task configuration transfer.  

> •

We introduce Reward-DAgger, an interactive imitation learning framework that uses this gating mechanism to trigger human takeover and iteratively finetune the policy from the resulting expert corrections.  

> •

We extensively evaluate Reward-DAgger across four sim-ulated and four real-world manipulation tasks with both task-specific diffusion policies and large pretrained VLAs, studying failure detection, cross-task transfer, downstream policy improvement, and human-effort efficiency. II. R ELATED WORK 

Interactive Imitation Learning. Interactive imitation learn-ing mitigates distribution shift in behavioral cloning by iteratively collecting expert actions on states visited by the learned policy [6, 7, 8, 9, 12, 21]. In human-gated methods, a supervisor monitors execution and decides when to intervene [7, 8, 9, 11, 22]. This monitoring requirement becomes burdensome when supervision scales to many robots [23, 24, 25]. Robot-gated methods instead automate this decision using signals such as action disagreement, estimated risk, or policy uncertainty [12, 14, 15, 17, 26, 27]. However, these gates commonly depend on task-specific learned criteria or policy-dependent signals that require additional training or calibration across tasks or policy updates. In contrast, Reward-DAgger uses an external task-progress signal that can be applied zero-shot across tasks and policy architec-tures. 

Runtime Monitoring in Robot Manipulation. Runtime monitoring provides a closely related approach for iden-tifying policy failures during deployment. These methods typically reduce observations, predicted actions, or internal policy representations to a scalar score, and then calibrate a conformal threshold for failure detection. Representative methods derive these scores from temporal inconsisten-cies among consecutive actions [28], deviations from the nominal observation distribution [16, 29], action chunk en-tropy [29], or learned failure predictors using internal VLA features [13]. However, these methods are primarily stud-ied as deployment-time mechanisms without updating the monitored policy. Reward-DAgger closes this loop between monitoring and learning by using gate firings to collect expert corrections for subsequent policy updates without inducing expensive retraining or recalibration overheads. 

General-Purpose Reward Models. Reward functions pro-vide scalar feedback about the desirability of states or actions and are commonly used to guide policy optimiza-tion. Recently, significant effort has focused on developing general-purpose reward models that provide dense reward signals across diverse tasks [18, 20, 30, 31, 32, 33]. A common approach is to estimate task progress from visual observations and language instructions and use this progress signal as a proxy for per-timestep reward [18, 19, 31, 32], with recent works demonstrating its use for reinforcement learning in real-world robotic manipulation. Despite these advances, directly optimizing policies with imperfect learned reward functions remains challenging, as they may exploit inaccuracies or spurious correlations in the learned reward, leading to reward hacking without completing the intended task [34, 35, 36, 37]. Rather than directly optimizing the progress signal, our key insight is to use it as a runtime monitoring signal for interactive imitation learning. Since progress is evaluated independently of the underlying policy and represented on a shared scale across tasks, it provides a natural signal for determining when expert intervention is needed without additional task- or policy-specific training or calibration. III. M ETHOD 

Reward-DAgger leverages dense progress signals obtained from general-purpose reward models to determine when a robot policy should transfer control to the expert. In this section, we first introduce how we construct a gating criterion from general-purpose reward models, and then discuss how we incorporate the criterion into an interactive imitation learning pipeline for fine-tuning the base policy.

Inspired by [18], we target two common temporal signa-tures of policy failure in terms of the predicted progress sig-nal. First, the policy may suddenly undo previously achieved progress. For example, a grasped object may slip from the gripper, or a partially inserted object may be pulled back out. These failures produce rapid decreases in predicted progress. Second, the policy may become stuck without advancing the task. For example, it may repeatedly miss a grasp or oscillate near the target pose without completing the placement. Such failures appear as plateaus in the predicted progress. Following this observation, we design two complemen-tary gates that constitute the overall reward gate . B. Reward-DAgger 

We now summarize the Reward-DAgger procedure. We start with a robot policy π0 and an offline dataset of expert demonstrations. During a rollout, the robot executes actions in the environment until the reward gate fires, after which the expert takes over and provides corrections. At each iteration 

i, we collect expert correction transitions until a fixed budget is reached. We then aggregate these expert interventions with the existing dataset and fine-tune πi to obtain the next student policy πi+1 . This process is repeated for a fixed number of iterations without changing the reward gate’s configuration. An overview of the framework is shown in Figure 1. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

Our experiments evaluate the effectiveness of Reward-DAgger as a robot-gated interactive imitation learning frame-work across different tasks and policy architectures. Specif-ically, we seek to answer the following research questions: 

Q1 . Does the gating mechanism of Reward-DAgger accu-rately detect failures with low detection latency? Q2 . Does the gating configuration of Reward-DAgger trans-fer across tasks without additional calibration? 

Q3 . Does Reward-DAgger efficiently improve downstream policy performance while minimizing human burden? 

Baselines. We compare Reward-DAgger against robot-gated DAgger methods as well as recent runtime monitoring ap-proaches adapted to interactive imitation learning:  

> •

ThriftyDAgger [12] trains a policy ensemble to estimate state novelty, and a Q-function to estimate risk. The human takeover is triggered when a state is deemed novel or risky.  

> •

Diff-DAgger [14] uses the diffusion policy’s denoising loss for the proposed action as an uncertainty score, which is then thresholded to trigger human takeover.  

> •

UCF [15] samples candidate end-effector poses within a radius of the current pose, fits a Gaussian mixture model to the denoising vectors, and combines inter-mode divergence with intra-mode variance to estimate uncertainty.  

> •

SAFE-LSTM [13] trains an LSTM on the final-layer features of a VLA policy to produce a scalar failure score, which is thresholded for detecting failures.  

> •

logpZO [16] trains a flow matching model to map in-distribution observations to the standard normal distribu-tion, and uses the distance between the current observa-tion’s latent representation and the origin as the score. 

Reward Model. We instantiate the reward model in Reward-DAgger using Robometer-4B [18], a general-purpose reward model that produces dense, frame-level estimates of task progress on a normalized [0 , 1] scale. We select Robometer because it outperforms prior general-purpose reward-model baselines across cross-domain robotic reward evaluations. 

Simulation Setup. For our simulation experiments, we use LIBERO-10 [38], a suite of 10 long-horizon tasks with diverse settings. Simulation enables us to evaluate different methods across multiple tasks at scale, collecting enough rollouts to reliably characterize their accuracy–latency trade-offs. In these experiments, we use a frozen π0.5 [39] LIBERO checkpoint as the expert policy. The student is a diffusion policy [1] trained on LIBERO demonstrations. We first identify Tasks 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, and 8, where the initial student policies show intermediate performance, yielding sufficient numbers of both successful and failed trajectories for meaningful evaluation. For failure-detection and downstream interactive learning experiments, we use Tasks 0, 1, 4, and 6, as these are the tasks our expert policy consistently recovers from frequent failure states. For experiments that only require student-policy rollouts, including hyperparameter selection and cross-task transfer, we use all tasks. 

Real Robot Setup. Our real-robot experiments use the DROID [40] setup, with a Franka FR3 robot arm equipped with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper. We evaluate Reward-DAgger on four tasks: stack the red block on top of the blue block, 

pick up the red marker and put it into the blue cup, put the slice of bread from the toaster to the bowl, and remove the towel and screwdriver from the basket and put them on the table. In these experiments, the student is a pretrained π0.5

policy, and the expert is a human teleoperator. An overview of the simulated and real-world tasks is provided in Figure 3. 

Q1: Failure Detection 

We first evaluate the effectiveness of Reward-DAgger’s gating mechanism on individual tasks based on two metrics:  

> •

Accuracy . The gate should fire on failure trajectories but not on successful trajectories. Since the evaluation data may contain an unbalanced number of successful and failed trajectories, we measure balanced accuracy: 

BA( c) = 12

 TPR( c) + TNR( c) (5) where c denotes a gate configuration. TPR corresponds to the fraction of failed trajectories where the gate fires, and TNR corresponds to the fraction of successful trajectories where the gate does not fire.  

> •

Latency . The gate should fire as early as possible when a failure occurs. Since it is difficult to manually label the precise onset of every failure, we use the mean trigger time on the failed trajectories as a surrogate for detection latency. For a failure trajectory where the gate does not fire, we assign the episode horizon as its trigger time. We normalize this value by the episode horizon. We compare Reward-DAgger’s gate against two categories of baselines: (1) Learned gates train additional models to produce the gating signal; this category includes ThriftyDAg-ger, SAFE-LSTM, and logpZO. (2) Post-hoc gates derive their scores directly from policies without additional offline training; Diff-DAgger and UCF fall into this category. For each of Tasks 0, 1, 4, and 6 from LIBERO-10 suite, we collect 200 rollouts from the student policy. We reserve 50 rollouts for training the learned gates and evaluate every method on the remaining 150 rollouts. For ThriftyDAgger, we use the policy ensemble and Q-risk estimator from its final checkpoint after three rounds of DAgger, which were trained on more transitions than the 50 reserved rollouts. We train SAFE-LSTM and logpZO using the 50 rollouts according to their respective training objectives. Reward-DAgger, Diff-DAgger, and UCF require no gate training. Comparing methods at a single operating point is insuffi-cient because they might be noisy and difficult to compare due to the tradeoff between accuracy and trigger time. Q2: Transfer of Gating Configurations 

Most existing baselines use task-specific gates, meaning that the same gating function or thresholds tuned on one task cannot be transferred directly to another. Several baselines are also policy-specific , meaning that updating a policy on the same task requires retraining or recalibrating the gate. In con-trast, Reward-DAgger depends on generic progress signals, which enable better transfer across tasks and policies. In simulation, we query the reward model at every environ-ment step. For real-world experiments, we address querying latency by employing asynchronous scoring and querying the reward model every k = 4 environment steps. For each method, we perform policy finetuning over multiple rounds. In each round, we collect a fixed budget of expert transitions using the corresponding gating method and then finetune the base policy on a replay buffer containing the original offline demonstrations and the aggregated expert corrections. Following [14], once takeover is triggered, the expert controls the robot for the remainder of the episode. After each round of training, we evaluate two metrics:  

> •

Autonomous success rate evaluates the updated student policy over K autonomous rollouts. It reflects how effec-tively the gate triggers informative expert interventions.  

> •

Return on human effort (ROHE) evaluates the perfor-mance of the combined student-expert system relative to the amount of expert interventions required. Following [26], ROHE is defined as 

ROHE = P(Sτ = 1) 1 + H/T . (7) The numerator corresponds to the task’s success rate when the gate is active and the expert can take over. H/T 

denotes the fraction of rollout episodes in which the expert intervenes. A high ROHE indicates that the combined system achieves a high success rate while requiring expert intervention in relatively few episodes. We measure ROHE at the episode level as each intervention in our setting results in expert takeover for the remainder of the episode.

Under our 20-rollout evaluation budget, we find that calibration is highly unstable. To compute ROHE, we use the evaluation rollouts, where the human annotator judges whether the state at which the gate triggers is recoverable. A rollout is considered successful for the combined student-expert system if the student completes the task autonomously or the gate triggers while expert recovery remains feasible. Summary. We introduced Reward-DAgger, a robot-gated interactive imitation learning framework that detects progress drops and plateaus using estimates from a general-purpose reward model. By decoupling the gating mechanism from the underlying policy, Reward-DAgger operates across policy checkpoints and architectures, including black-box policies, without retraining or recalibrating the gate between DAgger rounds. In simulated and real-robot experiments, Reward-DAgger achieves effective failure detection, cross-task trans-fer, and efficient downstream policy improvement. Reward-DAgger is modular with respect to the reward model: more accurate general-purpose reward models can be incorporated without modifying the interactive imitation learning pipeline. 

Limitations and Future Work. Reward-DAgger is not without limitations. Its long-window gate must observe a sustained progress plateau, so failures such as repeated unsuccessful grasp attempts may persist for several seconds before triggering an intervention. More broadly, the frame-work is reactive rather than preemptive, requesting human assistance only after evidence of a failure emerges, which limits its applicability in high-risk settings or when failures are difficult to recover from. A promising direction is to integrate predictive world models [42, 43] into the gating framework, allowing the robot to anticipate possible failure outcomes and request intervention before executing them. Frontier Transfer. We explore whether configurations that perform well on one task remain effective on other tasks. For each source task, we construct its task-specific Pareto frontier using 200 policy rollouts from a diffusion policy trained exclusively on that task. We then apply the configurations on this frontier to each of the other three tasks and retain the Pareto-optimal points to form a transferred frontier. To measure how closely each transferred frontier matches the target task’s native frontier, we compute the hypervolume ratio (HVR). Specifically, we divide the area under the transferred frontier by the area under the native frontier. The trigger-time range extends from zero to the latest non-dominated point reached by either frontier. An HVR near one indicates that the transferred configurations retain most of the accuracy–latency tradeoff obtained by task-specific tuning.
