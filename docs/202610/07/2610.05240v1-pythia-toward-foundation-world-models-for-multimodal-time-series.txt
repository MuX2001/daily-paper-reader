Xilin Dai ∗, Hongzhou Chen ∗, Yifan Hu , Yiding Liu , Zewei Dong †, Jiang-Ming Yang

Time-series foundation models offer a unified approach to forecasting across heterogeneous domains. Textual context and auxiliary observations provide complementary information about temporal dynamics, yet reusable multimodal predictive representations remain under-explored. We introduce Pythia , a foundation world model that learns context-conditioned latent dynamics across datasets through a joint-embedding predictive architecture. A stop-gradient numerical reference guides contextual corrections to predicted future states. A separate probabilistic decoder then adapts to the frozen predictive representation and ob-served history, decoupling world-model pretraining from observation-space forecasting. On MUSE, Pythia-Tiny’s normalized mean absolute scaled error (MASE) and weighted sum quantile loss (WSQL) are 0.6879 and 0.4269, reducing errors by 6.26% and 5.00% rel-ative to the strongest model evaluated in the published MUSE leaderboard. Through a series of controlled experiments, we investigate how to design a time-series world model through shared pretraining and how joint-embedding predictive learning can incorporate multimodal information. The results support separating predictive representation learning from probabilistic readout and show complementary contributions from entity descriptions, events, and covariates.

1 Introduction 

Time-series modeling supports applications in battery state estimation, infrastructure monitoring, and physiological forecasting (Dai et al., 2025; Cohen et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2026), with forecasting methods ranging from dataset-specific full-shot Transformers such as PatchTST and GPT4TS (Nie et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023) and linear models such as DLinear and TimePrism (Zeng et al., 2023; Dai et al., 2026c) to recent time-series foundation models. Time-series foundation models learn shared forecasting behavior from heterogeneous collections of series, allowing one pretrained model to serve many datasets without task-specific fitting (Ansari et al., 2024; Das et al., 2024; Woo et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025e; Ansari et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026a). Numerical history, however, describes only part of the information available at a prediction origin (Dai et al., 2026a). An entity description identifies what a signal measures; an event provides context for changes in its trajectory; and an observed covariate offers an additional view of its recent state. Multimodal foundation models extend forecasting to these complementary inputs (Wang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026). Paired 1arXiv:2610.05240v1 [cs.LG] 4 Oct 2026 time-series datasets and context-rich benchmarks expose both their promise and the challenge of using heterogeneous information consistently (Liu et al., 2024a; Williams et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026b; Xu et al., 2026). Complementing shared pretraining, ORCA performs black-box online adaptation by learning forecast residuals conditioned on a foundation model’s inputs and outputs (Dai et al., 2026b). This progress raises a representation-learning question: can a model learn context-conditioned predictive states across datasets before learning how to read out future observations? Direct forecasting trains the representation and output mapping together through an observation-space loss. Separating these stages instead makes latent prediction the pretraining objective and fits the probabilistic readout to the resulting frozen representation. A joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) provides a mechanism for this separation: it predicts representations of unobserved content from available observations, using an encoded target as supervision (Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024; Assran et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a). For multimodal time series, the challenge is to incorporate contextual information while retaining the numerical structure needed to decode useful forecasts. We introduce Pythia , a foundation world model for multimodal time series. Here, a world model 

denotes a model of context-conditioned predictive states learned in latent space for observational forecasting. A numerical predictor first estimates target states from observed history. A context module then refines these estimates using entity information, events filtered by their supplied timestamps, and observed covariates. The resulting world states are matched to targets from a frozen numerical encoder. An additional objective uses a stop-gradient numerical reference to emphasize contextual correction errors relative to the prediction from numerical history. Its gradients update only the context pathway. Direction and scale alignment keep both pathways in the representation space supplied by the pretrained numerical model. Training proceeds in two stages. The first learns latent prediction through forecasting and masked reconstruction across the pretraining corpus. The second freezes the predictive system and adapts a probabilistic decoder over observed history and predicted world states. Initialized from Chronos-2 (Ansari et al., 2025), both stages learn across datasets without adaptation to individual MUSE tasks. Pythia-Tiny provides the main benchmark result, while matched Small-model controls examine the learning recipe and contextual inputs. These experiments study how latent pretraining, nu-merical initialization, and multimodal integration support shared predictive representations across heterogeneous forecasting tasks. Our contributions are threefold: • A foundation world model for time series. We explore how joint-embedding predictive learning can produce a shared model of latent temporal dynamics through pretraining across heteroge-neous datasets. A separate probabilistic readout turns its frozen predictive states into zero-shot forecasts, and controlled comparisons examine the value of this separation. • Multimodal predictive states guided by a numerical reference. We integrate entity descriptions, timestamp-filtered events, and observed covariates as contextual corrections to numerical latent predictions. Numerical-reference guidance trains these corrections within a common representa-tion space; component and group-level analyses reveal both their aggregate benefits and their variation across forecasting tasks. • Leading zero-shot results on MUSE. Pythia-Tiny obtains the lowest aggregate errors among the compared methods, improving normalized mean absolute scaled error (MASE) and weighted 2sum quantile loss (WSQL) by 6.26% and 5.00% over the strongest published MUSE baseline, respectively. Extensive controlled experiments connect these forecasting results to the design of the predictive representation, multimodal integration, and two-stage training. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Time-series foundation models 

Large-scale pretraining on heterogeneous series produces reusable forecasting priors, as demon-strated by Chronos, TimesFM, and Moirai (Ansari et al., 2024; Das et al., 2024; Woo et al., 2024). Subsequent work expands the architectures and objectives used for shared forecasting: Time-MoE explores sparse experts, while Sundial learns continuous predictive distributions through flow matching (Shi et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025e). Chronos-2 supports univariate, multivariate, and covariate-informed prediction (Ansari et al., 2025). Beyond numerical inputs, ChatTime combines time series and text in a shared model, and Aurora studies universal generative multimodal fore-casting (Wang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026). Pythia makes context-conditioned latent prediction its pretraining objective and separately adapts a probabilistic decoder to its frozen predictive states. 

2.2 Toward multimodal time-series forecasting 

Textual and auxiliary observations provide information beyond numerical history. Time-MMD pairs numerical and textual data, Context is Key (CiK) isolates problems requiring textual information, and MUSE evaluates heterogeneous contextual inputs under a shared benchmark (Liu et al., 2024a; Williams et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026b). GPT4MTS and Texts as Time Series integrate text with numerical prediction (Jia et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026d); REST-TS addresses text collapse through forecast-residual supervision (Nguyen et al., 2026). Generative artificial intelligence (AI) methods LLMTime and Time-LLM use large language models (LLMs) via numerical serialization and reprogramming, respectively (Gruver et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2024). Pythia uses semantic context to refine a numerical latent estimate, with a detached numerical reference guiding the correction before readout training. We evaluate on MUSE using its published baselines. Appendix H provides the extended discussion, including predictive representation learning. 

3 Pythia 

3.1 Forecasting through predictive latent states 

For prediction origin T and forecast horizon H, let x1: T denote the observed numerical history and 

cT the accompanying context retained by input preprocessing. The forecasting task is to estimate future observations xT+1: T+H, including predictive quantiles, with a shared model across datasets. Pythia represents these future observations through predicted latent states w, first learning the mapping (x1: T, cT ) 7 → w and then fitting a probabilistic readout to the frozen mapping. Figure 1 summarizes this division of learning objectives. We divide numerical sequences into patches of 16 observations. The first eight blocks of a pretrained Chronos-2 model form a frozen numerical encoder E. For a sampled task, E encodes the visible numerical view into a memory h. A separate reference pass encodes the complete numerical view, including the target patches, with the same normalization statistics. Its target states tj, indexed by target patch j, are stop-gradient references and are never inputs to the predictor. During forecasting, future numerical values are unobserved and excluded from the predictor’s numerical memory. 3Figure 1 Pythia’s default two-stage recipe. Stage 1 learns numerical predictions n from memory h and a residual correction using context memory m, aligning world states w with stop-gradient (sg) target states t encoded from full numerical sequences. Matching uses absolute-distance ( L1) and log root-mean-square (RMS) terms. A detached numerical reference guides only the context pathway. Stage 2 freezes the predictive representation and adapts three decoder blocks over ordered history, the special REG token, and world states using pinball loss and a parameter anchor; the final decoder block, normalization, and quantile head remain frozen. Targets provide supervision only. 

A trainable predictor Pθ, with parameters θ, maps learned target queries qj and visible mem-ory to numerical predictions nj = Pθ (qj, h). Each predictor block contains query self-attention, cross-attention to numerical memory, and a feed-forward network. Queries combine a mask em-bedding with position, calendar features, frequency, signed logarithmic lead, and a task identifier. Pretraining mixes causal forecasting and contiguous masked-block reconstruction with equal prob-ability, supervising at most six target patches per sampled task. The task embedding distinguishes prediction beyond an observed prefix from reconstruction within a partially observed sequence. 

3.2 Context as a residual correction 

Parameters ψ belong to the context encoder and correction module. The context encoder Cψ receives entity information, event content with timestamps, and named numerical covariates. A frozen pretrained multimodal encoder supplies cached semantic embeddings. Event content and time projections are added to form one token per event; similarly, a covariate-name embedding and a numerical-summary projection are added to form one token per covariate. Numerical summaries describe observed values through level, variation, trend, coverage, and coarse temporal bins. A temporal transformer combines these tokens into context memory m = Cψ(cT ). Events are retained only when their supplied timestamps are no later than the prediction origin, and covariate summaries use historical observations only. 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental setup 

4.1.0.1 Benchmark and metrics. We evaluate on MUSE (Chen et al., 2026b), a multimodal fore-casting benchmark with 14 datasets spanning eight domains and six observation frequencies. Evaluation covers 12,729 test windows and 29 dataset–frequency–horizon scoring groups. We report MASE for median forecasts and WSQL over nine predictive deciles. Following MUSE, each group is normalized against Seasonal Naive on matching coverage, and the overall score is the equal-group geometric mean. Lower scores are better. Appendix C gives the evaluation protocol. 

4.1.0.2 Unified pretraining and evaluation. Both Pythia stages train across heterogeneous time series, without dataset-specific adaptation on MUSE. We reserve a held-out subset of the pretraining corpus for checkpoint selection and report forecasting results on the MUSE test set. Tiny is our headline configuration; Medium provides a larger representative, and Small is the common platform for matched mechanism experiments. Main results use the selected readout checkpoint. Capacity, budget, and batch analyses instead compare final checkpoints at the declared budgets, keeping training-horizon effects distinct from checkpoint selection. 

4.1.0.3 Comparisons. We retain all 31 baseline results published in MUSE, with their original zero-shot or dataset-specific training protocols. These cover statistical forecasters, numerical foundation models, multimodal models, and LLM-based approaches, including DLinear (Zeng et al., 2023), PatchTST (Nie et al., 2023), TimesNet (Wu et al., 2023), and TimeMixer (Wang et al., 2024). Chronos2 has the strongest published aggregate scores and supplies Pythia’s numerical initialization. The benchmark comparison evaluates the complete Pythia system, including its additional pretraining; matched Small experiments examine the contributions of predictive pretraining, context, and readout adaptation.

A separately trained query decoder also benefits from world states, but remains weaker than the adapted native readout, highlighting the importance of a compatible representation–decoder interface.

4.6 Capacity and training budget 

The full grid reveals a local capacity–budget relationship (Figure 5a,b): the best configuration on both metrics shifts from Tiny to Small to Medium as the per-stage budget increases from 10K to 30K to 50K. The corresponding optimum does not improve monotonically. Medium benefits from the larger budgets, whereas Tiny and XLarge do not. Batch effects also depend on the training horizon (Figure 5c). Tiny prefers batch 128 at 10K, but improves with batch 96 →128 →256 at 30K; Medium’s smallest batch is best at 30K, whereas its largest is best at 50K. Stage allocation adds another interaction: at 60K total updates and batch 256, Medium’s 50K+10K recipe improves MASE/WSQL by 3.34%/2.46% over 10K+50K (Figure 5d). Even equal sampled exposure need not give equal performance when batch and update count differ. 5 Conclusion 

Pythia explores foundation world modeling for time series through shared latent predictive pre-training and a separately adapted probabilistic readout. Numerical-reference guidance helps entity information, events, and covariates refine numerical predictions. Pythia-Tiny leads the MUSE comparison, reducing normalized MASE and WSQL by 6.26% and 5.00% relative to the strongest model evaluated in the published MUSE leaderboard. Matched Small-model controls support separating predictive pretraining from readout adaptation; removing contextual inputs worsens aggregate accuracy, although their benefits vary across tasks. Together, these experiments characterize how numerical initialization, contextual correction, and stage-specific adaptation shape multimodal predictive learning. The results support a shared latent interface between contextual pretraining and probabilistic forecasting, with the benefit of individual design choices assessed under matched training conditions. 11 References

16 A Implementation and training details 

A.1 Model components and configurations 

Pythia reuses the first eight blocks of the 12-block Chronos-2 encoder as its frozen numerical front end. The hidden width is 768 and the patch length is 16. The numerical predictor contains self-attention over prediction queries and cross-attention to visible numerical memory. Entity descriptions, event content, and covariate names are represented by cached 4,096-dimensional embeddings from the frozen Qwen3-VL-Embedding-8B encoder (Li et al., 2026b). Unit-normalized embeddings are multiplied by the square root of their dimension before learned projection. The context encoder uses paired additive fusion: event content and time share one token, and each covariate name and numerical summary share one token. The covariate summary has 12 entries: transformed mean, standard deviation, first and last values; standardized change and slope; observed fraction and count; and four standardized temporal-bin means. Summaries use only retained historical observations. Prediction queries combine learned target tokens with position, calendar, frequency, lead-time, and task information. The main pretraining task mixture uses forecasting and masked interior-block prediction with equal probability and supervises at most six target patches per sampled task.

Medium uses batch 256 and 50,000 updates per stage, with Stage 1 supplying its final checkpoint and Stage 2 selected at update 45,000. Large uses batch 128 and 10,000 updates per stage, selecting update 10,000. Each model uses one checkpoint across all benchmark tasks. Sampling is online; Appendix E defines the corresponding exposure counts. 

A.3 Training protocols for the learning-recipe controls 

Direct forecasting skips Stage 1 and updates the context encoder, predictors, and permitted decoder parameters using the Stage 2 forecasting objective for 60K updates. Joint readout adaptation reuses the reference Stage 1 checkpoint and updates the representation along with the readout for 30K Stage 2 updates. Numerical decoder adaptation has no Pythia context/world branch and adapts native numerical blocks 8, 9, and 10 for 60K updates using the same relative rates and anchor. Its front end, last block, final normalization, and quantile head remain frozen. The numerical-only two-stage control instead retains 30K latent-pretraining and 30K readout updates, disabling context and reference guidance throughout training. It shares Pythia’s numerical initialization, task mixture, latent-alignment objective, readout recipe, batch size, and checkpoint-selection rule. All controls train on the same pretraining corpus, with no MUSE-specific optimization.

36 H Extended related work 

H.1 Numerical time-series foundation models 

Large-scale pretraining over heterogeneous numerical series has enabled shared forecasting models to transfer across datasets. Chronos tokenizes numerical values for language-model-style probabilis-tic forecasting (Ansari et al., 2024); TimesFM uses a decoder-only architecture (Das et al., 2024); and Moirai accommodates variation in frequency, channel count, and predictive distributions through a universal forecasting architecture (Woo et al., 2024). Timer studies generative pretraining across forecasting, imputation, and anomaly detection (Liu et al., 2024c). Time-MoE and Moirai-MoE explore sparse expert architectures for forecasting (Shi et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025d), while Sundial uses a flow-matching objective to model continuous next-patch distributions without discrete tokenization (Liu et al., 2025e). Moirai 2.0 combines a decoder-only architecture with quantile forecasting and multi-token prediction (Liu et al., 2025a); Timer-S1 and Toto 2 further study scaling numerical forecasting under their respective training recipes (Liu et al., 2026b; Khwaja et al., 2026). Chronos-2 extends this line to univariate, multivariate, and covariate-informed forecasting (Ansari et al., 2025). Other designs emphasize compact architectures or flexible conditioning. Tiny Time Mixers stud-ies lightweight adaptive models for multivariate forecasting (Ekambaram et al., 2024). TS-ICL unifies probabilistic forecasting and imputation through timestamp-aligned in-context regression, including covariates and partially observed histories (Le Naour et al., 2026). Pythia builds on the pretrained Chronos-2 encoder and probabilistic decoder, adding context-conditioned prediction within the encoder’s latent space. 

H.2 Language models and multimodal forecasting 

H.2.0.1 Language models for numerical prediction. LLMTime serializes numbers as digit strings and performs zero-shot extrapolation through next-token prediction (Gruver et al., 2023). GPT4TS adapts a largely frozen pretrained Transformer across time-series tasks (Zhou et al., 2023). Time-LLM reprograms time-series patches through text prototypes and prefix prompts (Jin et al., 2024), while AutoTimes maps numerical segments into the language model’s embedding space for autoregressive forecasting with timestamp conditioning (Liu et al., 2024b). CALF aligns temporal and textual streams at multiple representation levels (Liu et al., 2025c). TimeCMA aligns time-series embeddings with embeddings of prompts derived from the same numerical series (Liu et al., 2025b). 

H.2.0.2 External context for forecasting. GPT4MTS combines textual context with numerical histories for multimodal forecasting (Jia et al., 2024). Texts as Time Series converts paired text representations into auxiliary variables usable by numerical forecasters (Li et al., 2026d). VoT combines event reasoning and textual alignment with adaptive fusion of numerical and event predictions (Wang et al., 2026); TESS extracts structured mean, volatility, shape, and lag information from text for gated contextual integration (Li et al., 2026a). At foundation-model scale, ChatTime supports numerical and textual inputs and outputs in a shared multimodal model (Wang et al., 2025), while Aurora targets universal generative multimodal forecasting (Wu et al., 2026). Contextual integration also raises the question of how to train a model to use text effectively. For context-aided forecasting (CAF), DoubleCast addresses the modality gap with semisynthetic CAF-7M training data (Zheng et al., 2026). REST-TS studies text collapse and supervises the textual pathway through structured numerical forecast residuals (Nguyen et al., 2026). TRACE uses 37 conditional estimation to address missing and irregularly sampled modalities before multimodal fusion (Kan et al., 2026). Pythia represents entity descriptions, events, and covariate names with a frozen semantic encoder, then uses a trainable context pathway to refine numerical predictive states. Its detached numerical error supplies a reference for contextual learning, with the probabilistic output loss applied in the subsequent readout stage. 

H.3 Forecasting datasets and benchmarks 

Time-MMD provides paired numerical and textual observations across multiple domains (Liu et al., 2024a), and Context is Key isolates forecasting problems in which textual information is essential (Williams et al., 2025). MUSE broadens evaluation to heterogeneous contextual inputs, domains, frequencies, and forecast horizons, comparing numerical foundation models, multimodal methods, and LLM-based forecasters under a common benchmark (Chen et al., 2026b). Numerical benchmarks offer complementary perspectives: the Toto study introduces the Benchmark of Observability Metrics (BOOM) to evaluate forecasting on observability data (Cohen et al., 2025). We use MUSE as the primary benchmark because contextual observations are central to Pythia’s prediction problem. Its published baseline results provide the principal comparison, and we evaluate Pythia on its test tasks without dataset-specific adaptation. 

H.4 JEPA objectives and target representations 

I-JEPA (Assran et al., 2023) and V-JEPA (Bardes et al., 2024) learn representations by predicting target embeddings rather than reconstructing pixels. VL-JEPA applies predictive learning to vision– language tasks by predicting continuous text embeddings and using a separate decoder to produce text (Chen et al., 2026a). 

H.4.0.1 Target spaces and representation stability. Variance-invariance-covariance regulariza-tion (VICReg) explicitly regularizes embedding variance and covariance (Bardes et al., 2022), and C-JEPA combines it with image-based JEPA to improve representation stability (Mo and Tong, 2024). SALT demonstrates masked latent prediction with a separately pretrained, frozen teacher in video (Li et al., 2026c). Pythia similarly preserves a pretrained target space in its default recipe, while supervising the direction and magnitude of predicted numerical states. Our encoder/decoder adaptation experiments examine how preserving or updating this initialization affects multimodal predictive learning. 

H.4.0.2 Theoretical perspectives. Analyses of deep linear self-distillation identify an implicit preference for predictive features (Littwin et al., 2024). A complementary temporal analysis relates JEPA’s emergent dynamical-regime clustering to Koopman-invariant representations and the inductive bias of a near-identity linear predictor (Ruiz-Morales et al., 2026). These studies connect latent prediction to the information retained in learned representations. 

H.5 Temporal JEPA and latent world models 

H.5.0.1 Latent forecasting and temporal representations. LaT-PFN combines a JEPA with a prior-data fitted network (PFN) for zero-shot time-series forecasting (Verdenius et al., 2024). It learns from synthetic series, uses related numerical series as in-context examples, and predicts future embeddings in an EMA-defined target space. Its probabilistic decoder receives detached latent predictions and is optimized with independent gradients. Pythia shares this separation of latent prediction and observation-space decoding, using a pretrained numerical foundation model 38 to define the target space and external multimodal observations to condition its predictive states. It then freezes the predictive modules and adapts a pretrained quantile decoder in a separate training stage. TS-JEPA predicts masked numerical patch embeddings using an EMA target encoder and evaluates the frozen encoder with classification and regression heads (Ennadir et al., 2025). CHARM con-ditions temporal JEPA learning on channel text and transfers frozen embeddings to downstream tasks (Dutta et al., 2026). SC-JEPA combines a soft codebook bottleneck with prediction at multiple temporal resolutions for anomaly anticipation (He et al., 2026). Together, these works explore temporal predictive learning, semantic conditioning, and structured target spaces across different downstream tasks. 

H.5.0.2 Latent world models. V-JEPA 2 extends predictive learning to large-scale video pretrain-ing and subsequently learns an action-conditioned world model for robotic planning (Assran et al., 2025). GenRL aligns pretrained vision–language representations with a generative world model for learning embodied behaviors in imagination (Mazzaglia et al., 2024). NormWear-2 models physiological signals and clinical interventions through shared latent dynamics (Luo et al., 2026). For multivariate forecasting, Phys-JEPA decomposes states into physical and residual components and constrains latent states and transitions through known physical variables (Nie et al., 2026).
