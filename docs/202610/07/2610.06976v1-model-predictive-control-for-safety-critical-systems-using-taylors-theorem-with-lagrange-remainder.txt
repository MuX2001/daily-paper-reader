# Model Predictive Control for Safety-Critical Systems using Taylor’s Theorem with Lagrange Remainder 

Shuo Liu 1, Liang Wu 2, Wei Xiao 3, J´ an Drgoˇ na 2 and Calin A. Belta 4

Abstract — Safety-critical Model Predictive Control (MPC) formulations based on Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) often require multiple tuning parameters and may become conserva-tive or difficult to keep feasible, particularly for high-relative-degree constraints. To address these limitations, in this paper we draw inspiration from the recently introduced Taylor-Lagrange Control (TLC) method, and propose an MPC formulation based on a Taylor expansion with Lagrange remainder (MPC-TLR). To enable digital implementation, we develop a numerical approximation of the Lagrange remainder under zero-order-hold control. The resulting safety constraint includes a tunable margin to account for approximation errors and balance safety, feasibility, and conservativeness. Comparisons with MPC us-ing high-order CBFs (MPC-HOCBF), discrete-time high-order CBFs (MPC-DHOCBF), and direct safety constraints (MPC-DC) on a unicycle obstacle-avoidance problem show that MPC-TLR yields safer trajectories than MPC-DHOCBF and MPC-DC, while requiring fewer tuning parameters and being less conservative than MPC-HOCBF. Its broader control-input cov-erage under the adopted discretization can improve feasibility and control performance for short prediction horizons. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Safety-critical optimal control is a fundamental problem in many areas. For example, in robotics, reaching a target while avoiding obstacles and minimizing control effort can be formulated as a constrained optimal control problem using control barrier functions (CBFs) [1], [2]. By discretizing time into small intervals, the control problem can be addressed by solving a quadratic program at each sampling instant, often in real time. Extensions of this framework have been developed for high-relative-degree constraints and adaptive control settings [3]–[7]. However, because each optimization is typically myopic and does not explicitly account for future system behavior, the resulting control actions can be overly aggressive. Model predictive control (MPC) with discrete-time CBFs (DCBFs) [8], [9] addresses this limitation by incorporating predicted future states over a receding horizon, thereby enabling smoother and more anticipatory control actions.

However, the resulting optimization problem is generally nonlinear and nonconvex, and its computational cost may increase substantially with the prediction horizon. Maintain-ing feasibility is another important challenge. For relative-degree-one DCBFs, relaxation techniques were introduced in [10] to improve feasibility. For higher-relative-degree constraints, an iterative convex MPC framework based on linearized discrete-time high-order CBFs (DHOCBFs) [11] was developed in [12], significantly reducing the computa-tional burden while incorporating relaxation techniques to improve feasibility. Despite these developments, several fundamental issues remain. MPC-CBF formulations, including their discrete-time and high-order extensions, have been widely applied to safety-critical control problems [13]–[15]. However, the validity of the candidate barrier function is not always explicitly established in these formulations. Consequently, the theoretical safety guarantees associated with a valid CBF may not directly carry over. Conversely, if a valid CBF has already been constructed and can be enforced by a feasible controller [16], [17], MPC is not required to establish safety itself, although it may still improve performance through prediction and optimization. This raises the question of whether an unverified CBF constraint offers a systematic advantage over other forms of safety constraints, and more generally, which safety formulation is preferable within MPC. In addition, high-order CBF formulations introduce more design parameters as the relative degree increases, making it increasingly difficult to balance safety, feasibility, and control conservativeness. The recently proposed Taylor–Lagrange Control (TLC) method [18] ensures system safety by leveraging Taylor’s theorem with the Lagrange remainder [19], [20]. Unlike CBF-based approaches, TLC provides a necessary and suffi-cient safety condition while introducing substantially fewer tuning parameters. Subsequent extensions have addressed inter-sampling safety, namely, possible safety violations be-tween consecutive sampling instants under zero-order-hold control, as well as adaptive control settings [21], [22]. Motivated by these developments, we develop an MPC safety formulation based on a Taylor expansion with a numerically approximated Lagrange remainder. Specifically, the main contributions of this paper are as follows:  

> •

We develop a numerically implementable formulation based on a Taylor expansion with Lagrange remainder (TLR) under zero-order-hold control by approximating the integral remainder using intermediate states and numerical quadrature. The number of quadrature points arXiv:2610.06976v1 [eess.SY] 3 Oct 2026 can be adjusted to improve the approximation accuracy.  

> •

We integrate the proposed TLR-based formulation into a receding-horizon MPC framework for safety-critical control. A tunable safety margin is introduced to com-pensate for numerical approximation errors and to ad-just the trade-off among safety, feasibility, and control conservativeness.  

> •

We show that MPC-TLR provides a good balance among safety, feasibility, and conservativeness, achiev-ing safer trajectories than MPC-DHOCBF and MPC-DC while requiring fewer tuning parameters and being less conservative than MPC-HOCBF. A unicycle obstacle-avoidance example is used to validate these advantages and compare the four formulations under different hori-zons and parameter settings. II. P RELIMINARIES

Problem 1. Find a feasible control policy for system (1) such that the safety requirement, control and state limitations are satisfied, and cost (11) is minimized. Conventional CBF controllers compute the current input through a one-step optimization. For a fixed state x, f (x)

and g(x) in (1) are fixed, so the CBF and HOCBF conditions are affine in u. With a quadratic objective, they can therefore be imposed as linear constraints in a quadratic program (QP) [3], [4]. Because only the current input is optimized, these controllers do not account for future state evolution and may produce myopic actions. MPC addresses this limitation by optimizing a sequence of control inputs over a finite prediction horizon while explicitly accounting for the predicted state evolution. It has therefore been integrated with DCBFs [8] and DHOCBFs [24]. In particular, it was shown in [8] that MPC-DCBF can increase the minimum distance to obstacles compared with directly imposing h(x) ≥ 0 as a state constraint. To formulate these predictive controllers, the continuous-time dynamics are dis-cretized with a sampling period ∆t, and the continuous-time cost in (11) is approximated by a finite sum over the prediction horizon. Nevertheless, the HOCBF and DHOCBF conditions pro-vide sufficient rather than necessary conditions for safety as discussed in Sec. II-B, and their class K functions introduce additional tuning parameters that may affect feasibility and conservativeness. In contrast, TLC provides necessary and sufficient conditions for satisfying the safety requirement while requiring fewer tuning parameters [18], [22]. However, existing TLC formulations do not explicitly account for predicted future states. Motivated by this limitation, we develop a predictive safety formulation based on a Taylor expansion with Lagrange remainder (TLR) and incorporate it into an MPC framework. We refer to the resulting approach as MPC-TLR and compare it with existing safety-critical MPC approaches in the following sections. 

Approach: In the proposed approach, the continuous-time dynamics (1) are first discretized with a sampling period ∆t

to construct the discrete-time model (6) used in MPC, and the integral cost in (11) is approximated by a finite sum over the prediction horizon. The safety requirement is incorporated into the resulting discrete-time optimization problem through TLR-based constraints imposed on the predicted states. A tunable safety margin is introduced into these constraints to provide an additional safety margin and adjust the con-servativeness of the controller. The TLR-based constraints are then integrated with the state and control input bounds, yielding a finite-horizon nonlinear and generally nonconvex optimization problem that is solved at each sampling instant. In particular, the TLR constraint requires evaluating h(m)(xt+k,j , ut+k) at each quadrature point and at every prediction step, resulting in approximately N (M + 1) additional nonlinear function evaluations and safety constraints. Therefore, in practice, (20) is typically solved using a local nonlinear programming method, such as sequential quadratic programming or an interior-point method, and the obtained solution is generally only locally optimal. 

D. Comparative Analysis with Existing Safety-Critical MPC Formulations 

The MPC-TLR formulation shares the same receding-horizon optimization framework with several existing safety-critical MPC methods. In particular, the objective function (20a), initial condition (20b), prediction dynamics (20c), and state and input constraints (20e), (20f) remain unchanged. The main difference lies in the safety constraint in (20d). Therefore, the different methods can be obtained by replacing (20d) with their corresponding safety conditions. For MPC with direct state constraints, referred to as MPC-DC, safety is directly imposed on predicted future states as 

h (xt+k+1 ) ≥ 0, k = 0 , . . . , N − 1. (22) For MPC-HOCBF, we rewrite the continuous-time HOCBF condition in (5) based on the continuous-time affine control system in (1) and impose the resulting safety con-straint at each prediction step as

From the above formulations, we can compare the number of control inputs that explicitly enter the safety constraints over the prediction horizon. MPC-TLR and MPC-HOCBF provide the largest input coverage, with up to N control inputs involved in their respective safety constraints. For MPC-DC, the control input affects the safety function h only after md prediction steps, where the discrete-time relative degree md depends on the chosen discretization scheme. Consequently, when md > 1, only N −md +1 control inputs explicitly enter the direct state-safety constraints. Similarly, MPC-DHOCBF constructs its safety condition through md

successive forward differences, so its constraint can only be imposed over the shortened horizon and also involves 

N − md + 1 control inputs. The intermediate points used in MPC-TLR are introduced solely to numerically approximate the Lagrange remainder under the same ZOH control input and therefore do not introduce additional control decision variables. Safety is still enforced only at the MPC sampling instants, as in the baseline methods; the baseline constraints require no such intermediate points because they contain no integral remainder. 

Remark 2. When the discrete-time relative degree md of the safety function h is close to the prediction horizon 

N , MPC-TLR and MPC-HOCBF involve substantially more control inputs in their safety constraints than MPC-DC and MPC-DHOCBF. This increased control-input coverage provides the optimizer with greater flexibility to shape the predicted trajectory for safety and may therefore improve feasibility and reduce conservativeness. In contrast, when 

N is significantly larger than md, the relative difference in control-input coverage becomes small, and its effect on the performance of the four formulations may become negligible. 

Compared with MPC-DC and MPC-DHOCBF, the safety constraints in MPC-TLR and MPC-HOCBF generally have more complex analytical forms. In particular, MPC-TLR requires the evaluation of higher-order derivatives and a numerical approximation of the integral remainder (20d), whereas MPC-HOCBF involves successive derivatives of the barrier function (23). These additional operations increase the cost of evaluating the constraints and their Jacobians and may therefore increase the computational burden of the nonlinear MPC problem. However, the overall solution time also depends on the number of imposed constraints, their sparsity, the prediction horizon, and the numerical solver, so the more complex constraint form does not necessarily imply a proportionally larger computation time. Although the HOCBF, DHOCBF, and DC formulations impose exact safety constraints in their respective formula-tions, the TLR constraint relies on a numerical approximation of the integral remainder. Nevertheless, the safety of interest is that of the original continuous-time system (1), whereas all four MPC formulations employ a discretized prediction model (20c) and are therefore subject to discretization error. Moreover, MPC-TLR and MPC-HOCBF derive their safety conditions directly from the continuous-time dynamics (1), while MPC-DC and MPC-DHOCBF construct them from the discretized dynamics (20c). Consequently, none of the four formulations provides an error-free safety guarantee for the actual continuous-time implementation (21) unless the discretization error is explicitly accounted for. B. Performance 

Figure 1 compares the closed-loop trajectories obtained by the four approaches under different prediction horizons and safety-constraint parameters. In general, increasing the prediction horizon allows the controller to anticipate the obstacle earlier, resulting in an earlier initiation of the avoidance maneuver and a smoother closed-loop trajectory. For MPC-HOCBF in Fig. 1a, the case with N = 12 becomes infeasible before the robot reaches the goal, as indicated by the premature termination of the trajectory. In contrast, the longer horizons remain feasible and successfully guide the robot around the obstacle. For MPC-DC in Fig. 1c, the case with N = 12 converges to an undesirable local minimum, preventing the robot from approaching the target state sufficiently closely, whereas increasing N enables the optimizer to identify feasible trajectories around the obstacle. Similar horizon-dependent behavior can also be observed for MPC-DHOCBF and MPC-TLR. The safety-constraint parameters determine the conserva-tiveness of the resulting trajectories. For MPC-HOCBF and MPC-DHOCBF, smaller values of ki and γi, respectively, impose more restrictive safety conditions, causing the robot to begin avoiding the obstacle earlier and maintain a larger minimum clearance from its boundary. In contrast, for MPC-DC and MPC-TLR, a larger value of δ produces a more conservative trajectory and consequently a larger minimum distance from the obstacle. These results illustrate the trade-off between conservativeness, feasibility, and trajectory ef-ficiency introduced by both the prediction horizon and the safety-constraint parameters. Figure 2 corresponds to the four trajectory plots in Fig. 1 and shows the distance from the robot to the obstacle bound-ary over time. Among the four approaches, MPC-HOCBF maintains the largest clearance and is therefore the safest under the tested settings, but its stronger conservativeness also leads to longer avoidance trajectories. The other three methods exhibit different degrees of inter-sampling safety violation, as indicated by negative minimum distances. As discussed in Sec. IV-D, this is caused by the mismatch between the discrete-time prediction model used in the MPC optimization and the continuous-time dynamics used to up-date the closed-loop trajectory. This paper proposed an MPC-TLR framework for safety-critical control. The TLR was approximated numerically un-der zero-order-hold control and incorporated into a receding-horizon MPC formulation with a tunable safety mar-gin. Comparisons with MPC-HOCBF, MPC-DHOCBF, and MPC-DC showed that MPC-TLR achieves safer trajectories than MPC-DHOCBF and MPC-DC under the tested settings, while requiring fewer tuning parameters and being less conservative than MPC-HOCBF.
