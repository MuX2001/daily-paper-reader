## Abstract 

Equipping artificial agents with spatial intelligence requires a comprehensive gener-ative prior over the dynamic 3D world. We propose World Motion Models (WMMs) that capture “what was, is, and will be where across time” via sparse SE(3) pose trajectories . WMMs are built on the observation that elements of dynamic scenes can be well approximated by a set of rigid SE(3) trajectories, a minimal yet ex-pressive primitive for 4D modeling. This representation unifies articulated objects, human bodies, hand-object interactions, piecewise-rigid scene dynamics, camera motion, and even robot states and actions into a single shared space. Given this representation, we cast the joint distribution of these entities as a flexible sequence modeling problem, utilizing flow-matching with per-token noise levels. Coupled with a context token mechanism for non-sequential conditioning, this formulation supports any-to-any marginal conditioning across an arbitrary number of entities and time steps. Tasks such as future prediction, motion infilling, model-predictive control, inverse kinematics, cross-embodiment retargeting, and policy learning all reduce to the application of different masks over the same network. Experiments on 6 diverse applications of 3D vision and robotics demonstrate the versatility and flexibility of WMMs with strong performance.

## 1 Introduction 

The world that surrounds us is inherently three-dimensional and highly dynamic—a 4D space where perception and interaction are fundamentally tied to movement. Physical actions are achieved by moving limbs or end-effectors, generating forces that translate into the motion of objects, while other agents dynamically navigate and act within this same environment. As humans, we naturally develop a strong prior over these moving entities: we intuitively anticipate how others will move, plan the physical motions required to achieve a desired goal, and foresee the physical consequences of our own actions. To act and reason in the physical world, intelligent systems benefit from the same 

40th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2026). arXiv:2610.01742v1 [cs.RO] 1 Oct 2026 capability: a generative prior over the 4D world that captures the evolution of “what” and “where” across time. In this work, we present a unified generative model which provides a general prior over the dynamic 3D world. Our approach is highly flexible with minimal assumptions and handles unstructured scenes and arbitrary object categories. By supporting various prompting modes, a single model enables diverse inference tasks ranging from world modeling (e.g., action-conditioned/free future prediction) to complex planning (e.g., motion infilling, inverse dynamics). To build such a prior, we first need a unified representation that expresses the dynamic world in a physically grounded manner. Our insight is that complex, dense motion of the physical world can be compactly approximated by a sparse set of moving coordinate frames — specifically SE (3) 

trajectories. Most entities of interest naturally reduce to this format: the dense flow of thousands of mesh vertices on a human or robot collapses into tens of joint trajectories [36, 60]; cameras and rigid objects track as single moving frames; and even non-rigid scene dynamics can be represented as interpolations of a small SE (3) motion basis [43, 49, 85]. We therefore adopt the SE (3) trajectory as our basic language for what is where across time . These SE (3) trajectories subsume prior point trajectories in R3 but can be far sparser, replacing, for instance, the flow of thousands of mesh vertices with the tens of joint poses of a SMPL human [60] or a robot [36]. We further impose no category-specific structure: every entity is treated equally as a pose proxy, generalizing class-bound representations (SMPL [60], MANO [80], URDFs) to any unstructured scene. It is also orthogonal to video-based world modeling [3, 9, 70, 92, 103], which captures dynamics implicitly through color changes on a screen, entangling the underlying 3D motion with appearance, lighting, and viewpoint changes. In short, the SE (3) trajectory provides a highly compact, physically grounded 3D abstraction for modeling arbitrary motion. The question now becomes: how do we learn a generative prior over these SE (3) trajectories? Since motion naturally unfolds over time, this is inherently a sequence modeling problem. Traditionally, this is treated as a strictly causal task: a standard world model or dynamics model predicts future states conditioned on past history and actions. However, humans can perform non-causal inference as well, such as determining the actions required to achieve a specific goal (inverse dynamics) or planning intermediate motions between waypoints. The inference type varies widely across applications. To support these diverse inference schemes, we propose World Motion Models (WMMs), flow-matching generative models with per-token noise levels. Inspired by Diffusion Forcing [15] and discrete diffusion [2, 69], WMMs treat each 6D SE (3) pose at a given time step as an independent token, assign each its own noise level, and denoise the entire token field in a single shared forward pass. Once trained, a WMM admits inference over arbitrary numbers of entities and time steps simply by dictating which tokens are clean (the condition) and which are noised (the prediction). Given past tokens, it acts as a future predictor p(s′|s); applied to action tokens, it becomes a feed-forward policy p(a|s); and when both past states and actions are provided, it acts as an action-conditioned dynamics model p(s′|s, a ) supporting model-predictive control. Ultimately, WMMs realize any 

p(unknown |known ). By simply changing what is known, the exact same network transitions seam-lessly between serving as a policy, an action prior, and a world model. While standard trajectory modeling assumes a strictly consecutive temporal horizon, many practical tasks require reasoning beyond it. For example, planning a complex interaction often relies on an abstract goal state situated arbitrarily far in the future. Similarly, tasks like cross-embodiment retargeting require inferring correspondences between trajectories, such as those of a human and a robot. Providing a few paired demonstration frames from outside the current episode serves as an in-context template that tells the model “what is what.” To support these non-consecutive anchors, we enhance the flexibility of WMMs by introducing a set of context SE (3) tokens . Rather than being bound to the continuous physical timeline of the current episode, they are assigned to a decoupled context timeline with its own learnable positional embeddings. Mechanically, these context tokens are treated identically to real trajectory tokens, meaning they can seamlessly condition the model or be generated themselves. Together, this general SE (3) formulation and our flexible sequence modeling enable WMMs to demonstrate broad versatility across applications, including robot policies and model-predictive control (MPC), scene future prediction, human-object interaction generation, and multi-embodiment humanoid retargeting (Sec. 3.1), all with SOTA performance. 2Contributions. We propose a general formulation that uses the sparse SE (3) trajectory as a unified representation for 4D world modeling. To learn a flexible generative prior over this space, we introduce WMMs, flexible flow-matching models with per-token noise levels. By supporting any-to-any marginal conditioning alongside a context token mechanism, WMMs deliver strong performance across diverse 3D vision and robotics tasks. 

## 2 World Motion Models 

2.1 Representation: A Language for Motion Modeling 

World Motion Models aim to answer what was, is, and will be where across time. The thread connecting an entity at t1 with the same entity at t2 is its motion, and motion in real-world scenes is largely low-rank: a complex scene is driven by a relatively small set of eigen rigid trajectories, while dense spatial deformation arises as a local combination of those bases [43, 49, 85]. We assume the dynamic 3D world can be approximated by a finite set of rigid SE(3) trajectories, with dense spatial deformation recovered by interpolation, skinning, or binary assignment. SE(3) pose trajectories serve as the language with which we model the dynamic world. We leave modeling general linear local motion (scaling, shearing) to future work. 

Coverage. As Fig. 2-(A) shows, this primitive approximates the motion of the principal subjects of 3D vision and robotics. Scene future prediction over 3D point trajectories [36, 46, 48] is the rotation-free special case of SE(3) trajectories. Human and hand-object motion fits naturally: parametric body and hand models [47, 60, 80, 106] are sets of bone-joint SE(3) frames, and articulated parts contribute additional rigid trajectories with per-part local shape. Robot kinematics treats each link as one rigid trajectory plus its mesh, the substrate for motion planning, retargeting, and control. Robot state and action are equally expressible whenever the controller (PD gains, etc.) is fixed, since the action then re-duces to a set of virtual target poses. Thus, the same language captures both the observation and action of a standard control problem. Camera motion is, by definition, an SE(3) trajectory of the viewpoint. 

A pose at a time as a token. The elementary unit of our representation is a continuous token of “where”, given by a pose T (l) ∈ SE (3) and paired with a feature F (l) ∈ RD that supplies the “what” at time l. 2.3 Inference: Various Marginal Distributions from the Same WMMs 

A trained WMM is an entire family of conditional samplers under one set of weights. At inference, the user picks a per-token mask of observed and target tokens, sets λi,l = 0 on the former and λi,l = 1 

on the latter, and integrates Mθ from λ = 1 down to 0: observed tokens stay as input, while target tokens are transported by the velocity field, yielding a sample from P (Tunk | T obs , F, A). Different masks cover various applications (Fig. 2-C), for example: 

(i) Future prediction and policy. λi,l = 0 for l ≤ l0, λi,l = 1 for l > l 0. State-only entities give future prediction; with action entities in T , the same mask is a robot policy. 

(ii) Action-conditioned predictive model (forward dynamics). Action entities known, future states unknown: a learned forward simulator for model-predictive control. 

(iii) Motion planning and in-filling. Sparse keyframes (possibly partial) are observed; the rest are targets: robot motion planning and motion interpolation. 

(iv) Retargeting, partner prediction, and inverse dynamics. Partition by entity: λi,l = 0 for 

i ∈ O , λi,l = 1 otherwise. Taking O to be one body gives cross-embodiment retargeting; taking it to be a hand gives hand-object HOI partner prediction (or vice versa); taking it to be the state entities gives inverse dynamics. Note that any known–unknown pattern is supported, beyond the examples mentioned above. Such a design has additional benefits: robustness to observation noise. Real observations are imperfect, and WMMs accommodate this by setting λi,l on observed tokens to a small positive value (e.g. 0.05 ) rather than exactly 0, which turns each observation into a strong hint and lets the integrator slightly rectify the token along the velocity field as the noise level decreases. Generalization to many sequences: 

RoPE in a transformer already helps the model generalize beyond the number of sequences seen during training. A simple inference-time algorithm (App. B.2) extends this generalization further when combined with the per-token noise formulation, enabling dense trajectory generation well beyond the training count.

## 3 Experiments 

We evaluate WMMs on open-source benchmarks spanning policy learning, future prediction, HOI, and retargeting, plus a set of ablations. We train a separate WMM checkpoint on each benchmark’s training split; joint training of one WMM across all benchmarks is left to future work. The entities modeled by a WMM are defined according to the task: examples include a link or joint of a robot, human body, or object (Secs. 3.1, 3.4, and 3.3), or any prompted query point in the general background (Sec. 3.2); see below for details.

3.3 OMOMO: Human-Object Interaction 

OMOMO [50] is a benchmark for object-conditioned full-body human-object interaction synthesis: given a partial object keyframe and a language prompt, the model generates the body and object motion. We follow the protocol of CHOIS [51]. Setup. The entity set comprises the SMPL body joints and the interaction object’s rigid poses. The Context tokens are always set to a SMPL human in an A-pose (which conveys entity identities) and the object at the origin. The time window is set to the dataset’s full length of 120 frames. Note that in this human body example and the following humanoid and hand experiments, WMM directly models pose trajectories even when a structure such as the SMPL model is known, enabling WMM to model general and diverse morphologies within a single architecture and model. The following visualizations are produced by directly animating the meshes with linear blend skinning (LBS) or rigid transformations using the poses predicted by WMM, showing the accuracy of our model. To conduct a fair comparison (e.g., baselines use SMPL parameters to compute metrics), we convert the output of WMM to the corresponding parametric space; further discussion of this projection is provided in Appendix A.3. 

Results. Tab. 4 and Fig. 6 show comparable or even better performance on the HOI synthesis task. Fig. 6 further shows that when CHOIS produces implausible predictions in which the human intersects the object, the WMM maintains plausibility and can generate diverse motions. Additionally, to compare model size and efficiency, we measure the parameter counts of TraceGen and WMM (674.6M vs 438.8M) and their inference times for 400 tracks over 32 steps (2.66 s vs 4.18 s). 

3.4 Human and Humanoid: Kinematic Retargeting 

We construct a benchmark that takes a SMPL human motion sequence and produces a Unitree G1 humanoid [84] trajectory. We evaluate retargeting quality by feeding the trajectory to a low-level robot controller (TWIST [97]), rolling it out in physics simulation, and measuring tracking error and success rate. Motion Generation and World Models. 

Existing generative motion models are frag-mented by actor class through morphology-specific parameterizations [41, 53, 83], while general unified approaches [36, 48, 70] operate in dense observation spaces such as pixel-aligned point tracks, 3D flow, or video entangling 3D dynamics with ap-pearance, or solely in 2D pixel motion space [68, 77, 78]. A piecewise-rigid lineage uses SE (3) 

transforms [10, 33] but is restricted to single-agent or fixed-object settings. WMMs instead encode every moving unit (robot link, body part, scene primitive) as a (sparse) set of SE (3) tokens in a shared abstraction, leading to a potentially more unified and shared motion prior over all moving subjects in the physical world. 

Flexible Policies and Sequence Modeling. Recent work couples world modeling with policy via video–action models [52, 56, 105] or latent-dynamics planners [28, 29], but ties the world model to a fixed morphology or pixel tokens. Diffusion Forcing [15] and discrete masked diffusion [2] introduce per-token noise schedules for flexible conditioning. Specifically, Diffusion Forcing [15] is the modeling method closest to ours. Although theoretically general, it primarily focuses on temporal scheduling across sequence frames. Our method generalizes the idea of per-token noise to spatial and temporal axes, and our architectural design makes this practical for more than 10K tokens. We provide a unified representation of heterogeneous SE(3) trajectories and add a decoupled context-token mechanism, so one network spans prediction, policy rollout, and non-causal planning. See App. B.1 for an extended discussion. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We propose World Motion Models, a unified generative prior over the dynamic 3D world via sparse 

SE(3) pose trajectories. A per-token-noise-level flow-matching model with context tokens supports any-marginal conditional inference from one network, attaining state-of-the-art results on policy learning, scene future prediction, human-object interaction, and humanoid retargeting. 

Limitations. (i) We train one WMM checkpoint per benchmark; joint training across domains and increasing the amount of supervision data are left to future work. (ii) Generalizing SE (3) to more general linear groups is also a future direction. (iii) The current framework requires geometry (e.g., depth and estimated current and past states) as input; relaxing these requirements and enabling direct inference from RGB videos are also directions for future work. (iv) Another limitation of the current model is that it does not generalize to novel morphologies unseen during training; further investigation of in-context learning with our proposed context mechanism may suggest solutions in the future.

## B Technical appendices and supplementary material 

B.1 Related Work Motion Generation and 4D World Modeling. Generative motion modeling has historically fragmented along actor classes. Body, human-human interaction, hand-object interaction, and scene dynamics each use their own state parameterization and task interface [7, 23, 41, 51, 83, 90]. A more recent line of work on unification scales LM backbones, million-scale corpora, compositional priors, and motion-understanding foundations on human motion alone [4, 53, 67, 72, 87, 91, 99], and three further papers consolidate human-side tasks under a single prior. MAGNet [65] models multi-agent body behavior with diffusion-forced canonical pairwise transforms, UMO [20] casts object-aware 19 human motion as a unified in-context problem, and FUSION [25] trains an unconditional SMPL-X body and hand prior. All of these inherit a body-parametric state and so remain actor-homogeneous within the human family. A second line writes motion into a shared observation space that crosses morphologies. MoMap [48] diffuses pixel-aligned dense 3D motion maps; PointWorld [36] rolls point cloud and gripper flow as a forward simulator; TraceGen [46] flow-matches camera-frame 3D traces conditioned on a single RGB-D start frame and language; GeneralFlow [96] predicts dense 3D flow from RGB-D; EC-Flow [18] maps embodiment-centric pixel flow to end-effector 

SE(3) via inverse kinematics; ATM [88] pretrains 2D point tracks as a representation for behavior cloning; TAPNext [102] casts point tracking itself as causal next-token prediction; and sparse-track or sparse-observation diffusion also extends to dexterous manipulation and SMPL completion [13, 44]. These methods share an observation space across actors, but the underlying tokens are translation-only points or dense flow that carry no per-entity identity and discard the rigid-body rotation that drives contact and articulation. A third line generates ambient 4D scene dynamics with explicit trajectories: Comp4D [89] factorizes a 4D world into per-object trajectories plus per-object deformation, and TC4D [5] conditions 4D generation on explicit SE(3) trajectories, but both target per-scene synthesis rather than a generative prior over heterogeneous moving entities. A piecewise-rigid lineage models scenes directly as per-part SE(3) transforms: SE3-Nets [10] and SE3-Pose-Nets [11] warp tabletop point clouds through K learned rigid motions conditioned on one robot’s actions, OASIS [16] predicts the end-effector pose trajectory for manipulation, and SPOT [33] denoises source-target object pose trajectories from a few demonstrations, yet each remains a single-agent system over a fixed rigid-object set tied to one manipuland or one robot. The closest points of comparison to WMMs are therefore MAGNet and UMO (unified conditioning, human-only), TraceGen and MoMap (trajectory-space world modeling without rotation or per-entity identity), and SPOT together with SE3-Nets ( SE(3) -trajectory denoising on a fixed manipuland set), each of which stays inside one of these regimes. WMMs instead encode every moving unit, whether a robot link, hand, body, object, or scene primitive, as a sparse SE(3) token in a shared rot6d+translation space (Sec. 2.1), so one denoiser is promptable across actor classes and queries arbitrary entities at arbitrary time steps, rather than tracking a fixed dense grid of points or a single morphology family. 

World Models and Robot Policy. A unified motion substrate must also support rollout and policy. Pixel-space world-action models keep the substrate in 2D video: DreamDojo [26], DreamZero [95], and Cosmos [70] synthesize frames or extract actions from pretrained video diffusion backbones, and World-VLA-Loop [56] closes the action-perception loop on this pixel substrate with a vision-language-action policy. A related dense-observation model, DyWA [63], runs dynamics-adaptive world-action coupling over single-view point clouds for non-prehensile manipulation, swapping pixel tokens for a 3D point cloud but keeping the world model in a dense observation space. Latent-dynamics planners abstract observations into a learned latent representation: Hierarchical Humanoid WM [29] and DreamerV3 [28] couple latent rollouts with model-predictive control or value-based control, but each instance hard-codes the morphology or the target RL environment. Two recent approaches directly model heterogeneous forward dynamics: Cross-Embodiment WM [30] runs particle-graph rollouts and Geom4D-Robot [59] jointly denoises RGB and pointmap, yet both recover SE(3) only post-hoc through an additional pose-extraction step rather than treating SE(3) as the modeling primitive; imagination-policy goal generators [34] and long-horizon scene or traffic generators [75, 98] likewise stay morphology- or domain-locked. Vision-language-action policies form a complementary set of output interfaces over various tokenizations of action [8, 79, 101], and Moto [17] bridges video pretraining to action through a latent motion-token language. Most closely related to our unification approach, UWM [105] and UVA [52] jointly denoise video and action and pursue multi-axis flexibility through shared backbones, but both still tie the world-model side to pixel tokens. WMMs close this gap by predicting SE(3) trajectories of every tracked entity under a per-token conditioning mask, so one flow-matching model with per-token noise serves as a policy, predictor, and world simulator across arm, humanoid, dexterous hand, human, and tabletop settings, with no morphology-specific head and no separate world and policy modules (Sec. 2.3). 

Sequence Modeling with Flexible Conditioning. Diffusion forcing [15] assigns each token an independent noise level, so any subset of tokens can be revealed, in-filled, or rolled out from one trained network, and CausalFusion [22] reconciles autoregressive and diffusion training under the same per-token schedule. This primitive has propagated along several tracks: streaming video adopts diffusion forcing for long-horizon rollouts and live generation [35, 37, 55]; motion and robot control adopt the same schedule on body, action, or interaction tokens [12, 32, 38, 39]; discrete 20 and masked sequence models reach the same flexible-conditioning behavior through token-level masking schedules [2, 14, 86]; a separate trajectory-level denoising line conditions whole state-action plans on goals or rewards [19, 40]; and earlier flexible-conditioning recipes provide classifier-free guidance, masked image inpainting, and masked motion completion [31, 61, 82]. Each of these instances, however, binds the per-token schedule to a single domain: video frames, one actor’s skeletal tokens, one morphology’s action horizon, or text. WMMs apply the same per-token noise schedule to SE(3) trajectories of heterogeneous entities (Sec. 2.2) and extend the trajectory with a context token prefix on a separate timeline for non-causal anchors such as goal states or retargeting exemplars (Sec. 2.1), so one trained network selects between motion synthesis, future prediction, policy rollout, and planning based solely on which tokens are noised at inference (Sec. 2.3). 

B.2 Dense generation beyond the trained budget 

WMMs are trained on a bounded number of entities and time steps, yet many uses require more entities or time steps than fit in one forward pass (even though RoPE helps to generalize beyond the context window): long-horizon rollouts, dense scene flow, and multi-agent simulations. Splitting into independent chunks fails because two chunks denoised independently disagree at their interface. B.3 MPC details 

At each ACT replanning step, the MPC mode will (i) run one text-conditioned plain inference pass to obtain an imagined goal trace; (ii) build K=4 candidate action chunks of length 10 (one baseline plus three perturbed by Gaussian noise of σ=0 .02 m on the xy components only, with z and rotation frozen); (iii) run the diffusion agent for 12 inner SGD iterations on the candidate xy components with all 10 DDIM denoising steps differentiable and with the language conditioning removed during the optimization rollouts, scoring each candidate by the mean ℓ2 distance of object positions at the end of the horizon from the imagined goal; and (iv) execute the best candidate for act_horizon =10 

environment steps before replanning. The environment, environment seed, 200 -step horizon, and 50

B.5 More Details 

A typical WMM transformer architecture uses 8 heads with an embedding dimension of 512 and an FFN comprising a 2-layer MLP with hidden dimension 2048. The attention types are applied sequentially, one type per block. The model is trained on each application dataset using AdamW, a learning rate of 5e-5, a weight decay factor of 1e-3, and a decay step at 300K.
