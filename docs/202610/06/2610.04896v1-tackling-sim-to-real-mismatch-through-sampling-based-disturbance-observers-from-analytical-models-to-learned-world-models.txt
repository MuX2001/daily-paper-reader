# Tackling Sim-to-Real Mismatch Through Sampling-Based Disturbance Observers: From Analytical Models to Learned World Models 

Tianqi Zhu 1, Jun Yang 2, Jianliang Mao 3, Cong Li 2, and Shihua Li 1

Abstract 

Robotic controllers increasingly rely on analytical models, simulators, cost-query interfaces, and learned world models. However, physical deployment can deviate from nominal assumptions, and additional disturbances may arise even when the model itself is accurate. In control systems, disturbance observers (DOB) are widely used to estimate such unmeasured effects from nominal models and measured feedback. Classical DOB formulations are generally built around explicit plant models. This paper develops the sampling-based disturbance observer (SDOB), extending the DOB principle to a broader range of models, including simulators and learned world models, through state-rollout or cost-query interfaces. SDOB separates two observable channels: state-effect disturbances, for which the feedback state differs from its prediction, and cost disturbances, for which the same query state receives different costs as the perceived environment changes. Diverse simulation and real-robot experiments across traditional and learned models demonstrate the effectiveness of SDOB in compensating for sim-to-real mismatch and improving control performance. 

Keywords 

Sampling-based disturbance observer, sim-to-real, sampling-based model predictive control, world models 

1 Introduction 

From early model-based control to modern robotics built on simulators and learned models, deployment has always involved discrepancies between simulator models and physical plant. In classical systems, these discrepancies appear as parameter uncertainty, unmodeled dynamics, and external disturbances (Ohnishi et al. 1996; Chen et al. 2016); in contemporary robotics, the same problem is often discussed as the sim-to-real gap (OpenAI et al. 2020; Jaquier et al. 2025). Such discrepancies can degrade tracking performance and closed-loop stability. If a disturbance is measurable, feedforward compensation can often reduce its influence. In practice, the relevant disturbances are often unmeasured or entangled with model uncertainty. Classical DOB formulations estimate these effects in systems described by explicit plant or low-dimensional state-space models (Ohnishi et al. 1996; Chen et al. 2016; Sariyildiz et al. 2020). Conventional MPC operates within the same model-based setting, optimizing behavior over a finite horizon using explicit dynamics and constraints (Garcia et al. 1989; Mayne et al. 2000; Ostafew et al. 2016). DOB estimates can therefore be incorporated into its prediction and optimization loop, for example by correcting output predictions (Yang et al. 2015). However, modern robotic systems increasingly use sampling-based MPC when the dynamics or costs are highly nonlinear, nonsmooth, or accessible only through simulators. Sampling-based MPC evaluates candidate action sequences through forward rollouts. Methods such as model predictive path integral control (MPPI) (Williams et al. 2018a; Carius et al. 2022; Koptev et al. 2024) and the cross-entropy method (CEM) (Rubinstein 1997; Kobilarov 2012; Pinneri et al. 2021) rank these candidates using scalar trajectory costs. Because they require only forward rollouts and scalar costs, these methods can use nonlinear simulators without analytic derivatives. At the same time, rollout evaluation has expanded beyond analytical dynamics to perception-derived cost maps and signed-distance fields (Hornung et al. 2013; Oleynikova et al. 2017; Zucker et al. 2013), while world models (Hafner et al. 2019, 2020) learn action-conditioned latent dynamics for prediction and planning. This flexibility allows MPC to use a broader range of models, but it also makes disturbance estimation less direct. The resulting interfaces may provide neither an invertible plant representation nor a clearly defined input-equivalent disturbance channel. Unmodeled effects are therefore revealed through deviations in the feedback state or changes in the costs assigned to queried states, for example after contact, perception updates, or obstacle motion. The controller observes these effects only after executing an action and receiving feedback. Taken together, these changes leave a mismatch between the information required by classical DOB formulations and the interfaces exposed by modern predictive models. To address this interface mismatch, we present SDOB as a framework for feedback-based disturbance compensation in analytical models, simulators, and learned predictors exposed through rollout or cost-query interfaces. Together, these channels specify how observed mismatch enters subsequent rollout and cost evaluation. SDOB therefore targets locally persistent, task-relevant mismatch rather than complete identification of the sim-to-real gap. The main contributions are: 1) A new interface-level disturbance representation is introduced for modern models. Unlike conventional DOB formulations that primarily describe distur-bances through plant dynamics and state evolution, SDOB separates observable deployment mismatch into two complementary channels: a state-effect dis-turbance that alters rollout states and a cost dis-turbance that changes costs at fixed query states. This extends disturbance observation to environment-dependent cost evaluation. 2) Based on this representation, SDOB introduces a next-generation approach to disturbance observation that operates through forward rollouts and cost queries. Unlike classical DOB designs built on explicit transfer functions or state-space equations, SDOB does not require access to the analytical expressions of the underlying model. It estimates both disturbance channels online and incorporates their effects into future state propagation and cost evaluation, without plant inversion or model retraining. 3) The framework is extensively validated across diverse simulation environments and real-robot platforms, spanning analytical-model disturbance compensation, moving-obstacle navigation, latent and visual world-model planning, mobile-robot navigation, dual-arm obstacle avoidance, and humanoid dual-arm manipu-lation with PointWorld, demonstrating its applicability across different model classes and robotic tasks. The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 introduces the classical DOB formulation used as a reference. Section 4 presents SDOB, including its deployment-oriented interpretation, two disturbance channels, and compensation interfaces. Section 5 reports the simulation and real-robot experiments. Section 6 concludes the article and discusses its limitations and future directions. 

2 Related Work 

SDOB connects disturbance observation, sampling-based MPC, dynamic environment estimation and cost prediction, sim-to-real adaptation, and world models. Disturbance observation provides the observer principle, sampling-based MPC provides rollout and cost evaluation interfaces, and sim-to-real adaptation motivates correction under deployment mismatch. Dynamic environment estimation and cost prediction provide the context for the cost disturbance channel. World models represent a frontier class within the modern models defined above and provide learned, action-conditioned representations for predicting future states. 

2.1 DOB-Based Control 

Classical DOB-based control estimates input disturbances from a nominal plant, commonly through an inverse model and low pass filtering, to improve motion control robustness (Ohnishi et al. 1996; Shim and Jo 2009; Sariyildiz et al. 2020; Park and Back 2024). Nonlinear DOB formulations extend this principle to robotic dynamical systems (Chen et al. 2000a,b; Mohammadi et al. 2013; Chen et al. 2016). Active disturbance rejection control (ADRC) and extended state observer (ESO) methods likewise estimate lumped uncertainty in feedback loops (Han 2009; Gao 2003), while DOB-enhanced predictive control incorporates disturbance estimates into prediction and optimization (Yang et al. 2015). Many classical DOB formulations assume an explicit plant model and a predefined disturbance channel. These assumptions are difficult to retain when control operates on images, latent or object centric states, or rollout distributions available only through forward simulation and cost evaluation. 

2.2 Sampling-Based MPC 

Sampling-based MPC optimizes candidate action sequences through forward rollouts and ranks, weights, or refits them using trajectory costs (Theodorou et al. 2010; Williams et al. 2015, 2018a; Pinneri et al. 2021). Requiring only simulated futures and scalar costs makes it suitable for nonlinear, nonsmooth, and black box models. Tube-MPPI uses MPPI to generate a nominal trajectory and an ancillary tracking controller to keep the actual state within a tube around that trajectory (Williams et al. 2018b). Robust model predictive path integral control (RMPPI) extends this architecture with augmented rollouts of the nominal and actual dynamics, tracking-aware importance sampling, and safety logic for nominal-state propagation (Gandhi et al. 2021). Parameter uncertainty can be incorporated directly into path integral control (Abraham et al. 2020), while Shield-MPPI integrates control barrier functions for safety (Yin et al. 2023). 

2.3 Dynamic Environment Estimation and Cost Prediction 

Planning in dynamic environments requires anticipating how environmental motion changes collision risks and trajectory costs. Dynamic occupancy grids estimate occupancy and motion (Nuss et al. 2018), while Occupancy Flow Fields jointly predict their future evolution (Mahjourian et al. 2022). ImplicitO supports continuous occupancy and flow queries at selected locations, avoiding dense scene prediction (Agro et al. 2023). To evaluate candidate trajectories, neural motion planners learn spatiotemporal cost volumes (Zeng et al. 2019), while QuAD queries predicted occupancy at planning-relevant locations (Biswas et al. 2024). These methods primarily focus on predicting occupancy or planning costs, with less emphasis on treating cost changes at fixed query states as a distinct disturbance channel for online estimation and compensation.

Domain randomization broadens the training variation (Tobin et al. 2017), system identification calibrates the dynamics (Tan et al. 2018), and feedback driven simulator adaptation updates the simulator from real experience (Chebotar et al. 2019). Adaptation methods can align latent representations under unknown target appearances (Yoneda et al. 2022), while ManiDreams propagates perceptual, parametric, and structural uncertainty through task specific predictive models (Wang et al. 2026). Greater simulator fidelity also does not guarantee that method rankings or task outcomes transfer to hardware (Kadian et al. 2020), particularly when learned states do not map directly to physical variables. 

2.5 World Models for Prediction and Planning 

World models learn action conditioned representations for imagined futures, from early latent models (Ha and Schmidhuber 2018) to PlaNet (Hafner et al. 2019), Dreamer (Hafner et al. 2020, 2025), and TD-MPC2 (Hansen et al. 2024). Learned dynamics for manipulation (Ai et al. 2025), broader predictive architectures (LeCun 2022), V-JEPA (Bardes et al. 2024), and PointWorld (Huang et al. 2026) extend this direction toward feature level and 3D action conditioned representations. These models improve prediction, but nominal replanning does not usually preserve recurring execution mismatch as an explicit disturbance. Actuator bias, contact mismatch, object drift, and environmental change can therefore reappear as rollout error at every control cycle. These developments motivate extending disturbance observation to a broader range of predictive models. To establish the conceptual basis for this extension, the next section revisits the basic principle of classical DOBs.

Remark 1. SDOB requires three conditions. First, the controller must provide a repeatable rollout interface or comparable cost queries across consecutive updates. Second, the selected disturbance coordinates must affect the available feedback or queried costs; components that do not affect these signals are unobservable. Third, the disturbances are assumed to vary slowly over successive control updates, allowing their current estimates to be used for short-horizon prediction. The state channel additionally requires the disturbance-enabled interface in Eq. (11). The cost channel assumes that local cost changes over one update can be approximated by short-horizon transport. Abrupt topology changes or several unresolved motions in the same neighborhood can violate this assumption.

Remark 2. Local cost queries and limitations. Cost motion cannot be identified in constant-cost regions and is ambiguous along straight obstacle boundaries. Multiple local motions or obstacle appearance and disappearance may violate the transport assumption, while incomplete observations near sensing or map boundaries can introduce spurious cost changes. In practice, restricting q to a region around the robot or its predicted trajectory helps keep query neighborhoods and shifted positions within the reliably observed area. This reduces errors near sensing or map boundaries without resolving local motion ambiguities.

5 Simulation and Experiments 

We evaluate SDOB through five simulation studies and three robot studies spanning analytical and learned prediction models. The comparisons assess the task-level performance of complete controller configurations incorporating SDOB, with task-specific disturbance representations and prediction interfaces. Each study addresses a particular use of feedback correction, and its conclusions are interpreted within the corresponding task conditions and execution protocol. Within each simulation task, methods share the nominal model, nominal task objective, action limits, initial conditions, evaluation seeds, prediction horizon, and nominal rollout budget. Observer, robustness, and cost-correction mechanisms form part of the evaluated controller configurations; their effects on rollout and cost evaluation are described in the task-specific setups below. Repeated runs use paired task or planner seeds, and method-specific settings remain fixed. SDOB is compared with the corresponding nominal controller and, when available, DOB, Tube-MPPI, and RMPPI. Reported planning time measures wall-clock computation and includes controller-specific observer and robustness computations. The simulation tasks and the real-robot controller computations use an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080 Laptop GPU. Table 1 lists the core simulation settings; the remaining observer and controller parameters are provided in the open-source configuration files. The analytical-model studies examine state-effect com-pensation and navigation under combined state and cost disturbances. The learned-model studies examine recovery from an imposed action bias, correction of environment-dependent costs, and initial feedback calibration for planning with a frozen predictor. Closed-loop task performance is assessed using success rate together with task-specific tracking and safety metrics. Command smoothness is quantified by control total variation (TV)

5.1 Simulation Results 

1) Inverted-pendulum state-effect task: This study evaluates state-effect compensation with an analytical nominal model and compares SDOB with a conventional DOB and robust MPPI variants. The task is an inverted pendulum subject to a sinusoidal torque disturbance. Figure. 7 shows representative snapshots under the common disturbance, and Table 2 summarizes 20 seeds. Except for the disturbance-free nominal reference, all methods are evaluated under the same sinusoidal torque disturbance d(t) = 1 .2 sin(1 .2t) N m . Success requires |θ| <

0.12 rad for at least the final 20% of the rollout. The uncompensated MPPI controller fails in all 20 trials, whereas MPPI+DOB, MPPI+SDOB, and RMPPI succeed in all trials and Tube-MPPI succeeds in 19. MPPI+DOB and MPPI+SDOB have similar tail error and control variation, but MPPI+SDOB reduces the disturbance estimation RMSE from 0.018 to 0.014 . RMPPI achieves a smaller tail error at more than twice the P95 planning time of MPPI+SDOB. 

2) Eight-obstacle navigation with state-effect and cost disturbances: This study evaluates the complete SDOB configuration in an eight-obstacle navigation task where both state and cost disturbances affect planning. A constant input bias changes the robot motion, while moving obstacles change the queried costs. The obstacles approach from several directions and may overlap. All controllers use the same obstacle margin. Figure. 8 shows six instants, and Table 3 reports 20 seeds. Each run has a 30 s limit, and success means reaching the 0.35 m goal region before collision. The path ratio is the traveled path length divided by the straight-line distance from the start to the goal, so a lower value indicates a more direct route. Goal time and path ratio use successful trials only; negative clearance denotes overlap. MPPI, MPPI+DOB, and Tube-MPPI collide in all trials, whereas MPPI+SDOB and RMPPI succeed in all trials. MPPI+SDOB has a shorter mean path ratio than RMPPI with similar clearance, while its local query update gives the largest planning time.

4) Rocket Collect cost-disturbance task: This study evaluates the complete cost-compensated controller under faster obstacle motion, using reconstructed cost maps to augment world-model planning scores. The rocket must collect a coin while avoiding moving asteroids. For control, the available feedback consists of the current RGB image and the rocket’s position and velocity. The released MMBench2 (Hansen and Wang 2026) world model uses the image and candidate action sequences to predict rewards and collision-risk scores through its reward head. A nominal motion model predicts rocket positions along each candidate path, and the coin detected in the image supplies the goal. The controller combines the learned scores with the distance to the coin and penalties on control effort and variation. For SDOB, the decoder reconstructs each current image, from which asteroid pixels are extracted to define an obstacle-distance cost map. The cost observer needs two successive maps queried at the same 17 × 17 grid points to estimate how the cost pattern moves. The controller then shifts the current map according to this estimate and adds a penalty where the predicted motion raises the cost along a candidate path. Thus, the reward head supplies the basic planning scores, while the reconstructed cost maps supply the SDOB correction. We increase asteroid speed to 1.5 times its nominal value and replan every two environment steps. MPPI, MPPI+SDOB, Tube-MPPI, and RMPPI are tested on the same 20 random seeds with the same world model and rollout budget. Success requires collecting the coin without collision within 140 steps. The controller has no access to simulator-provided obstacle states or collision geometry. 5) Visual Landing task: This study evaluates touchdown alignment when input bias and deck motion affect the landing task. The helicopter must land on a moving deck. For control, the inputs are the current RGB image and a fixed landing reference image. Helicopter and deck positions are detected in the current image, and successive helicopter positions provide a velocity estimate. The released MMBench2 world model scores candidate actions using predicted rewards and similarity to the reference image in its feature space. A nominal motion model also predicts helicopter positions and velocities, allowing the controller to evaluate distance to the detected deck and touchdown safety. SDOB uses two sets of feedback. The state observer takes recent image-based helicopter positions, the executed control inputs, and the nominal motion model to estimate an input bias that explains the observed motion. This estimate is included in both the world-model and motion-model predictions when evaluating new actions. The cost observer queries the landing cost defined by the detected deck at the same 11 × 11 grid points across successive observations. It estimates the motion of this cost map, which the controller uses to move its future landing targets. We add an unknown horizontal input bias of 0.16 and increase deck speed to 

1.6 times nominal. The same four methods are tested on the same 20 random seeds with a common world model, replanning every four environment steps within a 96-step limit. Remark 3. In the world-model simulations, planning is already slow without disturbance estimation: baseline MPPI has mean P95 planning times of 0.46 s, 2.12 s, and 12 .13 s for Reacher, Rocket Collect, and Landing, respectively. These exceed the corresponding simulation-time control intervals, and disturbance estimation adds further computation. In these three tasks, the simulator is therefore paused during planning, including disturbance estimation, and advances only after computation finishes. Environment-step durations and replanning intervals refer to simulation time; reported planning times measure wall-clock computation. Reducing the combined computation time of planning and disturbance estimation is therefore an urgent priority for real-time feedback control with world models.

3) PointWorld box lifting with initial feedback calibration: 

A dual-arm robot lifts a grasped box by 300 mm using a frozen PointWorld model. This experiment examines feedback calibration for planning with the frozen predictor. Forward inference is too slow for real-time replanning in the current implementation, so the study compares MPPI and MPPI+SDOB using a single plan, with no replanning or observer updates during execution. An initial 50 mm lift provides RGB-D feedback to calibrate the state-effect correction. Alongside the PointWorld prediction, a rigid-body prediction assumes that the grasped box follows the translation and rotation of the two hands. This rigid-body prediction specifies the task-specific correction prior. SDOB samples a scalar correction coefficient between zero and one and weights the candidates according to how closely their corrected predictions match the observed box motion during this initial lift. For each subsequent candidate action, the estimated coefficient scales the difference between its rigid-body and PointWorld predictions. This correction is added to the predicted box points at each horizon step. The estimated coefficient remains fixed, while the correction varies with the candidate hand trajectory. MPPI then evaluates the corrected box motion against the lifting target to select an action sequence, with the PointWorld model weights kept frozen. As shown in Figure. 14, the comparison includes point-cloud motion predictions and representative real-robot lifting results. The results over 20 trials are summarized in Table 7. The feedback-calibrated configuration achieved a lower mean final task RMSE than the uncorrected PointWorld planner over these trials. 

6 Conclusion and Discussion 

This paper presented SDOB for disturbance estimation and compensation through state-rollout and cost-query interfaces. The state channel estimates corrections to predicted motion, while the cost channel estimates local changes in the cost field. Simulation and robot studies demonstrated improved task performance across analytical models, simulators, and learned world models. Current world models have two limitations for disturbance-aware control. First, planning with these models requires repeated forward evaluations and already takes too long for high-frequency closed-loop control. Additional model evaluations for disturbance estimation further increase this computational burden. Second, their latent representations often lack a clear physical interpretation, making it difficult to represent disturbances and relate latent prediction errors to physical effects. Our world-model demonstrations are therefore limited to relatively simple tasks with task-specific disturbance representations. Future work will explore more efficient joint planning and disturbance estimation, as well as models trained with explicit disturbance inputs to support compensation in more complex tasks. 12 Acknowledgments
