# CF-JEPA: Improving Robustness of JEPA World Models via Controllability Factorization 

Morgan Byrd 1∗, Robert Wright 2, and Sehoon Ha 1

Abstract —Controlling an agent with vision requires being able to separate useful information from irrelevant background information. JEPA-style latent world models seem like a natural approach for this, as they do not perform pixel-level recon-struction; however, they are still sensitive to these distractor signals and experience latent collapse. In this work, we intro-duce Controllability Factorized JEPA (CF-JEPA), a JEPA-style world model which splits the latent space into controllable and uncontrollable subspaces. This factorization allows us to capture all the distractor information into the uncontrollable region, while we use the control-relevant latent information for our task. With this, we show comparable performance across 2D and 3D control tasks under nominal conditions and improved performance under distracted conditions, where CF-JEPA is the only model that does not experience latent collapse. We also validate our model under distracted conditions for a simulated robot task, highlighting the practical application of such a scheme. 

Index Terms —World models, JEPA, Visual Control 

I. INTRODUCTION Humans are capable of performing tasks using visual con-trol, regardless of what sort of background visual distractor information or noise might be present. Random objects moving into the visual field can be easily ignored and do not interfere with task progress or break our understanding of the world. Robots deployed in diverse environments should likewise accomplish their tasks despite such visual distractors. Thus, a reasonable expectation of a generative world model might be to have a similar invariant nature to these kinds of external visual stimuli. However, typical world models [1], [2] struggle under these conditions as they rely on pixel-level reconstruction to learn their representations, and reconstructing such random content is difficult and wasteful. There have been attempts to handle this [3], [4], [5], but they often still rely on reconstructing the scene along with additional techniques or rely on assumptions that may not hold in realistic robotic scenarios. Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) appear to be a natural way to handle this kind of distraction, as they remove the generative model built on pixel-level reconstruction and predict dynamics in latent space instead. However, we find that such JEPA world models [6] like LeWM are still very susceptible to task-irrelevant visual distractor signals, and the latent space collapses as a result. Incorporating an inverse dynamics loss as in Sensorimotor World Model (SMWM) [7] improves robustness, but it still uses the full latent rather than separating distractor elements from control. A. World Models 

World models [8] are a way of learning the dynamics of an environment via generative models directly from data. Learning the latent representation via reconstruction of the input pixels is a common method, seen in the Dreamer [1], [9], [10] series of models, along with many others including [2], [5], [11], [12], [13]. Other methods for learning models without reconstruction include contrastive learning [3], [14] and prototypical representations [15]. More recently, joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPA) [16] have gained prominence by constructing the model via predicting feature-space targets rather than pixels. JEPA style models vary widely and include many methods for preventing collapse, including using a pretrained encoder as in Dino-WM [17], via a VICReg loss [18] as in PLDM [19], and via a SIGReg [20] loss as in LeWM [6]. We build off LeWM for our work, where we add an inverse dynamics loss and augment the latent space with additional structure in order to improve robustness. 

B. Inverse Dynamics for World Models 

Inverse dynamics is the process of computing the required forces or torques to produce a given reference trajectory. It is popularly used in robotics for control of manipulator arms [21], [22] and walking robots [23], [24], [25] as well as used in guidance application [26]. In machine learning, it is leveraged for computing actions given a reference video [27], [28] or motion capture sequence [29] and for representation learning [30], [31], [32]. For world modeling specifically, learning an inverse dynamics model is commonly used for acting in imagination, as in [1], [33], as well as representation learning, as in [7], [34], [35]. The most relevant case for our work is the concurrent work Sensorimotor World Models (SMWM) [7], which builds off LeWM, removing the SI-GReg loss and adding an inverse model as an anti-collapse mechanism. We also leverage an inverse dynamics loss, but we use it to structure the latent space into controllable and uncontrollable subspaces, giving improved results in terms of robustness over the standard inverse dynamics loss. 

C. Distractor Robustness 

Rejecting irrelevant distractor signals and focusing on only task relevant information is key for effective visual con-trol. The reconstruction free world model methods above tackle this by not spending capacity modeling every pixel but capturing the underlying state [3], [15]. In model-free RL, bisimulation [4], [36] is one method for generating useful, robust representations from pixel based environments without reconstruction. Task Informed Abstractions [5] and Denoised MDPs [37] partition the latent space similarly to ours, but still require reconstruction and thus still expend some model capacity for representing the pixel level view of the environment, which can be especially difficult if there are distractor signals. We avoid pixel level reconstruction and gain robustness via separating the controllable latent subspace from the uncontrollable portion, and using only the controllable section for control. III. A. Problem Definition 

We study control from pixels without reward data. For each task, we assume access to an offline dataset D =

{(xt, a t, x t+1 )} of transitions, where xt is an RGB image and at is the continuous action that maps from xt to xt+1 .The trajectories carry no reward information or task label, and we do not require an optimal policy for generation. Sufficient coverage for accurate representation of the environment dy-namics is the primary requirement. From this data, we learn an encoder f that maps the input image xt to a latent vector zt

as well as a dynamics model that predicts the next latent zt+1 

using the current latent zt and action at. At inference, the agent receives a goal image xgoal , plans in the latent space using model-predictive control via our world model, and we register the success rate as the percentage of trials that successfully get within a threshold of the goal. We prescribe a factorized structure for the environment MDP dynamics. We split the underlying state s into a con-trollable part sc and an uncontrollable part su. The action input influences only the controllable section, with the uncon-trollable section dynamics progressing autonomously, entirely separate from the controllable dynamics. The agent has no direct access to either state, and only sees the image rendered from them: 

T (s′ | s, a ) = Tc(sc′ | sc, a ) Tu(su′ | su),xt = q(sct , s ut ). (1) Here q is the emission function that renders a state s into an image x. This structure of a factorized state paired with a joint emission is called an exogenous block MDP [38]. Note, this is a strict generalization of the standard MDP format and, if Tu = I, su = ∅, and sc = s, would reduce to a standard MDP. We further assume that visual distractors are captured in the uncontrollable su. A distractor signal enters the image through q, it follows its own dynamics Tu, and no action of the agent can change it. Movement in the image background is a distractor, while the arm controlled by the agent is not. Our goal is to learn a world model that is robust against variation in su, such that we maintain high quality control performance even when the uncontrollable state changes. Un-der ideal circumstances, the factorization perfectly captures the control relevant information and rejects all other information. In reality, it works well for things like extraneous background pixels or noise, but would be incapable of capturing things that directly interfere with the task, like occlusion of the task itself. 3SIGReg Loss

B. Background 

LeWM [6] learns a latent world model end to end from pixels. It has two parts, an encoder and a predictor, 

zt = enc θ (xt), ˆzt+1 = pred φ(zt, a t), (2) and it trains them so that the predicted latent ˆzt+1 matches the encoded next frame enc θ (xt+1 ) = zt+1 . This objective 

L = ( zt+1 − ˆzt+1 )2 alone has a trivial solution. The encoder can simply map every image input to a constant vector output, making the loss zero but removing any information about the environment and providing no benefit for dynamics. This failure is an example of the representation collapse that comes from the JEPA objective without the full pixel reconstruction loss. LeWM bypasses this using SIGReg [20], which pushes the distribution of latents toward an isotropic Gaussian along random one-dimensional projections. SMWM [7] removes SIGReg and regularizes with inverse dynamics to prevent collapse instead. A small MLP is given a pair of consecutive latents [zt, z t+1 ] and predicts the action at

that moved from zt to zt+1 . Reducing this loss L = (ˆ at −at)2

requires the latent space to not collapse, and it makes the latent space have action-relevant information while prescribing no distribution shape like SIGReg would. Neither model structures the latent along the factorization of Sec. III-A. Both treat controllable and uncontrollable infor-mation equally, allowing for distractor signals to impact the representation and collapse the latent space. 

C. CF-JEPA: Learning Controllability Factorized World Mod-els 

Where LeWM works well under nominal conditions across the tested tasks, it fails in the presence of visual disturbances. To address this, we modify the latent space formulation for increased robustness. Our insight is that we only need a certain amount of the information in the image in order to solve the control task, so we should arrange the learning objective with this as a goal. Our CF-JEPA explicitly factorizes the latent space for that goal, allowing us to use the control relevant latent subspace and ignore the other unnecessary visual information in a control irrelevant latent subspace. Learning our world model proceeds similarly as in LeWM [6]. We assume access to an offline dataset D of state-action transitions on a task-by-task basis. Given this reward free data, we then train an encoder f which maps a given observation xt to a latent vector zt. Once we have the latent vector, we train a separate predictor network which predicts the next latent vector given the current one and current action. We structure our latent space into two regions, zt =[zct , z ut ], where zct is a controllable region which should affect only the controllable part of the world and zut is an uncontrollable region which should handle everything else and explicitly not model the control. The learning objective of our model is as follows. 

Forward Dynamics. First, we have the forward prediction loss mentioned above. We compute this forward loss on a per-subspace basis, where the controllable region only influences the controllable region, and likewise for the uncontrollable region. Also, only the controllable region uses the action, as the uncontrollable region by construction is not influenced by the action. Specifically, we have one predictor g that is action conditioned for zct and another predictor h with no action conditioning for zut . g(at, z ct ) = ˆ zct+1 , h(zut ) = ˆ zut+1 . The two predictions are concatenated back into ˆzt+1 = [ˆ zct+1 , ˆzut+1 ].and the loss is: 

Lfwd = ( zt+1 − ˆzt+1 )2 (3) 

Inverse Dynamics. Next, we have an inverse dynamics loss. This is to set up the latent space controllability structure above and also to focus on the important aspect of the model, which is the ability to control downstream tasks. Specifically, we use another MLP predictor ψ that predicts the action given the current and next controllable latent, ψ(zct , z ct+1 ) = ˆ act .

Linv = ( at − ˆact )2 (4)

D. Implementation Details 

Our model architecture follows heavily from LeWM [6]. 

Encoder. The encoder f is a vision transformer, using the ViT-Tiny model from HuggingFace, with a patch size of 14. The output latent vector z is 192 dimensional, split so zc ∈ R128 

and zu ∈ R64 .

Forward model. The forward model consists of two trans-former predictor networks, one action-conditioned for the controllable latent ( g) and one action-free for the uncon-trollable latent ( h). The controllable network uses learned position embeddings and causal attention along with AdaLN-zero conditioning on the action to predict zct+1 given the input 

[zct , a t]. The action-free network uses a smaller transformer with causal attention to predict zut+1 given the input zut .

Inverse model. The inverse model ψ consists of a two layer MLP network with GELU activations that maps [zct , z ct+1 ] to 

at.

Adversarial model. The adversarial model ϕ consists of a two layer MLP network with GELU activations augmented with a gradient reversal layer that maps [zut , z ut+1 ] to at.

Training. We train all models using AdamW with warmup using a learning rate of 5e − 5 and weight decay of 1e − 3.We train for 10 epochs on each task using a batch size of 128. All training was done using a machine with a single NVIDIA 5090 GPU and each model took less than 8 hours to train. 5

Planning. For CEM planning, we consider 300 candidate actions per iteration and 30 iterations per planning session. We plan out five actions and replan over a receding horizon of five. 

Decoder. (Diagnostic Only) For latent space analysis after training, we also trained a decoder to map the latent vector z

from the trained frozen encoder f to the pixel inputs x. The decoder is a stack of transposed convolutions that maps to the input image, in a manner similar to DCGAN [41]. IV. E XPERIMENTAL RESULTS 

We evaluate our method to answer the following research questions: 1) How does CF-JEPA perform against the baseline meth-ods under nominal conditions? 2) How does controllability factorization improve control performance under visual distractors? 3) Does the robustness of CF-JEPA and SMWM hold when the action space requires perceiving the state of the agent? 4) How does controllability factorization separate control-relevant information from distractors in the latent space? 

A. Experimental Setup 

We compare CF-JEPA against two baselines. LeWM [6] is the base model that our method builds on, and it shows strong planning performance across our tasks. SMWM [7], in contrast, makes its latent action-informative through an inverse dynamics loss, so we expect it to be more robust to visual disturbances which do not impact the action. Comparing against both lets us test whether controllability factorization adds robustness beyond these state-of-the-art models. For the four tasks in Fig. 1, we use the datasets released with LeWM [6]. Each dataset was collected with an established policy for its task, ranging from expert demonstrations to scripted and learned controllers. Our method is agnostic to how the data is collected, and it only requires that the trajectories cover the dynamics of the environment (Sec. III-A). We train and evaluate all models with code adapted from [42], [43]. The datasets are as follows: 1) PushT, from [17]. We use 2,300,000 frames across 18,000 episodes collected from expert demonstrations. 2) OGBench Cube, from [44]. We use 2,000,000 frames across 10,000 episodes collected with a scripted policy. 3) TwoRoom, from [45]. We use 920,000 frames across 10,000 episodes of noisy scripted data. 4) Reacher, from [46]. We use 2,000,000 frames across 10,000 episodes collected using SAC [47].

Performance under Visual Perturbation 1) Distractor Configuration: To evaluate how our method captures the underlying controllable dynamics and ignores irrelevant features, we consider the case of perturbing the scene with additional visual distractors. Specifically, we add rectangles with a single randomly chosen color which are placed outward from a randomly chosen edge of the image frame. Each distractor is 49 pixels long perpendicular to the edge of the 224 × 224 image and the width is a random value chosen in the range [18 , 36] pixels. By default, two rectangles are used, although that number N is a hyperparameter that we adjust later. Each rectangle moves along the image edge with a constant random velocity in the range [3 , 18] pixels/step. The affected area of the frame is chosen such that the task is not occluded, so under ideal conditions, control performance should be unaffected. An example of each task with the distractor is shown in Fig. 3.

D. Performance on Perception-Dependent Tasks 

Because the action space of OGBench Cube bypasses the current observation, it cannot reveal whether a model perceives the agent robustly. We therefore evaluate on a task whose action space requires perceiving the state of the agent. We use a simplified variant of the ManiSkill [48] PickCube task (Fig. 4), in which the robot moves an already grasped cube to a goal position. Unlike OGBench Cube, the task uses joint position control, so the agent must infer the arm configuration from the image. We train all models on 250,000 frames and evaluate them with N = 16 distractors. Table V reports the success rate for different thresholds on the distance to the goal. At a loose threshold of 0.06 m, CF-JEPA and SMWM perform similarly, with a gap of only 0.04. As the threshold tightens, the gap widens to 0.15 at 0.03 m and 0.13 at 0.02 m. SMWM, which was as robust as CF-JEPA on OGBench Cube, falls behind once the task demands more precise perception.

Looking at the baselines, the 4th and 5th rows show that under distractor conditions, LeWM and SMWM are collapsed and entirely incapable of representing the Reacher agent. V. CONCLUSION This paper presents a method for training a JEPA world model with improved robustness to visual disturbances. We build our CF-JEPA off of LeWM, incorporating additional structure in the latent space via adding inverse dynamics losses so that we can factorize the space into controllable, action-conditioned, and uncontrollable, action-independent, spaces. We train our model for a set of four tasks under nominal conditions and under conditions with task-irrelevant distractor signals. Our model shows comparable performance against the baselines under nominal conditions, and improved per-formance under distracted conditions, being the only model tested that did not experience latent collapse. Our experiments suggest two directions for future work. First, we validate CF-JEPA in simulation with a single family of distractors, and applying it to a real robot would test the factorization under richer visual conditions. Second, we assume that the uncontrollable part of the scene never affects the controllable part, so settings where the two interact, such as a person working next to the robot, would require extending the model.
