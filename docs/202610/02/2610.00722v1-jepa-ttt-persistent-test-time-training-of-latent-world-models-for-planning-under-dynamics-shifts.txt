Abstract. World models enable agents to plan by predicting future states of the environment, but their predictions can become unreliable when test-time dynamics differ from those seen during training. We present JEPA-TTT , which adapts the latent dynamics predictor of a pretrained action-conditioned Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture world model throughout test time. Self-supervised updates accumulate across episodes, while the visual encoder and reward head remain fixed, preserving the pretrained representation and task objective. Planning requires neither a goal image nor online environment reward. JEPA-TTT uses dense replay, which forms prediction windows at every temporal offset, retains them in a growing buffer, and samples minibatches from that buffer for predictor updates. Across eight dynamics shifts in four continuous-control environments, JEPA-TTT improves planning on every shift. After 500 test-time episodes, it reduces autoregressive latent prediction error by 83% on average and improves planning performance by 153% over the frozen JEPA world model. These results show that persistent self-supervised test-time training can adapt a pretrained latent world model under changed dynamics. 

# 1. Introduction 

Agents can learn predictive models of environment dynamics (i.e., world models) to support decision-making [Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018]. Action-conditioned Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) world models predict future latent representations from observations and actions [LeCun, 2022; Maes et al., 2026]. The effectiveness of a world model for planning depends critically on the accuracy of its learned dynamics. Conventional world models typically assume that the transition dynamics are the same during training and testing [Hafner et al., 2019b; Maes et al., 2026]. However, in practice, this assumption can be violated by changes in actuator responses, contact dynamics, or object motion, causing a model trained under one set of dynamics to produce systematic prediction errors at test time and thus leading to poor planning performance. We study whether a pretrained action-conditioned JEPA world model can adapt to new dynamics throughout test time and thereby improve planning. We introduce JEPA-TTT , which adapts the model’s latent dynamics predictor through self-supervised updates that accumulate across episodes. Since JEPA learns predictive representations without a reconstruction objective, it eliminates the computational cost of a decoder, making it a practical choice for test-time training under a limited compute budget. The model’s visual encoder maps observations to latent states, and its predictor predicts how actions change those states. JEPA-TTT updates only the predictor using executed actions and consecutive encoded observations. The visual encoder and offline-trained reward head remain fixed, preserving the visual representation and task objective. During planning, model predictive control (MPC) uses the adapted predictor and fixed reward head to rank candidate actions without requiring goal images or online environment rewards. To train from online experience, JEPA-TTT uses dense replay, which forms prediction windows at every temporal offset and samples minibatches from a growing buffer. We evaluate two controlled dynamics shifts in each of four continuous-control environments. The shifts span changes to actuation, contact dynamics, and state-dependent action mappings. JEPA-TTT 

improves planning on all eight shifts. It achieves a mean normalized held-out planning-score AUC of 0.571, compared with 0.267 for the frozen JEPA world model, and raises mean best held-out score from 0.267 to 0.678. After 500 test-time episodes, the adapted predictor reduces autoregressive latent prediction error by 83% on average. We also conduct ablation studies to assess persistence, temporal coverage, and replay. 

Contributions. To summarize, our main contributions are: (1) JEPA-TTT , which adapts a pretrained JEPA predictor for planning without goal images or online environment rewards, using an offline-trained reward head; (2) consistent improvements in planning and latent prediction across eight dynamics shifts; and (3) controlled ablations of test-time training.

Test-Time Training and Online Dynamics Adaptation. Test-time training adapts model parameters using data available during inference, typically through self-supervised objectives or entropy mini-mization [Sun et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020]. Continual and online variants carry adaptation state across sequential image and video inputs, allowing recent observations to inform subsequent predictions [Wang et al., 2022, 2025]. Self-supervised policy adaptation updates visual representations without online rewards [Hansen et al., 2020]. JEPA-TTT applies this principle to action-conditioned latent 3JEPA-TTT 

dynamics by using observed transitions as self-supervised targets for test-time training. Related work has also viewed parameter updates as a form of memory for long input streams [Hardt and Sun, 2024; Sun et al., 2024]. Online adaptation has a long history in learning-based control, including adaptive dynamics models for MPC [Nagabandi et al., 2018; Williams et al., 2020] and latent environment representations inferred from recent interaction histories [Kumar et al., 2021]. Offline world models can also be finetuned through online model-based RL with environment rewards [Feng et al., 2023]. Off-dynamics RL instead corrects the rewards used for source-domain policy training to account for differences in transition dynamics [Eysenbach et al., 2020]. Feng et al. [2026] further explores test-time adaptation from human demonstrations in world-action models. Unlike these approaches, JEPA-TTT 

updates a pretrained JEPA world model directly from the agent’s actions and observations in online transitions. The most closely related work is the concurrent work AdaJEPA, which also adapts a JEPA world model from observed transitions within an MPC loop [Wang et al., 2026a]. AdaJEPA resets its adapted model and buffer at each episode, conditions planning on a goal image that is unavailable in our setting, and updates from a short within-episode history. In contrast, JEPA-TTT retains adaptation across episodes, plans with a frozen reward head and no goal image, and uses dense replay over accumulated transitions. 

# 3. Persistent Test-Time Training of Latent World Models 

3.1. Problem Setting 

We study finite-horizon control after a single change in transition dynamics that remains fixed throughout test time. The task itself does not change: the state space, action space, observation mapping, score function, initial-state distribution, and episode horizon are identical before and after the shift. Only the transition dynamics differ. All offline training data are collected under the dynamics Ptrain , while testing is under dynamics Ptest .At time t, the environment has a physical state st ∈ S . The agent receives an observation ot, selects an action at ∈ A , and the next state is sampled from P (· | st, a t). Each episode lasts at most T steps. The dynamics shift satisfies 

Ptest (· | s, a )̸ = Ptrain (· | s, a ) for some ( s, a ) ∈ S × A . (1) The task defines a scalar score r(s) that measures task progress. This score is available during offline training and used for evaluation. It is not observed during test-time training or planning. The objective is to recover high performance under Ptest by adapting to the changed dynamics. Our experiments instantiate this setting with RGB observations and continuous actions.

3.4. Dense Replay

It forms prediction windows at every valid temporal offset, retains them in a growing buffer, and uniformly samples minibatches for predictor updates. The comparisons isolate the three factors in sequence: sparse versus compute-matched sparse tests additional optimization; compute-matched sparse versus dense stream tests temporal coverage; and dense stream versus dense replay tests replay from the same available dense window set under a matched cumulative update budget. Tasks and shifts. We evaluate four diverse continuous-control environments: PushT [Florence et al., 2022; Chi et al., 2025], Two-Room [Sobal et al., 2025b], Reacher from the DeepMind Control Suite [Tassa et al., 2018], and OGBench-Cube [Park et al., 2025]. All four were also used in LeWorldModel [Maes et al., 2026]. Each environment has a fixed task objective with a score in [0 , 1]. For every task, we design two test-time dynamics shifts while keeping the observation mapping, task score, action space, and episode horizon unchanged. The resulting eight shifts cover changes in actuator mapping, contact response, state-dependent control transformations, cross-axis coupling, and delayed actuation. Figure 4

At episodes 0 , 50 , . . . , 500, we evaluate the current model on the same fixed set of 100 held-out episodes. 4.2. Test-Time Training Improves Planning under Dynamics Shifts 

For RQ1, JEPA-TTT improves planning across all eight dynamics shifts. Relative to the frozen JEPA world model, JEPA-TTT improves best score by 153% and mean AUC by 113%, with both metrics averaged across shifts. JEPA-TTT outperforms all three baselines on every evaluated shift for both reported planning metrics. Figure 5 summarizes these results, and Appendix Table A2 reports the exact values. The large PPO-TTT variance on the Two-Room spatial-wave shift reflects qualitatively different learning outcomes across deployment runs. One run remains trapped in the first room, one discovers the doorway after roughly 350 test episodes, and one discovers it within the first 50 episodes. The full learning curves in Figure 6 show that JEPA-TTT improves as interaction accumulates. Its best score and mean AUC improve over the frozen JEPA world model on all eight shifts. We include a data-rich offline reference trained directly under the test-time dynamics to contextualize the remaining adaptation gap. It is not a deployable baseline and is not a mathematical upper bound because it uses a different offline data collection and uses 3,000 complete episodes from the shifted environment for 20 epochs. On six of the eight shifts, the best score of JEPA-TTT is very close to, or even slightly surpasses, the offline reference using only 500 episodes of online interaction. 4.4. Ablation Studies 

For RQ3, we ablate dense replay by separately varying update count, temporal coverage, and replay. Averaged across shifts, the compute-matched sparse stream improves both best score and mean AUC over the sparse stream, indicating a benefit from additional optimization. The dense stream further improves both metrics under the same presentation budget, suggesting a benefit from broader temporal coverage. Dense replay achieves the highest aggregate best score and mean AUC among the four update rules. Compared with the dense stream, it reaches a similar best score (0.678 versus 0.671) while improving mean AUC (0.571 versus 0.548). Table 1 reports the full comparison. These results support dense temporal coverage and suggest a small additional benefit from replay. We also test the effect of retaining adaptation across episodes. In a matched control with episode-local dense windows, retaining the predictor and optimizer state increases the best score from 0.289 to 0.729 and mean AUC from 0.273 to 0.637, with the same number of predictor updates. # 5. Conclusion 

We studied whether a pretrained action-conditioned JEPA world model can adapt its dynamics model during deployment. JEPA-TTT updates only the latent dynamics predictor from observed transitions while keeping the visual encoder and reward head fixed. The adapted predictor is retained across episodes, allowing adaptation to accumulate over time. Across eight dynamics shifts in four continuous-control environments, JEPA-TTT improves planning on every shift. The planning gains are accompanied by lower latent prediction error. Ablation studies show the benefit of dense temporal windows, with a small further gain from replay. 

# 6. Limitations 

Scope of dynamics shifts. We study a single dynamics change that remains fixed throughout test time. The visual observation mapping, task score, action interface, and episode horizon do not change. Because the encoder and reward head remain frozen, JEPA-TTT is not designed to handle simultaneous visual or reward shifts. 

Continual adaptation and catastrophic forgetting. We do not study sequences of dynamics changes or a return to the training dynamics after adaptation. Persistent predictor updates may degrade predictions for previously learned dynamics, including the training dynamics. Our experiments therefore do not measure retention, forward/backward transfer, or catastrophic forgetting in continual learning settings. 13 JEPA-TTT

# Appendix A1. Experimental Protocol and Implementation 

A1.1. World-Model Pretraining 

All observations are 224 × 224 RGB images. All four world models use the same LeWorldModel configuration. The image encoder is a ViT-Tiny with 14 × 14 patches and 192-dimensional outputs. The autoregressive predictor has six transformer layers, 16 attention heads of dimension 64, hidden dimension 192, MLP dimension 2048, and dropout 0.1. The action encoder maps one five-action block to 192 dimensions. The latent and prediction projections are two-layer MLPs with hidden dimension 2048 and BatchNorm. Each sample contains three context keyframes and one prediction keyframe separated by five raw simulator steps. We collect 3,000 complete episodes under the training dynamics for each task. PushT uses an equal mixture of block-local and arena-wide target actions held for four steps. Two-Room uses the upstream noisy heuristic, Reacher uses random actions, and OGB-Cube uses an equal mixture of guided and Gaussian exploration, with 30% uniform actions during guided episodes. Maximum collection horizons are 300, 100, 200, and 50 steps, respectively. These collection distributions do not use task rewards and need not equal the fixed-task evaluation reset distribution. Models are initialized from scratch and trained for 20 epochs using AdamW, batch size 128, maximum learning rate 5 × 10 −5, weight decay 10 −3, gradient clipping 1.0, linear warmup followed by cosine decay, and bfloat16 precision. We use a random 90/10 window split and the predeclared epoch-20 checkpoint rather than selecting an epoch on test performance. The upstream LeWorldModel latent prediction and SIGReg objectives are unchanged. SIGReg uses weight 0.09, 17 knots, and 1,024 projections. After pretraining, the reward head is fitted on an episode-disjoint split collected under the training dynamics. It is a sigmoid MLP with dimensions 192 → 256 → 256 → 1 and ReLU hidden activations. Training uses AdamW, batch size 512, learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 10 −5, and early stopping with patience 15 for at most 100 epochs. The selected epochs are 67, 64, 82, and 61 for PushT, Two-Room, Reacher, and OGBench-Cube. The optional decoder is trained for 20 epochs with AdamW, batch size 32, learning rate 2 × 10 −4, weight decay 10 −4, gradient clipping 1.0, and bfloat16 autocast. Neither the decoder nor its output is used for planning.

AdaJEPA. We evaluate AdaJEPA’s episodic adaptation procedure using the same pretrained model, reward head, and CEM planner in JEPA-TTT without a goal image. Every replanning step initializes a new Adam optimizer and performs one update. All adaptation is discarded at the end of the episode. The predictor learning rate is 5 × 10 −4 and the encoder-head rate, when adapted, is 10 −5, without 21 JEPA-TTT 

weight decay or gradient clipping. We evaluate adaptation on the last layer of the predictor, either with a frozen encoder head (pred-last+enc-frozen) or with the default trainable projection head (pred-last+enc-last). We evaluate AdaJEPA in three runs of 100 episodes. Before every episode, AdaJEPA reloads the pretrained model, adapts only from transitions observed during that episode, and produces one task score. We average the 100 episode scores within each run, then report the mean and sample standard deviation across the three run averages. Because no adaptation carries between episodes, we use this per-episode performance estimate for both best score and mean AUC.

A6.4. Offline Reference under the Test-Time Dynamics 

We include a data-rich offline reference trained directly under each set of test-time dynamics to contextualize the remaining adaptation gap. To construct it, we collect 3,000 complete episodes from the shifted environment, train a new world model from scratch for 20 epochs, fit a reward head using score labels from the same dynamics, and evaluate on 100 held-out episodes. It is not a deployable baseline and is not a mathematical upper bound because it uses a different data-collection distribution and receives 3,000 complete offline episodes from the shifted environment. Specifically, its collection policy is not task-optimal, whereas JEPA-TTT receives on-policy CEM trajectories during test-time training.
