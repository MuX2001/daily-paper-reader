# ReCo: Response-Consistent Locomotion with Policy-Aware MPC for Legged Manipulation 

Kuankuan Sima 1, Yichao Gao 1, Chenxi Gu 2, Kefan Zhao 2, and Lin Zhao 1

National University of Singapore 

Abstract — Continuous legged manipulation requires accurate end-effector tracking while the base keeps walking. Combining reinforcement learning (RL) with model predictive control (MPC) suits this task: the learned policy provides robust locomotion, while MPC coordinates the base and arm to compensate for tracking errors. However, MPC can compensate only for base motion that it can predict, and a learned policy’s command response varies with gait phase, contact, and payload. We present ReCo, a framework that couples response-consistent locomotion with policy-aware MPC for legged manipulation. Response shaping trains the policy to respond to commands consistently and repeatably across randomized dynamics. An identified closed-loop response model then lets MPC jointly plan locomotion commands and arm motion. On the simulation benchmark, ReCo reduces position and orientation root-mean-square error (RMSE) by 28.7% and 27.4% relative to the best baseline for each metric. Real-world experiments demonstrate onboard continuous legged manipulation with coordinated base and arm motion. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A quadruped equipped with an arm can bring manipulation capabilities to environments that are difficult for conventional mobile platforms to traverse. Tasks such as inspection, scan-ning, and tool positioning can require the robot to track a world-frame end-effector (EE) trajectory while walking. Every step moves the arm’s supporting frame: base transla-tion, attitude changes, and gait oscillations all propagate to the EE. Continuous legged manipulation therefore requires coordination throughout locomotion, beyond repositioning the base to extend the arm’s workspace (Fig. 1). Robust locomotion and precise manipulation place differ-ent demands on control. Reinforcement learning (RL) has enabled quadrupeds to traverse challenging terrain under uncertain contact conditions and dynamics [1]–[4]. Model-based control explicitly represents task geometry, motion constraints, and future references, supporting coordinated locomotion and manipulation [5], [6]. Learned whole-body controllers also demonstrate effective leg–arm coordina-tion [7], [8]. However, (i) model-based control relies on accurate contact and dynamics models, which are hard to maintain on uneven terrain with uncertain contact, and (ii) learned whole-body control balances task-space accuracy and stability within one reward, which can require retuning across terrains, payloads, and disturbances.

A modular alternative assigns locomotion to RL and base– arm coordination to model predictive control (MPC) [9]. In such architectures the key difficulty lies at the interface: the manipulation planner must predict how the policy-controlled base will move under candidate locomotion commands. To MPC, the base is a closed-loop subsystem whose command response can vary with gait phase, contact conditions, and payload. To the locomotion policy, arm motion changes the dynamics it must stabilize. Assuming that a commanded velocity or posture is realized immediately neglects these interactions. Even an accurate mean-velocity model can miss the periodic height and attitude motion that propagates to the EE. Our key insight is that a locomotion policy should be trained not only to be robust but also to be predictable by the planner that commands it . Locomotion supplies sustained displacement and disturbance recovery, while the arm supplies fast local corrections within its workspace and actuator limits; a planner can exploit this division of labor only if the base response is repeatable. A consistent delay can be compensated by feedforward, whereas a variable delay cannot. We therefore seek a locomotion response that a low-order model can predict over a useful horizon. We present ReCo, a framework that couples response-consistent locomotion with policy-aware MPC for legged manipulation. Our contributions are:

II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Model-Based Legged Manipulation 

MPC enables legged locomotion through ground-reaction-force optimization [10] and terrain-aware motion plan-ning [11]. For legged manipulation, ALMA combines online planning, torso posture optimization, and hierarchical whole-body control [5]; perceptive MPC incorporates environmen-tal geometry into continuous mobile manipulation [6]. These approaches coordinate base and arm motion through explicit models and constraints. When a learned policy executes locomotion, the manipulation planner additionally needs to predict the command-to-motion response of the policy–robot closed loop; ReCo supplies this prediction with an identified closed-loop response model. 

B. Learning-Based Locomotion and Manipulation 

Actuator modeling, domain randomization, and distur-bance training support robust sim-to-real locomotion [1], [12]. Proprioceptive and perceptive policies extend locomo-tion to challenging terrain [2], [3], while online adapta-tion [13] and parallel training [14] improve robustness and training efficiency. Walk These Ways provides a single policy with gait, posture, and velocity commands [4]. ReCo adopts this command interface and shapes the policy’s transient and periodic responses for predictive planning. Deep Whole-Body Control learns a unified leg–arm pol-icy [7], and RoboDuet learns cooperating locomotion and manipulation policies [8]. Visual whole-body control com-bines a visuomotor policy with a controller conditioned on base velocity and EE pose [15]. UMI on Legs learns to track task-frame manipulation trajectories with future-target obser-vations [16]. Portela et al. demonstrate EE pose tracking over a large workspace and rough terrain [17], and learned force control supports compliant interaction [18]. ReCo focuses on the predictive interface between a learned locomotion policy and policy-aware MPC for joint optimization of locomotion commands and arm motion.

We first compare ReCo with alternative control architec-tures, then isolate the effects of response shaping and model identification. Prediction and repeatability experiments ex-amine how shaping affects base responses, a further experi-ment evaluates the gait-periodic component of the response model, and hardware demonstrations evaluate onboard exe-cution. 

A. Experimental Setup Benchmark. We evaluate a Unitree Go2 with a six-DoF X5 arm in MuJoCo [24] at a 2.5 ms integration step. The deterministic 16 × 16 m rolling heightfield has elevations between −0.10 and 0 .10 m and RMS height 0.0438 m. The benchmark contains 50 world-frame EE pose trajectories generated from randomly sketched paths with varied geome-try and timing. Targets can lie outside the arm workspace at a fixed base pose, requiring locomotion to make them reach-able. These trajectories test sustained base–arm coordination across diverse motion demands. Each controller executes every trajectory nominally and with an 80 N world-+X force applied at the base center of mass for 0.1 s (8 N s impulse), yielding 100 runs per controller. References and their timing are fixed across controllers. 

Baselines. We compare representatives of three control paradigms. RL+MPC: Ma et al. [9] combine learned locomo-tion with model-based manipulation. We reimplement their approach with predicted wrench inputs and the corresponding locomotion training, using the same OCS2 infrastructure, horizon, and arm model as ReCo. This provides a closely related hybrid comparison with a different locomotion– manipulation interface. Model-based: Pure MPC [25] co-ordinates the whole body without a learned locomotion policy. Learned whole-body control: RoboDuet [8], UMI on Legs [16], and Visual Whole-Body Control (Visual WBC) [15] are retrained from their public implementations for Go2–X5, retaining their reward and observation designs and matching our training budget. 

Command interfaces. RoboDuet and Visual WBC require externally supplied base velocities; their evaluated low-level controllers do not generate these commands directly from a world-frame EE trajectory. We therefore pair both with a classical omnidirectional pure-pursuit (PP) tracker and convert EE targets to their native body-frame interfaces, denoting the results RoboDuet+PP and Visual WBC+PP. Table II groups these two adaptations separately. UMI on Legs receives task-frame trajectory chunks and coordinates base and arm motion through its learned controller. All methods use the same reference clock; imposed trajectories replace high-level visual or task policies.

B. Trajectory Tracking Performance 

Figure 3 illustrates the spatial and temporal demands of the benchmark. ReCo achieves the lowest mean position and orientation RMSE in Table II, 28.7% and 27.4% below the best baseline on each metric (Pure MPC and Ma et al., respectively). Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over the 100 runs favor ReCo against every baseline on both metrics (p < 10 −3 after Holm correction). Decomposed into lateral-path and arc-length timing components, the mean position errors of ReCo are 0.1261 and 0.1701 m, compared with 0.2270 and 0.3608 m for Ma et al. ReCo also completes more of each reference: its mean progress is 11.4 percentage points above the next-best con-troller, at a mean base speed (0.205 m/s) comparable to Ma et al. (0.217 m/s) and above Pure MPC (0.158 m/s). It succeeds in 75/100 runs (39/50 nominal and 36/50 push), compared with 42/100 for RoboDuet+PP, the strongest baseline by success count, and records the fewest falls. Thus, the tracking improvement accompanies greater completion and fewer falls. 

Baseline behavior. RoboDuet+PP and Visual WBC+PP complete at most 65.7% of the reference on average, with position RMSE 5.0 and 10.8 times that of ReCo. Their externally supplied base paths are not jointly optimized with arm motion for the imposed EE reference. These results are consistent with a benefit from planning base displace-ment and arm motion together, although the comparison does not isolate planning from differences in the underlying controllers. UMI on Legs is trained to track task-frame trajecto-ries drawn from manipulation demonstrations [16]. On in-distribution trajectories, our retrained policy achieves posi-tion and orientation RMSEs of 0.035 m and 4 .7◦, respec-tively. On the benchmark trajectories, it falls in 80/100 runs. This contrast highlights the challenge of generalizing from demonstration-derived motions to the diverse geometry and timing of independently generated references. 

Disturbance response. ReCo’s mean position RMSE in-creases from 0.1326 m in nominal runs to 0.1371 m under the applied push (3.4%), compared with a 6.4% increase for Ma et al. ReCo records two falls in nominal runs and one in push runs. Aggregate tracking error thus changes little under the tested impulse, which covers one direction, duration, and application schedule. Figure 4 shows a selected 3.43 m trajectory with an 8.92 s reference duration. ReCo achieves 0.0695 m position and 8.20 ◦ orientation RMSE, less than half the error of either Pure MPC or Ma et al. on both metrics. Both ReCo and Ma et al. succeed. Pure MPC reaches the endpoint without satisfying the hold requirement; UMI on Legs falls at 12.2% progress. 

C. Response Shaping and Model Identification 

Table III reports a 2 × 2 factorial ablation of response shaping and model identification on the same benchmark. We refer to policies trained without and with response shaping as unshaped and shaped , respectively. Without identification, MPC assumes instantaneous, exact command execution. Both response shaping and model identification reduce position error, and their combination achieves the lowest error. Shaping alone reduces position RMSE from 0.257 to 0.166 m (35.4%). For the shaped policy, identification further reduces the error to 0.135 m (18.7%), compared with a 9.7% reduction for the unshaped policy. The prediction experiments in Sec. IV-D examine how shaping changes model accuracy. Model identification improves position accuracy but in-creases orientation RMSE from 21 .7◦ to 24 .3◦ for the un-shaped policy and from 21 .5◦ to 23 .8◦ for the shaped policy. This trade-off may reflect attitude dynamics and translation– rotation coupling omitted by the per-channel response model. All four configurations have lower mean orientation error than the best baseline in Table II. ReCo has the lowest EE jerk and leg torque RMS among the ablations, but not the lowest base jerk. Identification therefore primarily improves position accuracy in these runs.

F. Hardware Experiments 

The Go2–X5 platform integrates a Unitree Go2, an ARX X5 six-DoF arm, and a Jetson Orin NX onboard computer. The control stack uses ROS 2 Foxy [27] and runs entirely onboard. The complete software stack, including concurrent programs, uses an average of 75% CPU and 43% RAM. 

Motion-capture tracking. To bound the hardware accuracy without state-estimation drift, motion capture supplies base-state feedback and EE ground truth on eight trajectories (Fig. 8). Mean position and orientation RMSEs are 0.0760 m and 9 .93 ◦; the shaped policy lowers them by 29.1% and 7.4% relative to the unshaped policy. Outside the motion-capture arena, world-frame EE tra-jectories for object organization, cabinet-door opening, and pick-and-place are prerecorded using VR teleoperation (Fig. 1). The experiments demonstrate onboard execution of ReCo on physical manipulation tasks. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented ReCo, a framework that couples response-consistent locomotion with policy-aware MPC for legged manipulation. Response shaping and an identified closed-loop response model allowed MPC to jointly plan locomotion commands and arm motion. On the simulation benchmark, ReCo reduced position and orientation RMSE by 28.7% and 27.4% relative to the best baseline for each metric and suc-ceeded in 75 of 100 runs, compared with 42 for the strongest baseline. Response shaping lowered normalized prediction MSE by 59.6%, and hardware experiments demonstrated on-board manipulation during locomotion. Model identification reduced position error but increased orientation error, and shaping did not improve height repeatability. Future work could address both by modeling coupled arm–base dynamics in the response model and by shaping the height response, and could extend the predictive interface to perceptive loco-motion.
