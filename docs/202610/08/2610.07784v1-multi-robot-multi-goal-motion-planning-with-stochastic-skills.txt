# Multi-Robot Multi-Goal Motion Planning with Stochastic Skills 

William Schnyder, Valentin N. Hartmann, Stelian Coros 

Abstract — As robots are increasingly deployed in groups and share workspaces to execute real-world tasks, planning their concurrent motions around complex manipulation skills becomes essential. These skills involve continuous physical execution and may exhibit stochastic behavior, resulting in variable execution times and uncertain continuous trajectories. Existing planners either limit execution to single-robot scenarios, rely on open-loop paths, or use post-hoc scheduling that prevents dynamic coordination. In this paper, we address this gap by integrating stochastic skills into sampling-based multi-robot planning by formulating the problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) over a multi-modal composite roadmap. For stochastic skills, solving the MDP yields a reactive policy that allows controllable robots to dynamically adapt their motions in response to other robots’ execution of manipulation skills. By resolving skill uncertainty directly at planning time, this approach avoids the pessimism of conservative baselines and unlocks robust, dynamic multi-robot coordination. Code for the planners is available at www.vhartmann.com/stochastic-skills .

I. IN T RO D U C T I O N 

In modern industrial and automated environments, maximiz-ing throughput is a central objective. This naturally drives the adoption of multi-robot systems, where several robots share a single workspace to execute tasks in parallel rather than in sequence. Realizing this gain requires reasoning jointly about the motion of all robots and the tasks they must complete, since robots operating in the same space can block, delay, or collide with one another. Relying on decoupled approaches, such as prioritized planning, avoids the complexity of the composite state space but often recovers only a fraction of the achievable throughput. While motion planners excel at collision-free navigation, real-world tasks like grasping involve complex physical con-tact dynamics. To abstract this, robots typically execute tasks as skills driven by feedback controllers or learned policies, rather than following purely geometric paths. Integrating these skills into a multi-robot planner presents two major challenges. First, a skill involves a continuous physical execution governed by an autonomous low-level controller that the motion planner cannot steer. Consequently, while the skill-executing robot acts as an uncontrollable dynamic agent during skill execution, the planner actively coordinates the remaining controllable robots to operate safely in the shared workspace. Second, physical skills are inherently stochastic .In real-world assembly tasks, uncertainty requires the con-troller to adjust, leading to variable execution times (e.g., a fast grasp vs. a slower one) or spatial trajectory variations. The multi-modal multi-robot multi-goal (MRMG) for-mulation of Hartmann et al. [1] provides composite-space planners for multi-robot problems. However, it assumes purely geometric planning and cannot handle the uncertain execution of real-world skills. To address this gap, we extend MRMG planning to integrate skills natively at planning time. Within this framework, we treat deterministic skill planning as a noise-free special case. As skill uncertainty increases, open-loop deterministic plans fail. To solve planning problems in stochastic environments, we formulate the problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) over a multi-modal composite-space roadmap, and compute a reactive policy that minimizes expected cost and allows controllable robots to adapt their motions in real time as skills execute. We evaluate our framework across three experimental settings. First, we validate the native integration of skills into multi-robot planning under nominal, deterministic execution, comparing how different underlying planner architectures han-arXiv:2610.07784v1 [cs.RO] 6 Oct 2026 dle continuous skills. Second, in environments with stochastic skills, we compare our closed-loop reactive policy against a conservative open-loop baseline to highlight the advantages of real-time adaptation over worst-case scheduling. Finally, we evaluate our reactive planner on multi-arm manipulation tasks to demonstrate scalability to higher-dimensional composite state spaces. II. RE L AT E D WO R K 

A. Planning with Manipulation Skills 

A skill is a temporally extended, closed-loop behavior whose execution the planner does not steer. Such behaviors are commonly modeled as options, which assume that the planner only needs to make a decision at the temporal boundaries of the skill [2]. In a multi-robot shared workspace, however, this abstraction fails: the planner must continuously coordinate controllable robots to avoid collisions with the autonomously executing skill. Existing planners compose skills sequentially for a single robot. Task and Skill Planning (TASP) wraps skill policies in head and tail motion plans [3] and chains them with a Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) solver, allowing force-controlled skills to be sequenced with kinematic motion [4], [5]. MOSAIC instead lets the skills guide the search via physics simulation, handling stochastic skills by summarizing a batch of rollouts into a success fraction that penalizes graph edge costs to yield a single nominal skill sequence [6]. For multiple robots, skills are typically coordinated only after planning. Multi-Robot Asynchronous Planning and Execution (APEX-MR) produces a turn-based sequential plan in which only one robot moves or executes a skill at a time, with each task solved as a single-robot motion planning problem, and skills entering only through a nominal reference path used for collision checking [7]. APEX-MR then recovers parallelism and absorbs timing variations online by post-processing its sequential plan into a temporal plan graph (TPG) [8]. 

B. Planning under Uncertainty 

Planning under uncertainty is commonly formulated as computing a policy over a discretization of the state space. The Stochastic Motion Roadmap (SMR) tackles this by randomly sampling configurations and simulating the robot’s motion model to estimate transition probabilities. This transforms the roadmap into an MDP that can be solved with Dynamic Programming (DP) to maximize the probability of reaching the goal [9], [10]. Incremental algorithms, such as incremental Markov Decision Process (iMDP) and incremental Policy (iPolicy), refine this construction over time by expanding the state space and updating the policy iteratively, yielding anytime solutions that converge to the optimal one [11], [12]. Other approaches make a single open-loop trajectory robust [13], or plan in belief space under imperfect state observation [14]. While their observability assumptions vary, these methods primarily address environmental uncertainty such as dynamic obstacles, sensing noise, or the motion disturbances of a single controlled robot. The Anytime Task and Motion Markov Decision Process (ATM-MDP) computes branching task and motion policies for a single robot by coupling a stochastic shortest-path solver with a motion planner [15]. To handle uncertainty, ATM-MDP branches on the discrete success or failure of high-level tasks, such as a grasp failing. In contrast, uncertainty in multi-robot settings has largely been handled by abstracting away continuous motion entirely, relying instead on discrete graphs to solve task allocation or robust scheduling against timing delays and task failures [16]–[18]. Prior work either addresses continuous uncertainty for a single robot or coordinates multi-robot skills through open-loop and post-hoc methods. Bridging these domains, our framework computes feedback policies for concurrent multi-robot motion, focusing specifically on the execution uncertainty of autonomous skills in a shared workspace rather than general environmental uncertainty. PL A N N I N G 

In many robotics applications, multiple robots share a workspace to complete a sequence of tasks as efficiently as possible. The multi-modal, multi-robot, multi-goal (MRMG) framework on which we build [1] treats these scenarios as a single centralized planning problem, avoiding the suboptimality of sequential decomposition at the cost of planning in a much higher dimensional space. We summarize only what our extension reuses and refer to the original work for further details. 

1) Tasks, modes, and the mode graph: A task consists of the assigned robots and the goal constraints that mark it complete. Because completing tasks changes the physical attachments and collision geometry of the scene, the frame-work adopts a multi-modal approach. A mode is defined as the tuple m = ( s, q o), pairing the active task assignments 

s ∈ S with the relative object poses qo ∈ Q o (e.g., an object resting on a table versus attached to a gripper). This tuple defines a continuous subspace where the planning constraints are fixed and only the robot joints q ∈ Q R

are actuated. The valid progressions between modes are governed by an oracle O(m) ⊆ S of admissible successor assignments. For dependency-constrained tasks, the oracle returns multiple admissible successors, allowing the planner to order independent tasks. 2) Mode transitions: To transition into a successor mode, the robots must reach a transition configuration where the current task’s goal constraints are satisfied. Crossing this transition instantaneously completes the task, updating the assignment s and potentially the attachments qo. Transitions are therefore the place at which the discrete and continuous parts of the problem meet, and crucially, define the entry points from which skills are executed inside the respective skill modes (Section IV).

S K I L L -A WA R E MRMG F O R M U L AT I O N 

To incorporate physical manipulation into the multi-agent planning problem, we replace the instantaneous geometric mode transitions of the standard MRMG framework (Sec-tion III) with skill modes, containing continuous physical skill executions. Upon entering a skill mode, the skill-executing robot acts autonomously. The planner’s role then shifts to coordinating the motion of the controllable robots to avoid collisions with the skill-executing robot throughout its execution.

In skill modes, the robot set is partitioned into autonomous skill-executing robots Rskill and planner-steered robots Rctrl . Before addressing stochastic skills, we establish a foun-dation by integrating deterministic skills into the MRMG framework. This provides a baseline for scenarios where uncertainty is negligible. We adapt three planner classes to evaluate how skill integration performs across fundamentally different search strategies. 

1) Prioritized Planning (PP): PP assigns a priority to each robot and plans their paths sequentially. During a skill mode, the deterministic skill trajectory is unrolled over time, and lower-priority controllable robots treat the skill-executing robot as a known, time-varying dynamic obstacle. As discussed in Section VII-B, this approach is inherently suboptimal and often incomplete. 

2) Tree-Based Planning (RRT*): Tree-based planners search forward in time from an initial state, making skill integration a natural extension. Instead of unrolling full skill chains upfront, the tree grows by incrementally querying the skill controller f to advance qskill while concurrently steering 

qctrl toward sampled targets. 

3) Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM*): Standard PRM* con-nects sampled configurations using undirected edges. To enforce the same forward progression as in RRT*, PRM* embeds each skill as an unrolled sequence of discrete states that must be traversed from start to finish. For each state along this sequence, candidate configurations for Rctrl are sampled and combined with the corresponding state of Rskill 

to form a layer of composite vertices. Edges connect vertices from consecutive layers, so Rctrl can move as Rskill progresses. This creates a directed, layered network where a graph search (A*) finds the optimal collision-free path through this network for Rctrl while respecting the skill rollout. Across the asymptotically optimal planners, standard enhancements such as informed sampling and path short-cutting are retained. The shortcutting algorithm is specifically constrained to interpolate only non-skill branches, preserving the physical validity of the skill executions. VI. A. Experimental Setup 

We evaluate our framework in three parts. First, we evaluate deterministic planning in four multi-robot manipulation environments with two 6-DoF arms, resulting in a 12-DoF composite robot configuration space QR ⊆ R12 . Second, we introduce stochastic skills in planar environments with two 2-DoF robots ( QR ⊆ R4) to compare conservative and reactive planning. Finally, we evaluate scalability on an eight-object stacking task with two to four 6-DoF manipulators, resulting in composite configuration spaces with 12–24 DoF. Unless stated otherwise, each planning configuration is evaluated over 5 independent planning runs with a maximum planning time of 1000 s per run. For stochastic experiments, each resulting path or policy is additionally evaluated over 100 independent skill realizations. Variation across planning runs reflects differences in the sampled planning process and resulting solutions, whereas variation across executions reflects stochastic skill outcomes. In convergence plots, solid curves show the pointwise median across planning runs and shaded regions show the corresponding pointwise interquartile range. All experiments are implemented in Python and executed on an Intel Core i7 CPU with 16 GB of RAM. 

B. Deterministic Planning (Baseline) 

We first compare PP, PRM*, and RRT* to assess how different planner architectures handle deterministic skill execution. As shown in Fig. 2, RRT* typically finds initial solutions fastest and converges to optimal costs. PRM* achieves comparable solutions, but constructing a multi-modal roadmap across the entire composite space can be less efficient for this single-query setting than the goal-directed search of RRT*. In contrast, the suboptimal PP is more sensitive to the strict priority ordering and frequently fails or produces substantially higher costs in tightly coupled workspaces. These results establish the deterministic planning baseline and motivate the stochastic experiments, where such open-loop plans fail under variable skill execution because they cannot adapt to the realized skill outcome.

The results expose the increasing difficulty of covering and connecting the stochastic branches required for reactive policy construction. This setting is more demanding for reactive planning because the policy must provide valid continuations across many stochastic branches, whereas the conservative planner only needs one feasible route through the task graph. The dependency results therefore expose, as expected, a scalability bottleneck in achieving sufficient coverage and connectivity in the roadmap–MDP, rather than in the online feedback mechanism itself. This higher difficulty means that in some rollouts, no solution is found for the problem, and it is possible that a plan execution fails. VIII. CO N C L U S I O N 

We presented a sampling-based framework for multi-modal, multi-robot, multi-goal motion planning that integrates stochastic manipulation skills at planning time. The frame-work represents skill execution and geometric motion in a common composite space and supports both conservative open-loop planning and reactive closed-loop coordination. The conservative planner handles uncertainty through worst-case scheduling, which can cause spatial detours and temporal idling. To overcome these limitations, we presented an anytime batched reactive planner that formulates and incrementally solves an MDP over the multi-modal roadmap. The resulting policy adapts the controllable robots’ motions to the observed skill execution using lightweight online action lookup. The planar experiments show that this feedback enables the controllable robot to exploit space and time that remain unavailable to the conservative baseline. The high-dimensional stacking experiments demonstrate that this advantage extends to composite spaces of up to 24 DoF for prescribed task sequences. Task dependencies expose the main scalability bottleneck: reactive planning must cover feasible continua-tions across reachable stochastic branches, while conservative planning can stop after finding one feasible path. In the current framework, sparse roadmap coverage can therefore limit complete policy synthesis as the number of modes and branches grows. Future work will focus on more efficient transition-configuration sampling and vectorized collision checking to improve roadmap coverage and policy synthesis in larger mode graphs. We will also validate the framework in physics-based simulation environments such as MuJoCo, and on physical hardware. Further extensions include richer multi-agent skills, learned skill policies, and explicit handling of skill failures such as dropped objects.
