# FORCA: A Force-Aware Coding Agent for Contact-Rich Robot Manipulation 

Xiao Tan 1,∗, Yixuan Wang 1,∗, Zhexi Wang 1,2, Wenzhen Yuan 1

Abstract —Coding agents can translate language instructions into robot programs. For contact-rich manipulation, a key chal-lenge is to generate and refine control logic that responds to phys-ical interaction. We present F ORCA , a coding agent that takes visual observations and wrist force/torque (F/T) measurements as input to write Python programs for contact probing and force-responsive motion. Between executions, the agent inspects force records alongside visual outcomes to diagnose failures and revise both motion and force-feedback control logic. F ORCA requires no demonstrations, task-specific training, or simulation. Across five real-robot tasks, we demonstrate its effectiveness in three forms of contact-rich manipulation: improving manipulation accuracy, adapting to changes in object or surface properties, and regulating contact under surface variation and object motion. Compared with a vision-only variant of the same agent, F ORCA 

improves grasp accuracy and task success and reduces contact loss in wiping and peeling. 

Index Terms —Force and tactile sensing, manipulation plan-ning, embodied agents, contact-rich control. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Coding agents based on foundation models turn open-ended instructions into executable robot programs that compose perception, grasping, and motion skills [1]–[4]. They ground their decisions mainly in vision and proprioception, which is often insufficient for contact-rich manipulation: occluded or reflective surfaces yield uncertain geometry, mass and friction are not visible, and maintaining contact requires responding to surface variation and object motion. Force and contact measurements complement vision by providing evidence about contact states and mechanical re-sponses that are difficult to infer from visual observations alone. Learned policies exploit force and tactile signals for reactive control but require demonstrations or robot data [5]– [8]. Foundation-model agents have used force through compli-ant or force-thresholded primitives [9], [10], inferred physical properties largely from priors [11]–[13], and revised force targets after failed attempts [14]. Supporting these diverse tasks requires agents to determine when contact information is needed, how it should guide ongoing motion, and how unex-pected contact outcomes should inform subsequent attempts. During a rollout, the program uses measurements from contact probes to guide subsequent actions and uses live force feedback to regulate its motion. Between rollouts, the agent relates the recorded force traces to visual outcomes, diagnoses failures, and revises the program. FORCA addresses three types of contact-rich manipulation: (i) precision manipulation , where force cues help refine grasp poses and improve manipulation accuracy; (ii) physical adap-tation , where actions must be adjusted to changes in object or surface properties; and (iii) contact regulation , where motion must respond to surface variation and object movement. We evaluate F ORCA on five real-robot tasks spanning these cate-gories: drawer opening, door-handle operation, cube-to-target knocking, vase wiping, and cucumber peeling. Compared with a vision-only variant of the same agent, F ORCA increases task success from 40% to 100% for drawer opening and from 10% to 100% for door-handle operation, while reducing contact-loss rates in wiping and peeling from 40.6–55.9% to at most 2.2%. Our contributions are twofold. First, we introduce a coding-agent framework that generates task-specific motion and force-feedback control logic from language instructions. The result-ing programs use measured contact to refine grasp poses and regulate motion during execution. Second, we extend program refinement to the force-feedback logic itself, enabling coding agents to revise how their programs respond to contact based on evidence from real-robot execution. II. Their feedback is visual, proprioceptive, or linguistic. Agents that do use force expose compliant, force-thresholded primitives to code generation [9], call force-feedback routines from a knowledge base [10], or have a VLM output target wrenches that it revises after a failed attempt [14]. Contact-based perception and physical adaptation. Contact complements vision with local observations of geometry and interaction state: visuotactile estimators fuse vision, propri-oception, and force under occlusion [19], and active tactile exploration selects informative contacts to estimate pose and shape [20]. Interaction also reveals latent properties, estimated by active force-based methods [21] or inferred across repeated trials by a learned memory [22]; these methods address a fixed estimation problem with exploration designed or learned for it. Foundation-model approaches infer properties largely from priors [11], [14], by matching observations in a physics engine [12], or through online system identification [13], and Matcha [23] lets an LLM choose exploratory actions for material recognition in simulation. In F ORCA , the agent decides at deployment time when a visual estimate is unreliable and where to touch, and adapts a developed program to changed physical conditions from measured interactions, without task-specific training. 

Force feedback for reactive manipulation. Force feedback lets robots adjust their motion as contact conditions change. It is widely used in compliant control and guarded motion [24]–[26]. Learned policies add force or tactile feedback to visuomotor control [5], [27]–[29] or fuse it into vision-language-action models [6]–[8]. Classical methods rely on thresholds and nominal traces specified per task, and learned policies require demonstrations or robot data. F ORCA builds on the same control principles but has the agent write the force-responsive controller at deployment time and revise it from the recorded F/T trace. III. M ETHOD 

Given a language instruction and RGB-D observations, FORCA writes a Python code with access to wrist force/torque (F/T) measurements. The program executes on the robot without querying the foundation model. After execution, the agent reviews the recorded interaction and revises the code (Fig. 1). We refer to one execution of a program as a rollout .

A. Force-Aware Program Synthesis 

For each task, the agent generates a Python task program that specifies the action sequence and the force-dependent motion logic. To construct this program, the agent has ac-cess to an initial skill codebase for perception, grasping, motion planning, and pick-and-place, assembled from open-source components under human supervision. These prede-fined modules provide object pose estimates, grasp candidates, and collision-free trajectories computed with PyRoKi [30]. Figure 2 illustrates these modules as the robot acquires a whiteboard eraser for the instruction “Wipe the vase.” The agent writes the force-dependent logic for each task using wrist wrench measurements exposed by the robot inter-face. For example, this logic can specify when an approach should stop or how motion should respond to a force error. To implement these behaviors, the agent can modify existing routines or add measurement and control code as needed. 

B. Contact-Responsive Execution 

During a rollout, the generated program uses F/T measure-ments to guide subsequent actions and adjust ongoing motion. The conditions and feedback rules governing these responses are encoded in the program and executed without querying the foundation model. Contact measurements can inform actions by refining the geometric estimates on which they depend. For example, the program can probe a surface before grasping, specifying both the probing motion and the force condition used to detect contact. The robot pose at contact, together with the tool geometry, provides an updated estimate of surface height or grasp position. Subsequent motions use this estimate within the same rollout. For actions that require sustained contact, the program uses live force feedback to regulate motion. A host-side 100 Hz loop reads the wrench and robot state over RTDE and updates Cartesian velocity commands according to the generated control logic. C. Execution-Grounded Program Revision 

After each rollout, the agent inspects visual observations alongside the recorded force and robot-state traces. The agent can write analysis code to examine relevant time windows and relate force changes to robot motion and visible outcomes. The agent uses these records to identify possible causes of failure and revise the relevant code. Changes can affect motion parameters or contact conditions, and can also modify the control logic or action sequence. Figure 3 illustrates one such revision in cucumber peeling. Skin remains exactly where the recorded force shows that the blade lost contact, so the agent makes the blade wait for contact before moving on. Before execution, checks screen the revised code and planned motions for errors, reachability, and workspace vi-olations. Revision stops when the agent declares the program converged or when either the rollout or wall-clock budget is exhausted. Development budgets and evaluation protocols are described in Sec. IV-A. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

We consider three ways in which force/torque feedback can benefit manipulation: (i) improving manipulation accuracy , by complementing visual estimates with contact-based geomet-ric information to refine grasp poses and guide subsequent actions; (ii) adapting to physical changes , by providing evi-dence about how object properties affect action outcomes to adjust manipulation accordingly; and (iii) regulating contact ,by using live force measurements to adjust ongoing motion and maintain contact as surfaces vary or objects move. Our experiments evaluate how effectively F ORCA uses contact in-formation across these three settings, combining comparisons against a vision-only agent with case studies of the generated programs. We additionally examine how F ORCA revises its programs across iterations and the time and robot interactions required.

Execution and evaluation. The agent generates a complete task program, executes it, inspects the resulting records, and revises the program. One rollout consists of the agent writing or revising the task program and executing it once on the real robot. Each run is limited to 20 rollouts and 4 hours of wall-clock time. The run stops when the agent declares the program converged, after which we freeze the final program and run it over repeated trials and assess the physical task outcome; otherwise, it ends when either limit is reached. A rollout whose execution reaches the hard-coded 30 N force limit is stopped and counted as a failure. Human involvement is limited to execution authorization, scene operations, and final acceptance assessment. 

Baseline policy. We compare FORCA with a vision-only agent that uses the same foundation model, task instructions, and basic skills, but has no access to force/torque data in program generation, revision, or execution: it receives no live or recorded wrench and no force-responsive control routines. Its programs guide motion with visual observations and robot state, and the agent can write visual feedback code and in-spect visual outcomes when revising them. Robot-level safety protections remain active but do not expose force data to the agent, and the force recordings used for offline evaluation are withheld from it.

Our evaluation leaves several open questions about the cost and scope of deployment-time adaptation. (i) Iteration cost. Each iteration takes several minutes, with agent reasoning accounting for most of the time (Table IV). The current refinement process also relies on real-robot roll-outs. Future work could investigate ways to reduce reasoning overhead and whether preliminary program refinement in simulation can reduce physical trials, while evaluating how well the resulting programs transfer to the robot. (ii) Contact sensing. FORCA relies on wrist wrench mea-surements for contact feedback. These measurements capture the combined effects of contact but do not directly reveal where contact occurs or how forces are distributed across the fingers. This limits the contact information available to the agent. Future work could explore distributed tactile sensing and evaluate its usefulness for contact diagnosis and finer manipulation. (iii) Task complexity. For tasks involving longer action sequences or tighter force constraints, the agent may struggle to diagnose failures and revise the program effectively, failing to produce a successful program even after repeated attempts. Future work could investigate whether improved reasoning and tool use help the agent solve such tasks. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented F ORCA , a coding agent that learns at deploy-ment time by generating and revising robot programs using force feedback. Our real-robot experiments show that F ORCA 

can refine geometric estimates through contact, adapt existing programs to changed physical conditions, and maintain contact during wiping and peeling, without task demonstrations or task-specific model training. We view this as a step toward deployment-time learn-ing in which physical experience accumulates in executable, inspectable programs. Over repeated deployments, an agent could retain useful contact routines together with evidence about when they work, then revise them as conditions change. Further progress requires more reliable reasoning from contact signals and a more efficient revision process, so that agents can solve complex tasks with fewer physical trials and manual resets. Policies learned through imitation or built on vision-language-action models could also serve as skills within these programs.
