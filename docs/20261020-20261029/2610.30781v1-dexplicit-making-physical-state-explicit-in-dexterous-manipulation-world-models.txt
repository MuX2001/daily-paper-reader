ABSTRACT
Before acting on an object, a robot needs to anticipate how its hand motion will change the physical
world and what the resulting scene will look like. Existing approaches typically entangle action-
conditioned dynamics, camera motion, and visual synthesis within a video generation model, or
represent dynamics as point trajectories without explicit object structure. We introduce DEXPLICIT,
a dexterous manipulation world model that instead predicts how the world evolves explicitly in metric
3D before rendering its visual consequences. Given an initial scene and a future hand trajectory, a
lightweight 3D dynamics network predicts object-centric motion and deformation, providing struc-
tured and geometrically grounded future world states. These predictions then serve as a strong scaffold
for video generation: appearance is transported through the predicted 3D motion and projected into
future views, allowing the renderer to focus on completing uncertain and newly visible content
rather than implicitly learning world dynamics from pixels. This explicit-3D-first decomposition also
enables efficient causal generation, requiring only two denoising steps per block while conditioning
on generated history. Across HOT3D, OakInk2, and PhysTwin, DEXPLICIT reduces moved-point
error by 27–40% relative to PointWorld and improves object fidelity IoU+ by 47% on HOT3D under
a shared renderer. Our design with two-step causal generation achieves a 57× speedup from our 14B
bi-directional configuration in video synthesis. Project page: https://dexplicit.github.io.

Introduction
World models for dexterous manipulation increasingly use action-conditioned video generation (Ha and Schmidhuber,
2018; Hafner et al., 2023; Hansen et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024; NVIDIA, 2025)
to predict the visual consequences of an agent’s actions. However, visual plausibility does not ensure that an object
responds correctly to the action or preserves its identity throughout the interaction. This gap is especially important in
dexterous manipulation, where small errors in object pose or local geometry can break hand-object contact relationships.
A key difficulty in using action-conditioned video generation for this task is that pixels entangle the object’s physical
evolution only with its visual appearance (Du et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2025; Goswami
et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). A model may synthesize a convincing grasp while placing the object at an incorrect 3D
pose, or allow its geometry and surface features to drift through occlusion. These failures reflect the burden of leaving
physical state implicit: the generator must learn how hand actions change the object while simultaneously determining
how those changes appear under varying viewpoints and occlusion (Sudhakar et al., 2024; Akkerman et al., 2025; Wang
et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2025; Goswami et al., 2025).
Metric 3D prediction (Huang et al., 2026, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Duisterhof et al., 2026; Soraki et al., 2026) provides
this missing interface by representing the object’s physical evolution as an explicit intermediate state for visual synthesis.
PointWorld expresses actions and scene evolution as point trajectories in a shared coordinate system (Huang et al.,
2026), making motion measurable before rendering. However, this generic point trajectory does not explicitly encode
the distinct structures of the hand and the manipulated object (Goswami et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026;
Xie et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2025). In physical hand–object interactions, hand joints are linked by a kinematic hierarchy,
whereas object points share coherent motion with local changes caused by articulation or deformation. Leaving these
relationships implicit (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020; Pfaff et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2025) can allow object geometry to
distort or drift relative to the interacting hand, compromising both physical prediction and subsequent visual synthesis.
To address these limitations, we introduce DEXPLICIT (Figure 1), a dexterous manipulation world model that explicitly
predicts how the 3D world evolves before generating visual consequences. Our key idea is to separate physical state
prediction from visual synthesis, rather than requiring a single heavy video model to implicitly learn action-conditioned
3D dynamics, camera motion, and appearance generation altogether. 1) Explicit 3D state prediction. Given an initial
scene and a future hand trajectory, we predict the resulting geometric changes directly in metric 3D space using a
lightweight dynamics network. By reasoning over object-centric motion and deformation, the model explicitly captures
how hand actions transform the world state, providing geometrically grounded and directly interpretable future states.
2) 3D-guided video readout. Once the future 3D states are predicted, video generation becomes a substantially easier
rendering problem rather than implicit world-dynamics prediction. We propagate appearance through the predicted 3D
motion and use it as a strong visual and geometric scaffold for generation. This explicitly accounts for object motion
and naturally incorporates viewpoint changes through 3D projection, allowing the video model to focus primarily
on completing uncertain or newly visible content. Building on this decomposition, we employ a lightweight causal
renderer that efficiently synthesizes temporally coherent consequences.
We evaluate DEXPLICIT on HOT3D, OakInk2, and PhysTwin (Banerjee et al., 2025; Zhan et al., 2024; Jiang et al.,
2025a), covering rigid, articulated, and deformable interactions. Compared with PointWorld, DEXPLICIT reduces
long-horizon moved-point error by 39.9%, 28.0%, and 27.1% on the three datasets, respectively. These improvements
also translate into more accurate object response in generated videos: under the same renderer, replacing PointWorld
predictions with ours increases object IoU+ from 0.373 to 0.549 on HOT3D. Our causal renderer also enables fast
visual synthesis, generating 32 future frames in 4.90s on a single NVIDIA A800 GPU. Together, these results establish
structured physical-state prediction as a key driver of interaction fidelity in dexterous world models, linking accurate
action-induced object motion to faithful visual outcomes.

Related Work
World models for interaction.
Learned latent dynamics support imagination and control (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018;
Hafner et al., 2023; Hansen et al., 2024), while UniPi and GR-1 connect video prediction to robot policies (Du et al.,
2023; Wu et al., 2023). UniSim and RoboDreamer broaden visual simulation through interactive and compositional
prediction (Yang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). IRASim and Ctrl-World emphasize action-conditioned robot
interaction (Zhu et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2026), supported by scalable video priors such as Cosmos (NVIDIA, 2025).
Together, these models connect visual prediction to embodied control.
Dexterous and egocentric world models.
CosHand, InterDyn, and Mask2IV use silhouettes or masks to specify
interaction (Sudhakar et al., 2024; Akkerman et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). DWM combines articulated hand control

with scene reconstruction (Kim et al., 2025). Hand2World and Generated Reality model egocentric interaction from
hand and camera inputs (Wang et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026), while HandsOnWorld disentangles these controls and
RynnWorld enables digital teleoperation (Chen et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). EgoSim maintains an updatable 3D
scene state (Hao et al., 2026). DexWM and DexAC-WM emphasize hand-consistent latent prediction and structured
action conditioning (Goswami et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2026). We focus on measurable object dynamics, coupling
metric forecasts of transport and deformation to visual synthesis.
Explicit physical dynamics.
Particle and mesh simulators learn spatial interactions through graph networks (Sanchez-
Gonzalez et al., 2020; Pfaff et al., 2021). ParticleFormer and particle-grid neural dynamics extend learned simulation to
perceived objects and materials (Huang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). PointWorld unifies scene and action trajectories
in point space (Huang et al., 2026), and PointZero learns transferable dynamics through track completion (Duisterhof
et al., 2026). ObjectForesight predicts object poses from human video, while RigidFormer imposes coherent rigid
motion (Soraki et al., 2026; Dou et al., 2026). Our shared object pose captures transport, and an embedded graph (Sumner
et al., 2007) expresses residual deformation, preserving coherence without restricting all objects to rigid motion.
Controllable video generation.
ControlNet and VACE introduce spatial conditioning (Zhang et al., 2023; Jiang et al.,
2025b), while MotionCtrl, CameraCtrl, and GEN3C address motion and viewpoint control (Wang et al., 2023; He et al.,
2024; Ren et al., 2025). ManiVideo and PAM incorporate hand–object geometry and appearance, and ContactFlow
transfers contact-based controls across embodiments (Pang et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2026; Azirar et al., 2026). DVG-WM
separates interaction prediction from visual refinement (Shan et al., 2026). Closest to our appearance-transport interface,
WanMove transports initial features and E3C maintains 3D visual memory (Chu et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2026). We
couple initial appearance to persistent object points, using depth, visibility, and forecast confidence to determine valid
support.

coherence implicit. We therefore structure the dynamics predictor Fθ in Sec. 3 around joint-aware action encoding and

Training and causal adaptation.
We train the conditioning branches and VACE adapters (Jiang et al., 2025b) using
flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023), keeping the pretrained video backbone and VAE frozen. For
efficient generation, we transfer conditioning parameters learned with a separate 1.3B model to a pretrained causal
backbone, align them through endpoint reconstruction and video adversarial supervision, and apply on-policy distillation
for robustness to generated history. Training and adaptation details are provided in Appendix D.

5.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets and baselines.
We evaluate one dynamics model across HOT3D, OakInk2, and PhysTwin (Banerjee et al.,
2025; Zhan et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025a), spanning rigid, articulated, and deformable interactions. We retrain
PointWorld on the same forecasting task using the same initial metric scene points and 3D hand-joint trajectories
through its native point-action interface. On HOT3D, video systems use their native controls. DEXPLICIT predicts
future object geometry, as with PointWorld (Full protocols in Section A). All temporal windows derived from the same
source unit remain in the same partition, preventing shared source interactions between our task-specific training and
evaluation data. Within each comparison panel, all methods are evaluated on the same fixed set of source windows and
reference targets.
Evaluation metrics.
For 3D forecasting, we report moved-point and all-point errors at early and long horizons. Video
metrics include PSNR, SSIM (Wang et al., 2004), LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018), hand PCK20, object overlap and position
error, and CLIP foreground similarity (Radford et al., 2021). The initial frame is excluded; cohorts, missing-prediction
handling, support, and metric definitions are detailed in Section A.

5.2
Quantitative and Qualitative Results
3D dynamics prediction.
As shown in Table 1, DEXPLICIT reduces moved-point error on all three datasets by
42–48% at the early horizon and 27–40% at the long horizon, demonstrating consistently more accurate prediction
of interaction-induced geometric changes. All-point error also improves on HOT3D and PhysTwin, but is higher on
OakInk2 despite the lower moved-point error.
Conditional video generation.
DEXPLICIT achieves the strongest perceptual quality and object overlap in the shared
video panel. Baselines with higher PCK20 but much lower object overlap show that hand agreement alone does not
capture interaction fidelity.
Figure 5 shows object orientation and identity during rotation and placement; Causal-DEXPLICIT retains these properties
under generated-history conditioning.
Generation efficiency.
Causal-DEXPLICIT generates four frames in 0.54 seconds and all 32 in 4.90 seconds, with
substantially higher object overlap than similarly fast autoregressive baselines (Table 2) operating at comparable latency,
demonstrating a favorable trade-off between generation efficiency and object-level consistency. These results further
validate our explicit-3D-first design: by resolving object dynamics in lightweight 3D prediction before visual synthesis,
the renderer can produce geometrically consistent future observations without relying on expensive iterative video
generation.

Conclusion
We presented DEXPLICIT, a dexterous manipulation world model built around a simple principle: predict how the
world changes explicitly in 3D before synthesizing how those changes look. By separating structured metric dynamics
from visual synthesis, our approach provides geometrically grounded future states while reducing the burden on the
video generator to implicitly learn action-conditioned world dynamics. The predicted 3D states further provide a strong
scaffold for efficient causal rendering, enabling coherent future observations with substantially reduced generation cost.
Reproducibility Statement
We provide detailed evaluation sets, protocols, prediction horizons, baseline interfaces, metric definitions, and missing-
prediction handling in Appendix A. Dynamics architecture and optimization details are provided in Appendix C.1,
video-generation architecture and inference settings in Appendix C, and causal adaptation objectives and training
procedures in Appendix D. Additional controlled ablations are reported in Appendix E. Together, these sections specify
the data, model architecture, training, evaluation, and inference procedures used to obtain the reported results.
ETHICS STATEMENT
The experiments concern visual prediction for manipulation. Plausible generated futures can mis- represent physical
outcomes. The reported evaluations do not establish suitability for safety critical robotic control.

Evaluation Details
For HOT3D, split membership is assigned by source clip rather than independently by window. The video split
construction preserves the canonical evaluation clip list while allocating development subsets from the remaining clips.
For OakInk2, split assignment is determined from the source sequence identifier before temporal windows are exported,
and manifest construction rejects sequences shared across partitions. For PhysTwin, split assignment precedes both
temporal window extraction and camera-view expansion, so different views of the same interaction cannot enter different
partitions.
Video comparisons use a shared evaluation population with explicit window and source identifiers. Input and reference-
file hashes are recorded, and the evaluation pipeline checks generated outputs against the declared population. Missing
generated files trigger an error rather than silently reducing a method’s evaluation set.
Evaluation sets.
The dynamics datasets contain 2,720 training and 302 evaluation windows for HOT3D, 9,010 training
and 1,127 evaluation windows for OakInk2, and 91 training and 76 evaluation windows for PhysTwin. DEXPLICIT and
retrained PointWorld receive identical initial geometry, hand actions, and cameras in the 3D forecasting comparison.
Conditional video inputs and qualitative scope.
The video comparison uses method-specific controls. Methods
retain their native conditioning interfaces, model capacities, and inference procedures. The 14B DEXPLICIT results use
predicted future object geometry and learned video adapters. PointWorld supplies predicted point trajectories and uses
the same renderer. These video outputs evaluate the respective conditional systems. The causal model uses predicted
geometry and hand conditioning and generates its own video history. The main RGB examples are selected illustrations.
Additional appendix examples use the same 14B renderer. Geometry forecasting results remain separate from these
conditional-rendering studies.
Retrained PointWorld baseline.
We retrain PointWorld (Huang et al., 2026) on the same hand-object prediction
setting. Its original action representation propagates robot-gripper surface points through a supplied joint trajectory using
forward kinematics. We retain this point-action interface and substitute the supplied hand trajectories H ∈RT ×NH×3.
The baseline therefore receives the same metric action information through its original conditioning pathway. Initial
scene coordinates and scene features, including color, normals, visibility, and precomputed DINO features and hand-
point trajectory are supplied across the prediction horizon. Training follows the public PointWorld implementation with
the same training and evaluation samples. Table 5 summarizes the configuration.
Causal evaluation.
The causal model generates eight four-frame blocks from the initial observation, using two
denoising steps and guidance scale 1. Each completed latent block enters the cache for subsequent prediction. Future
reference images are excluded from the generation history. All 32 generated frames are scored at 480 × 832. Metrics
requiring successful estimates are distinguished from those that explicitly penalize missing predictions.

Implementation Details
C.1
Dynamics Architecture and Optimization
This section specifies Fθ in Sec. 4.1, including the architecture and optimization of the shared model used for cross-
dataset evaluation.
Scene and action tensors.
Each example contains one observed scene and a supplied 35-state action sequence, in
which state 0 is context and states 1–34 are predicted jointly. The scene has at most 12,000 persistent points. Smaller
clouds are padded and masked, while larger clouds are subsampled with consistent indices across time. Coordinates are
expressed in meters without additional normalization. Only initial-state scene features enter the predictor. Precomputed
pointwise DINO features and the initial geometric and appearance attributes are projected to width 256. Future object
coordinates are supervision targets. The two hands retain 21 anatomical joints each. The joint encoder has four
Transformer layers, width 256, four heads, feed-forward width 1,024, and dropout 0.1. The separate MANO-relative
action encoder has two layers with the same width, head count, and dropout, using 15 pose coefficients and 10 shape
coefficients per hand. Joint and action tokens are appended to the supplied hand-point tokens. Unavailable annotations
are masked or zero-filled. Both absolute action states and backward differences are represented. For HOT3D, object
membership follows the sampled object-mesh groups, and canonical coordinates are the corresponding object-local
points. Legacy samples recover these coordinates using the inverse object pose at the context frame. MANO parameters
are taken from the supplied hand annotations. These structured annotations are part of the declared input setting.
PhysTwin provides sparse tracked hand-surface controls without reliable MANO joint identities. We retain these
controls through the point-action interface.
Point Transformer and object-time network.
The Point Transformer V3 (Wu et al., 2024) uses the base encoder–
decoder configuration in Table 9. Scene and action tokens have width 256. Spatial serialization uses a 0.015 m grid and
the Z, transposed-Z, Hilbert, and transposed-Hilbert orders, with order shuffling enabled. Every attention patch has
256 points, and stage transitions have stride 2. The MLP expansion ratio is 4, attention uses biased query, key, and
value projections, pre-normalization, zero attention and projection dropout, and maximum stochastic-depth rate 0.3.
Relative positional attention is disabled. A two-layer, four-head temporal action Transformer at width 256 follows the
point backbone. Masked mean and max object pooling and canonical-coordinate embeddings supply an eight-layer
object-time Transformer with width 256, eight heads, feed-forward width 1,024, GELU, pre-normalization, and dropout
0.1. The pose head uses hidden width 128 and outputs three translation and six rotation coordinates. The latter are
orthonormalized into a proper rotation (Zhou et al., 2019). Dense displacement and log-variance heads also use hidden
width 128 and predict all 34 future states.

Gauge fixing.
Global pose and local graph motion can encode the same rigid component. A weighted proper Kabsch
transform (Kabsch, 1976) A fitted from canonical points to their warped positions is absorbed into the global pose G.
Replacing (G, Y ) by (GA, A−1Y ) preserves predicted world positions and leaves the graph responsible for residual
deformation. This gives the pose refinement a consistent rigid reference.
C.3
Video Generation
Backbone and optimization.
The bidirectional model uses the Wan2.2-VACE-14B backbone with rank-32 LoRA (Hu
et al., 2022) on VACE attention and feed-forward projections. The frozen backbone and VAE use BF16, while trainable
adapters, encoders, and optimizer states use FP32. Training uses native full-range flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023;
Liu et al., 2023), AdamW at 10−4, and effective batch 21. Sampling uses 30 Euler steps, guidance 5, and shift 5 for
33 × 480 × 832 video.
Hand conditioning.
The hand branch combines 42 Gaussian joint heatmaps at 60 × 104 with a 642-dimensional per-
frame vector. Each joint contributes current-camera and first-camera XYZ, wrist-relative XYZ, backward displacement,
and three validity flags. A 12-value relative camera transform completes the vector. Four-frame packing yields nine
temporal slots, repeating frame 0 for the initial group. The spatial encoder maps 168 channels through widths 128 and
1,536 with a 2 × 2 patch stride. The action MLP maps 2,568 inputs through width 256 to 1,536 and broadcasts spatially.
Both output heads are zero-initialized.
Appearance transport.
Persistent object points carry 16-channel VAE descriptors from the separately encoded initial
image. Calibrated projection, closest-depth selection, and MANO occluders determine support, and a coverage channel
marks valid features. Three temporal encoder stages of widths 256, 128, and 128 reduce 33 frames to nine slots, and a
zero-initialized output supplies 96 VACE channels. The reference slot receives zero additional hand-conditioning or
appearance-transport residual. The appearance-transport stream in Figure 3 uses one PCA basis and color range per
sequence, with unsupported cells shown in black.
Inference settings.

Limitations
Structured inputs.
DeXplicit assumes an initial metric 3D scene and a future hand trajectory. Errors in these
inputs may propagate into both physical-state prediction and video synthesis, and obtaining them reliably may require
dedicated perception systems.
Unobserved appearance.
Appearance transport can only propagate features visible in the initial observation. Newly
revealed or heavily occluded regions must therefore be synthesized by the renderer. Although the confidence-gated
visual readout explicitly handles unsupported regions, large disocclusions can still reduce visual fidelity.
