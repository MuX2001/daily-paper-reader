# Learning Dexterous Manipulation: Hands, Simulators, Training, and Evaluation 

Luai Abuelsamen 

Independent Researcher 

luai_abuelsamen@berkeley.edu 

Abstract —A dexterous hand can change an object’s pose without setting it down. This survey covers 113 learning-based methods that attempt it, the hands they run on, the simulators they train in, and how they report results. The field has largely converged on one recipe: reinforcement learning on privileged state in a GPU simulator, distilled into a vision-only student for transfer to hardware. Almost half the corpus trains that way, nearly all of it in one simulator, whose own successors are barely used. A second and faster-growing branch learns from teleoperated demonstration instead, through vision-language-action models, diffusion policies, and systems whose contribution is the collection rig itself. Published work has tripled since 2022. Grasping remains the largest task family and bimanual coordination the fastest-rising, and almost every experiment runs on one of four hands. What the survey adds comes from reading released code and vendor specifications beside the papers. Eight hands that can be bought or built from published designs appear in no method paper here. Where the depth a hand sinks into the object it holds is reported at all, it is not measured in the physics that produced the motion. Section VII proposes a protocol for the unreported quantities; the body gives the counts behind every claim above. 

Index Terms —Dexterous manipulation, multi-fingered hands, bimanual manipulation, reinforcement learning, imitation learning, physics simulation, contact modeling, benchmarking, reproducibil-ity. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A hand is dexterous when it can change an object’s pose without putting the object down. That property, and not the finger count, is what separates a hand from a gripper. Bicchi [1] draws the same line in Sec. 1.1 between restraining an object and manipulating it with the fingers: restraint is a static question about whether the contacts prevent motion, while in-hand manipulation is a dynamic one about whether contacts can be broken and remade while the object stays held. A parallel-jaw gripper can hold almost anything and reorient almost nothing. The classical theory of grasping is about holding, and it is good at it. Given a set of contact points and an assumption about friction, it can decide whether an object is immobilized, and what force each finger has to apply to keep it that way. What it cannot do is say what the fingers should do next. Contact is what makes that hard: a finger is either touching or it is not, and it can push but not pull, so the equations of motion change form every time a contact is made or broken. The tradition produced tests. You can check a candidate grasp against them; you cannot run them on a robot. Learning changed what a practitioner has to produce. The old deliverable was a proof that a configuration holds. The new one is a policy that scores well over many simulated attempts, which means the simulator now stands in for the analysis and the score stands in for the proof. That is why the field moved, and it is also why this survey is organized as it is. It shifts two burdens onto the author. The simulator has to be faithful where fidelity matters. The measurement has to mean something. Much of what follows asks how well this literature carries those two. One recipe now carries most of the field. Train a policy with reinforcement learning on privileged state, the object pose, velocities and physical parameters the robot cannot sense, in a GPU simulator running thousands of environments in parallel; distill that teacher into a student that sees only what a camera and joint encoders provide; transfer the student. Fifty-four of the 113 method papers here train this way, most of them in one simulator, and about half of those add the distillation stage. In-hand cube reorientation is the recipe’s benchmark, inherited from [2] and still the task a new method is expected to show. A second branch is growing underneath it and inverts the dependency. Instead of a simulator and a reward it takes human demonstrations through a teleoperation rig and fits a policy directly, as a vision-language-action model, a diffusion policy, or a data-collection rig whose design is itself the contribution. Section V counts them, and Fig. 1 shows how fast they have arrived. It is the newer literature, most of it from the last two years, and it trades the reward-design problem for a data-collection problem. Which of the two branches ends up feeding the other is not settled here, but the growth is the clearest trend in the corpus. Both branches run on remarkably little hardware. Four hands carry almost every experiment in this corpus, led by the Allegro at 35 of the 113 papers; Fig. 3 gives the rest of the distribution. Eight further hands can be bought today or built from published designs and appear in the experiments of none of them. What this literature cannot currently do is compare its own results. Nearly every paper states a success criterion, but they are not the same criterion, and only 70 of the 113 say how many trials a reported rate rests on. The criteria run from never dropping the object to reaching a pose within a tolerance each paper picks for itself. Two success rates from two papers on nominally the same task are usually not measuring the same event, and none of the surveys preceding this one supplies a protocol that would make them comparable. Section VII derives one, with the trial counts a stated confidence interval actually requires. Every number in this survey is traceable to a stated source. Appendix A says how the papers were selected, read and coded, what the method cannot do, and why each coverage figure here is a lower bound. One part of that method is unusual enough to flag now. Where a paper published a repository, its reward was read from the repository as well as from the paper, so the two can be compared. Reading an implementation against its own description is established practice outside robotics, in repeatability and reproducibility studies [3], [4], in audits of how implementation detail moves reinforcement-learning results [5], in reward-design review [6], in benchmark re-releases [7], and most directly in recent work on paper–code alignment [8], [9]. We are aware of none that does it for a robotics field’s released reward functions. Section V-F reports what this one found. The scope is single-hand and bimanual multi-fingered manipulation under learned control. Parallel-jaw work enters as a comparison and not as a subject. Two limits apply to every number here. The corpus is of the learned era, which is a selection and not a verdict on the analytic tradition; and where this survey failed to extract a value, the counts look the same as if the source had never stated one.

II. A TAXONOMY OF THE PROBLEM 

A. Task families 

Dexterous manipulation is not one task, and what separates its tasks is what makes some of them hard: what is held, what moves, which way gravity points, and what counts as failure. Six families cover most of the corpus, and the last paragraph here says what they miss. Grasping is the largest, with 58 of the 113 papers carrying the label; the labels are not exclusive, so one paper can sit in several families. Success is a lift that survives a hold, and the thresholds differ by more than an order of magnitude: one paper requires the object “held 10 cm above the table for 20 s” [10], another “held at least 0.5 s in simulation” [11]. What makes grasping hard at scale is the continuum of starting configurations. UniDexGrasp++ [12] states it in Sec. 4.3, that “we are dealing with an infinite number of tasks considering the initial object pose can change continuously”. Functional and tool use is second with 45 papers. It is the family where a stable grasp can still be the wrong answer. Dexterous Functional Grasping [13] gives the case in Sec. 2.1, that “grabbing a hammer from the head or handle are both equally valid ways of using it”, and only one of the two lets the tool be used. Success is defined against the tool’s function, and the field has no shared way to state it. In-hand reorientation has 31 papers and the most settled criteria, because the community inherited one number: OpenAI declared the goal achieved below 0.4 rad of orientation error [2], and its successors keep that tolerance or tighten it [14], [15]. A shared tolerance is not a shared protocol, because the stopping rule differs. Visual Dexterity [16] measures error “when the controller predicts it has reached the goal and stops”, which makes the policy a judge of its own trial. Tracking a human reference has 12 papers and inverts the problem. The target is a trajectory rather than an endpoint, so success is a per-frame error band, and the bands are compound: one method requires rotation, translation, joint and fingertip error all to stay inside separate thresholds at once [17]. The difficulty is that the reference came from a human hand and was never dynamically feasible for the robot. DexMachina [18] warns in App. B.4 that scoring by timesteps inside the thresholds makes “the results highly sensitive to the threshold values”. Bimanual coordination has 43 papers and handover has 10. Handover is the smallest family and the one whose failure has a single moment, because the giver must release only after the receiver has the object. Dynamic Handover [19] reports a hit rate above its success rate and blames the gap in Sec. 5.4 on “occasional challenges encountered during the grasping phase of the catcher”. Not every paper here learns both sides. In [20] only the receiver is learned and the giver is a scripted arm. Those six families do not cover the corpus. Twenty-four of the 113 papers carry a label from outside them and eight carry no label from the six at all. Twenty fall in a catch-all class, mostly generalist policies evaluated on a general task suite, among them [21], [22], [23], [24] and [25]. Three adjacent families are named here instead of being folded into the six. Locomanipulation is [26], [27] and [28], where the base is not fixed and the gravity argument below changes character. Piano playing is [29], [30] and [31], discussed in Section VI, where success is a per-timestep F1 against a MIDI score. Deformable manipulation is [32], whose object carries its own state and its own physics. Ferrari and Canny’s grasp-quality measure [33] carries no task label, because it is a measure rather than a task. Table I’s six families are the ones with enough papers to compare, and not a partition of the corpus. 

B. Sources of difficulty 

Contact is non-smooth, and that is a property of the problem rather than of any solver. Bicchi [1] states in Sec. 1.2 that contact constraints are unilateral, and that losing a contact “involves an abrupt change of the structure of the model under consideration”. Its Sec. 1.4 gives the sharper version, that a rod sliding on rough ground has configurations with no consistent solution and configurations with more than one. Simulators inherit the difficulty. The re-implementation study [34] concludes in Sec. V that “there is no fully satisfactory approach at the moment, as all existing solutions compromise either accuracy, robustness, or efficiency”. Pang et al. [35] treat non-smoothness as the thing to remove, curating every contact pair in Sec. III-D so that contact points and normals change smoothly. The hand has more actuators than the object has degrees of freedom and is still short of authority over it. The object moves only through contacts, and the contacts are what cannot be commanded. Bicchi [1] states in Sec. 1.4 that in multifingered grasps “the number of independent contact forces is much larger than the number of actuators. Thus, from a controllability standpoint, not all the contact forces are controllable”. Vision is occluded by the hand doing the work. Bai et al. [36] name it in Sec. 4.3, that “occlusion hampers object tracking”. DexPoint [37] keeps vision and adds imagined hand points to the point cloud. Yin et al. [38] remove vision entirely and rotate objects from 16 binary touch sensors over the palm, links and fingertips. Gravity direction changes the task. Visual Dexterity [16] states that reorientation with the hand below the object “is much easier”, because “with a downward-facing hand, the hand must manipulate the object while simultaneously counteracting gravity”. AnyRotate [39] makes the direction a randomised variable by “randomly initializing hand orientations between episodes”, and its Sec. 5.3 measures the cost, with performance dropping progressively from palm up and palm down, through base up and base down, to thumb up and thumb down. 4 PREPRINT, OCTOBER 2026 

Three counts describe two hands and they measure different things. Fifty-three of the 113 papers record two hands on the robot, which is all the corpus’s two-hand flag claims. Forty-three carry the task label for bimanual coordination, the narrower claim that coordinating the hands is the task. Section VI narrows again, to the 28 papers whose notes place a learned closed-loop controller on two multi-fingered hands, and its opening paragraph names every exclusion that takes the 53 down to the 28. That 28 is the denominator for every architecture count in this survey. Every bimanual claim here names which of the three it uses. Four things genuinely change when the second hand arrives. Contact stays non-smooth, occlusion stays, and gravity stays the same problem. Role asymmetry is one. AsymDex [40] assigns a dominant hand with full finger and wrist control and a facilitating hand with 6-DoF base pose only, so that “the facilitating hand repositions and reorients one object, while the dominant hand performs complex manipulations”. Two hands on one object also close a kinematic chain through the object. In [1] Sec. 1.4 the hand and object dynamics are separate and are “linked through the n rigid-body contact constraints”. A second hand adds a second constraint set on the same object rather than a second independent problem. That is where physical plausibility fails first. BimanGrasp [41] is the one paper here that measures it, rejecting a synthesised grasp when “total penetrations exceeded 1.5 mm” and reporting in Sec. IV-C that “penetration remains the primary cause of grasp failure”. Two-arm collision does not exist for one hand at all. DyDexHandover [42] resets an episode when “any unintended arm contact with the environment or self-collisions” occurs. Bunny-VisionPro [43] pays for it in the controller, where adding a sphere-approximated self-collision cost raises motion-control time from 0.74 ms to 7.85 ms. Bidex [44] solves it in hardware, mounting the hands so the human arm and the teacher arm “are perpendicular to each other and do not collide”. DexMimicGen [45] states plainly that it does not handle inter-arm collision at all. The action space doubles as well. Bi-DexHands [46] runs 20 tasks on two Shadow Hands and finds single-agent PPO beating multi-agent RL on most of them, offering in Sec. 5.2 that “PPO algorithm is able to use all observations for training the policy, while MARL can only use partial observations”. AsymDex [40] attacks the dimensionality from the other side, halving the observation and action dimension through its role split and a frame relative to the facilitating hand’s object. Two cells say the field has no agreed criterion, and both are in families where a second body is involved. A criterion naming a distance, a duration and a trial count can be re-run by someone else. A rubric cannot, and Section VII takes up what follows from that. 

C. Terms used throughout 

Six terms recur in every section that follows, and each is used here in one sense only. 

Method paper. A paper whose own experiments train or evaluate a dexterous policy. It is the unit every count is taken over: 113 of the corpus’s 219 entries are method papers, the rest being hands, simulators, datasets, benchmarks, surveys, tactile sensors and evaluation protocols. 

Privileged state. Information a simulator can supply and a robot cannot: object pose and velocity, contact forces, mass, friction. A policy trained on it cannot be deployed as it stands, which is what makes the next term necessary. 

Teacher–student distillation. Training one policy on priv-ileged state, then fitting a second that sees only deployable observations, usually camera images and joint encoders, to reproduce the first. Section V finds 23 of the 113 methods do this, and it is the second half of the recipe Section I describes. 

Reference and rollout. A reference is a pose or a trajectory scored before anything executes: a retargeted human motion, a synthesized grasp, a planned path. A rollout is what a trained policy actually did. The distinction matters because a quantity can be measured carefully at the reference and never at the rollout, which Section VII finds is the case for interpenetration. 

Interpenetration. The distance by which two bodies that should be touching instead overlap, a consequence of solving contact constraints approximately. Section IV covers what each engine exposes; the depth is a property of the compliance someone chose, not of the engine. 

Success criterion and per-cell trial count. The criterion is the predicate a paper uses to call an episode a success: a pose within a tolerance, an object never dropped, a task completed. The per-cell count is how many trials one reported rate is computed from. Section VII finds 98 of them state a criterion and no two state the same one, and that 70 state a count. 

D. What has changed since 2022 

The corpus roughly tripled between 2022 and 2024, and its composition changed while it grew. Three shifts are visible in the record, and none of them is small. Sorting each method by whether it trains in simulation, through reinforcement learning or distillation, or from human demonstration, through behavior cloning, a vision-language-action model, a diffusion or flow policy, or a collection rig, the simulator branch led 13 papers to 4 in 2023 and trailed 15 to 18 in 2024. It has trailed since: demonstration-trained work is 22 of the 34 papers from 2025 and 8 of the 13 so far from 2026. The recipe Section I describes is this field’s mature pipeline and not its growth area, and a reader who knows only that recipe is reading the field as it was in 2023. It was 8 of the 11 papers in 2022 and 3 of the 34 in 2025. The absolute count falls while the corpus triples, which is not a shift in emphasis but a change in what the field treats as worth publishing. Bimanual coordination moved the other way over the same span, from one paper to 16, and functional and tool use from 2 to 15. The cube-rotation benchmark that Section V shows the reward literature organized itself around is the task the newer literature has left. No paper earlier than 2025 uses Isaac Lab or Isaac Sim at all. Three do in 2025 and four in the partial 2026, against nine and two respectively on Isaac Gym, so on small numbers the successors already carry more of the most recent year than the original does. A. Design axes 

A policy is only as dexterous as the hand it runs on, and the hands available divide sharply: a few a laboratory can buy, a few more it can build from published designs, and a growing number that exist only in an announcement. What can be said about them is less than it looks. No degree-of-freedom, force, weight or price figure in Table IV or Table V, both set in Appendix B, was measured by anyone outside the maker. Six entries are the exception, from peer-reviewed papers with stated protocols: ILDA, Pisa/IIT, ORCA, RUKA, LEAP and BiDexHand. Every other claim is dated at its source. Table V prints the date beside each announced hand, and the repository tabulation carries a claim-date column for the hands that can be bought, running from Shadow’s specification of 4 December 2024 to vendor pages fetched on 17 September 2026. The degree-of-freedom count is the first number a vendor states and the least comparable one, because three different quantities get printed under that heading: joints, actuated joints and actuators. Shadow’s own specification separates them, reading “20 actuated DOF and a further 4 under-actuated movements for a total of 24 joints” [51]; Inspire’s page gives a DoF figure and a joint count that differ by a factor of two [52]; a tendon hand can list more actuators than it has kinematic degrees of freedom [53]. C. Tactile sensing 

DIGIT set the cost floor, stating a manufacturing cost of about 15 USD per sensor in batches of a thousand [64], which is where the price of touch sensing stopped being the obstacle. Durability was as much of the contribution: its gel barely degraded over an abrasion test that destroyed the two gels compared against it. Digit 360 is the same lineage at a different operating point: millions of taxels, force resolution around a millinewton, and on-device processing that cuts event-to-action latency by most of an order of magnitude [65]. D. The catalog against the literature 

Behind Table V’s entries sit three kinds of evidence, and conflating them is how a DoF figure with no source ends up in a survey. Vendor prose carrying numbers is the strongest, as on 1X’s page of 9 July 2026 [76]. Video is second and carries none. Press or bibliography assertion is third, as with Tesla’s V3 hand, known here only through a paraphrase of patents because the USPTO PDF parsed empty [77]. Table V blanks the cells resting on the third class and footnotes who did the arithmetic. A survey can go one step past recording that a claim is unverified, which is to say which claims are implausible on their face. One vendor’s actuator count and mass work out to about six grams per actuator including structure, tendons, routing and skin [53]. Another’s mass figure appears to exclude a half-kilowatt pump [78]. A third’s degree-of-freedom figure sits on the same page as a finger count exactly half of it, so it is almost certainly the pair of hands [79]. A fourth, widely repeated for the Optimus hand, is one article’s arithmetic and not a vendor statement, and the previous generation’s own figure was half of it [77]. The engines ran a restricted common model, hinge joints with sphere and capsule geometry and no meshes; the authors call it “an open question whether this model system can be tuned to work better in the gaming engines”; and they wrote MuJoCo and say so. A grasp is hard for nameable reasons. It is many contacts at once, all persistent, all near stiction, on an object much lighter than the mechanism holding it. Persistence and multiplicity make the problem hyperstatic, and Le Lidec et al. show that per-contact solvers of the projected Gauss-Seidel family, and RaiSim’s, then inject spurious jamming forces at stiction that vanish only once the object slides. The mass ratio makes it ill-conditioned, and in their stacked-cube test at a 10 3 to 10 −3 kg ratio those same methods fail to converge. Global methods with proximal regularisation stay robust in both cases [34, Sec. IV-A]. What degrades a truncated solve is therefore conditioning and redundancy, not stiffness, and a grasp supplies both by construction. Locomotion is forgiving by comparison. On flat ground the contact model and solver choice “hardly affects” their quadruped’s tracked base velocity, and only on rough, slippery terrain do RaiSim and CCP deviate. A walking robot makes and breaks a few contacts against ground far heavier than itself. A hand does neither. Speed enters by the same door. B. Contact models and solvers 

Table VI, set in Appendix B, is the engine-by-engine comparison, one line per simulator, built only from what a note confirmed. Le Lidec et al. supply the taxonomy that organises it, checking each formulation against the Signorini condition, Coulomb’s law, and the maximum dissipation principle. Linear complementarity, the family of Bullet, ODE, PhysX and DART, satisfies Signorini alone, because linearising the friction cone to a pyramid biases friction toward its corners. The cone complementarity problem satisfies the other two but relaxes Signorini, so contact acts at a distance of size ∆t·μ·∥ cT ∥. The full nonlinear problem satisfies all three and is non-convex, by Table II of [34]. A contact model is not the algorithm that solves it. PhysX linearises the cone, which places its model in the LCP family, and it does not solve an LCP. Its Temporal Gauss-Seidel scheme folds substepping into the Gauss-Seidel sweep and exposes position and velocity iteration counts separately [92, Sec. 3]. MuJoCo is inside that taxonomy. Le Lidec et al. classify it as CCP-MuJoCo, a convex relaxation solved by a Newton method on the primal QCQP, in the same family as CCP-Drake, the SAP-style scheme, by Table III of [34]. The relaxation makes two errors of opposite sign in one engine. A loaded contact carries a violation, and a sliding contact carries force at a positive gap. Drake’s SAP inherits the same pair, and its gliding effect at distance φ ≈ δt · μ · ∥ vt∥ “unfortunately does not go away as δt → 0” [93, Sec. V-A]. Le Lidec et al. call that compliance a “numerical trick designed to circumvent the issues due to hyper-staticity or ill-conditioning at the cost of impairing the simulation”. The depth a loaded contact carries is a chosen number, not a property of the engine. In a regularised formulation the steady-state violation at a contact is the normal load times the compliance: zero at zero load, growing with what the contact carries. MuJoCo drives that violation with a critically damped stabiliser whose steady-state depth, for an object resting under gravity, has a closed form independent of the object’s mass [94, Sec. V]. Whoever sets the stiffness sets the depth. Published penetration figures do not compare with each other, and the reason matters before anyone quotes one. Dojo solves a hard-contact problem and stays above the floor on an Atlas drop test where MuJoCo overlaps by tens of millimetres [95, Sec. V-A]; Drake’s figures for the same quantity are orders of magnitude smaller, and they are analytical bounds for a point mass at rest rather than measured depths [93, Sec. V-B]; ComFree-Sim reports a depth directly, on small convex primitives dropped under their own weight [96, Sec. IV-A]. A point-mass bound, a humanoid drop transient and a dropped primitive differ in load, inertia and regime, so the distances between those figures measure nothing about each other. What the set does establish is the part that matters for a hand: in a compliant formulation the depth follows from a stiffness somebody chose, and the GPU era moved that knob without removing it. The oldest engine comparison here puts the consequence plainly, reporting that engines pushed past their stable step “effectively simulate a different physics model which can no longer hold the object” [90, Sec. IV-D]. No paper in this corpus reports the depth for a fingertip loaded by a grasp, which is the one configuration a dexterous result depends on. Table VI’s most interesting column is the one that records whether an engine’s own paper reports a penetration depth, and it is nearly empty. Two engines report one, and both are engines whose paper is about contact accuracy. The word “penetration” appears nowhere in the Isaac Gym paper, and Isaac Lab’s contact sensor reports force, duration and an average contact point with no measure of contact quality at all. That is a documentation gap rather than a capability gap: NVIDIA’s own benchmark repository already computes an interpenetration depth per environment for its assembly tasks and scales the reward down as the depth approaches a millimetre. The quantity is a few lines of code away in the engine most of this literature trains in. The rest of Table VI is largely empty, and the emptiness is a result: 38 percent of the cells in its property columns hold a value no source stated, which is what its caption counts. No engine paper states a default physics timestep, and the three that report one report it for a named experiment, so the timestep column carries experiment settings and no engine’s default. Fewer than half the engines ship any dexterous hand. Two columns not printed here are worth the trip: the solver iteration count, which four engines state, and the license, which is the question a reader choosing an engine actually has and which almost none of them answers. 

C. GPU-parallel engines and their throughput 

Isaac Gym set the pattern. Physics, observations, rewards and actions stay on the GPU, and PhysX resolves contacts with the Temporal Gauss-Seidel sweep described above. Its per-task timesteps are published, which is rare: the Shadow Hand runs a 1/120 s physics step under a 1/60 s control step, or 1/20 s in the OpenAI variant. The result that reorganised the field is that reproducing OpenAI’s Shadow Hand cube reorientation took under an hour on one A100, against 30 hours on 6144 CPU cores and 8 V100s [92, Sec. 6.4.1]. Thirty-five of the papers examined run on it. Orbit and Isaac Lab moved the stack to PhysX 5, and the dexterous offering is thinner than the predecessor’s: the first-party suite is lifting, grasping and reorienting with the KUKA Allegro hand, while Shadow and Ability hands appear only through third-party grasp evaluation [97, Sec. 7.2.3]. MJX and MuJoCo Playground took the other route, putting MuJoCo’s own solver on the GPU through JAX at the cost described above. A. Sources of supervision 

A dexterous policy is defined by what supervises it, and four sources are in use across the corpus’s 113 papers: a reward func-tion supervises reinforcement learning, a human demonstration supervises imitation, a human reference trajectory supervises a physics-based tracker, which is imitation with a simulator in the loop, and nothing supervises a model-based planner, which is handed a cost and a model instead. The labels do not partition the corpus. Reinforcement learning is the largest at 54 of the 113 papers, behavior cloning next at 27, distillation at 23, and ten further tags cover the rest. They sum to far more than 113, because most methods published since 2024 sit on two branches at once: a reinforcement-learning teacher and a behavior-cloned student are one paper, not two. The repository carries the paper-by-paper version of the same thing, one line per method, and that is what to scan when looking for work comparable to your own. What the deployed policy looks like once training is done matters more than the name a paper gives itself, and on that axis the field has converged hard. Section V-E shows why. The emptiest columns of the method tabulation are the ones a reader most needs. So three of the 21 reorientation methods put a hand–object contact quantity in the reward, and none puts interpenetration in it: the one method that penalises interpenetration does so while building its references offline, not in the policy’s reward [113]. The physical quality of the contact is not something this literature optimises. Term counts range from one to ten. DemoStart [114] gives a single binary terminal reward of 1 and lets a curriculum over demonstration states do the shaping. AnyRotate [39] and ViserDex [115] each publish ten weighted terms. ViserDex is the cleanest specification of the 21, with every term, weight and equation in one appendix table, and a statement that the same weights are used for every object. Weights are frequently unrecoverable. Three of the 21 papers give no numeric weight that survives PDF conversion, because the equation blocks are images, which is a limit of this survey and is recorded as such. Robot Synesthesia [70] is a worse case. Its six coefficients are described only as “tuned hyper-parameters” and no number is printed anywhere in the paper, so the reward it reports cannot be reconstructed from the paper. DexReMoE [116] names an angular-velocity penalty weight in prose with no entry in its hyperparameter table, and DexTrack [117] names an affinity reward that is missing from its weight table. Curricula in this literature relax physics or tighten tolerances. DexPBT [110] tightens a success tolerance from 0.075 to 0.01 by a factor of 0.9 every 3000 environment steps once three successes are logged, and ManipTrans [17] starts at zero gravity and high friction and restores both while narrowing a fingertip threshold from 6 cm to 4 cm. DexMachina [18] attaches virtual spring-damper controllers to the object, lets them do the task at first, then decays their gains to zero. The two that ablate the curriculum report it is load-bearing: RotateIt [71] sets its rotation-penalty weight to zero and ramps it to 

−0.1, because applying it from the start makes the policy learn only to hold the object still, and ViserDex [115], whose performance-based curriculum runs over regularisation weight, action latency and time between successes, fails completely without the regularisation component. Population-based training appears once. DexPBT [110] trains populations of up to 32 agents, mutating the middle third of each population and replacing the bottom with mutated copies of the top, and reports 0.32 trillion environment steps for its largest run. The technique is routine outside robotics and nothing else in this corpus uses it. A language model can write the reward outright, which removes the hand-tuning and replaces it with a search whose objective nobody has read. Eureka [15] has GPT-4 write the reward function directly, constrained to return a total and a dictionary of named components, and feeds per-component statistics back between iterations. Its appendix gives an example whose reward correlates at −0.26 with the human-written one and still scores 1.45 on the human-normalized metric, the strongest argument in the corpus that hand-tuned weights are not a ceiling. DrEureka [118] moves the safety requirement into the prompt instead of a penalty term, asking in words for a cube rotating at about 0.25 radians per second with fingers penalized for leaving their initial pose. Neither can be checked against a file. Eureka’s generated rewards are written at run time to a gitignored directory and never committed, so the reward behind any reported number cannot be recovered from the repository, though its README documents the paper’s budget of five iterations and sixteen samples as the default and marks the shipped one-iteration block a fast-test preset. DrEureka’s repository contains only the locomotion and globe-walking trees, so its LEAP-hand reward has no code at all. Reinforcement learning stalls on objectives that are not learnable as stated. AnyRotate [39] found the angular-velocity objective unlearnable in the multi-axis setting and replaced it with a moving keypoint target. RotateIt [71] reports that its multi-axis policy “does not converge when training with only reinforcement learning” and adds an imitation loss against single-axis oracles. Eureka [15] cannot spin a pen from scratch and needs a pretrain-then-finetune split, with the from-scratch ablation failing to complete one cycle. Section V-C’s contact-graph term exists because of the general shape of the problem: contact moves the object off the reference, so return goes down, so the policy learns not to touch the object. It stalls on geometry too. Hora [75] fails on objects under 4 cm across because the fingers collide with each other. What it does not stall on is publication: the real-trial counts behind these headlines are small enough that Section VII-A treats them as the reporting problem they are. 

C. Learning from human data 

Thirty papers in this corpus describe a system for getting human motion onto a robot hand; the repository lists them with the retargeting objective compressed to one clause each. Retargeting splits four ways. The dominant family matches task-space vectors between the human and the robot hand, weighting fingertip distances and adding a minimum separation between the primary fingers, solved online; DexPilot [119] set that pattern and four later systems use the same formulation [120], [121], [43], [122]. The second copies joint angles wherever the kinematics allow it and solves only the thumb by inverse kinematics [123]. The third learns the map offline, which buys about an order of magnitude in rate over online optimisation and raises configuration-space coverage from 38 to 90 percent [124]. The fourth removes retargeting from the loop altogether, building an exoskeleton whose fingertip workspace is designed to match the target hand and regressing encoder values straight to motor values [69]. 14 PREPRINT, OCTOBER 2026 

What that tabulation does not carry is the point. Fewer than half of the teleoperation papers state a latency at all, and only a handful state a rig cost. Where costs are stated they are low and falling, from a few hundred dollars for a headset or a glove [123], [125] to a few thousand for a full mocap backpack [126], [44]. Latency is not one quantity either: some figures are end to end [119], [123], others are a single retargeting stage [124], [120], and several cells hold a control rate instead, so the column’s spread is not a range. Fewer than half report how many trajectories they collected, and fewer still report hours of data. Where throughput is measured, human collection beats teleoperation by a wide margin. Qiu et al. [127] time a grasp demonstration at 3.79 seconds for a bare human and 19.72 seconds through a teleoperated humanoid, and a pour at 4.81 against 37.31 seconds. It attributes the gap to retargeting latency and to the workspace of a 7-DoF arm, not to the hand. Four architectures cover the corpus. Action chunking came from [128], which predicts a chunk of joint targets with a CVAE and combines overlapping chunks by exponentially weighted ensembling, reaching 80 to 90 percent on fine bimanual tasks from about 50 demonstrations each. Diffusion came from Diffusion Policy [129], which denoises an action sequence and reports a 46.9 percent average success improvement over LSTM-GMM, IBC and BET across 15 tasks. 3D Diffusion Policy [130] swaps the image encoder for a small point-cloud encoder and reports 74.4 percent against 59.8 percent across 72 simulated tasks, and 85.0 against 35.0 percent on four real tasks from 40 demonstrations each. Flow matching is the 2024-onward default for large models and carries eight papers. Discrete action tokens are the fourth, used by [24] and [131], and are the only one of the four that needs no continuous head at all. Data generation is a separate lever and is underrated. DexMimicGen [45] turns 60 human demonstrations into 21,000 simulated ones, and a real two-handed humanoid trained on the generated set succeeds where the same policy trained on the source demonstrations never does. Dex1B [132] iterates optimisation, a proposal model and a simulator filter into roughly a billion synthetic grasps, and beats the prior best by a wide margin on its own benchmark. Seven papers train a dexterous-hand policy from video with no teleoperation at any stage. DexMV [133] retargets 700 self-recorded demonstrations by matching palm-to-fingertip task-space vectors and estimating actions by inverse dynamics, VideoDex [134] mines 965 trajectories from EpicKitchens to pretrain a neural dynamic policy, and DexVIP [135] goes further into the reward, clustering 715 curated HowTo100M frames into one consensus grasp pose per object category and adding it as a reward term. OKAMI [136], Lum et al. [137] and Guzey et al. [138] work from a single video each, and Goswami et al. [139] pretrain a world model on 829 hours of EgoDex. Three papers measure the gap directly. OKAMI [136] runs the same reference-plan and warping pipeline with and without human body and hand retargeting, and the version without it loses 75.0, 42.1, 82.0 and 74.0 points across four task and setting pairs. That is the largest clean embodiment-gap number in the corpus. Qiu et al. [127] ablate two mitigations separately on the same vertical-grasp task. Removing the interpolation that slows human trajectories down cuts success to a quarter of the full method’s, and removing the unified state space collapses it to zero, because the policy is handed a shortcut for telling the two embodiments apart. EgoZero [140] reports the sharpest version of the same effect: removing its 3D augmentation drops every one of its seven tasks to no successes at all, and substituting monocular depth for triangulated depth does the same, because the best metric depth models it tested carry more than 5 cm of error. Chen et al. [141] supply the counterexample. Their method retargets only the wrist and lets reinforcement learning discover finger motion, and their ablation that adds a fingertip-matching reward “does not yield benefits and even leads to lower performance”. Richer human correspondence is not uniformly better, and this is the one result in the corpus that says so with an ablation. Twelve papers sit in the human-reference tracking family. Eight track a human hand on an object and are covered here; the other four sit at the edges, Lum et al. [137] tracking only the object’s trajectory, DexMan [142] retargeting bimanual video onto a full humanoid, and HumanPlus [26] and OmniH2O [27] tracking whole-body motion. A reference is retargeted onto the robot and a policy trained to make the simulated body follow it: the reference supplies the shaping reward engineering would otherwise have to invent, and the simulator the physical consistency pure imitation lacks. The multiplicative reward that most human-reference meth-ods now use has a single source. PhysHOI [143] is the origin of the form. It multiplies a body term, an object term, an interaction-graph term and a contact-graph term, and reaches 95.4 percent success on GRAB against 27.0 percent for a DeepMimic baseline. The contact-graph term exists to stop the policy learning not to touch the object. Omnigrasp [11] replaces the action space with a pretrained latent motion prior and reports 94.6 percent grasp success and 84.8 percent trajectory success on GRAB, while stating outright that it omits penetration metrics. The next step was to stop treating demonstrations as fixed and let the method generate its own. DexTrack [117] adds an imitation loss to PPO and mines its own demonstrations through a homotopy search over easier neighbouring trajectories, beating the best baseline by about eight points at either threshold. It has a penetration-depth formula and applies it only to its input references, never to its own rollouts, and presents tolerance of “severe hand-object penetrations” as robustness. ManipTrans [17] freezes a generalist hand imitator and trains a per-task residual on top, with single-hand success about half again its bimanual figure. Its stance on contact is to raise the friction coefficient above the real value instead of modelling skin deformation, and its real deployment is open-loop replay. Two papers in this corpus ran the same curriculum and disagreed about whether it helps. DexMachina [18] moves to Genesis with 12,000 environments and six hand URDFs, and drives the object with virtual controllers that decay to zero. D. Generalist and vision-language-action policies 

The finding is the size of the hand. Eighteen papers carry the generalist tag. Fourteen of them settle whether the reported evaluation ran on a multi-fingered hand. Eight of those fourteen did, six report no hand result at all, and the remaining four never say, GR00T N1.6 [28] naming no end effector anywhere on its page. F. Paper against released code 

A paper that states its reward and also ships the code that trained it can be read twice, and the two readings sometimes disagree. In eight of the papers here they state different things. Table II sets each one out the same way: a repository, a file linked at the revision this survey read, the value the paper prints and the value that file contains. Most of the usual explanations for such a difference are ordinary ones. A repository is refactored after a paper is published, cleaned up for release, or carried forward into the next project, and the version a reader clones months later is not the version a result came from. A public default need not be the setting an experiment used: a configuration committed for others to run is often the general case, with the paper’s run specified elsewhere or not at all. A value can also be a typo in a table rather than a different objective, and one entry here is exactly that. So a row in this table is a note for a reader who intends to use that code, telling them which of two documents to believe about what it computes. It is not a statement about whether the paper’s reported results are right, and nothing here is a statement about intent or about work that released nothing to read. When an author has told us which document is authoritative, the answer has been the code. The first author of PianoMime [31] confirmed that the released implementation is the one behind that paper’s results and that its description of the reward is the imprecise side of the pair (personal communication, C. Qian, Oct 2026). That is the most useful thing a reply can settle, and it is the direction a reader using the code needs. The sharpest difference in the table is PhysHOI [143]: its paper assigns the object’s rotation error a nonzero weight on the GRAB dataset, and in the released reward function that error is set to zero with the computation that would produce it commented out on the same line, so no weight on it could move the reward. Its reported success criterion uses position alone, so a run of that file would not expose the difference either. Every line of Table II can be confirmed or refuted in a browser, without this survey and without anyone’s agreement. The authors of every paper named in the table were written to on 19 September 2026, before this survey was posted, with the claim, its evidence and the sentences the survey would print, and were given until 10 October 2026 to answer. One reply changed a comparison and removed it from the count. A second, quoted above, settled one in the code’s favour. The rest are recorded as they came, and silence is recorded as silence rather than as agreement. A disputed comparison is corrected at the address in the author block, and Appendix C prints every comparison beside the note that revised it. The practical lesson for a reader of this literature is simple: read the reward function before the reward table, and treat a printed weight as a hypothesis about the code. A. Coupling, roles and collision 

When both hands hold the same object, the object closes a kinematic loop between them. Neither hand can move without changing what the other must do. BiDexGrasp [156] reports that the coupled objective “often yields imbalanced solutions, where one hand dominates stability while the other contributes marginally.” ArtiGrasp [158] reports simulation speed scaling “roughly quadratically with the number of contacts,” and trains each hand alone before pairing them. Role asymmetry is an assumption most of these systems make silently. BiDexHD [159] states it outright: “we assume the robot to be right-handed by default, i.e., the left hand handles the target object and the right hand handles the tool,” and Lin et al. [160] bake the same split into their reward, putting reference contact keypoints on the bottle base for the left fingertips and on the lid for the right. The bias is in the data first: TACO [161] recruited 14 right-handed subjects and measures the right hand moving consistently faster. The arms collide, and the corpus handles this by construction, not by control. RoboPianist [29] ships a forearm-forearm collision term that its own reward table never lists. Two papers name arm collision as unsolved. DexMimicGen [45] “does not explicitly handle collisions,” and DexMan [142] says its control parameterisation “overlooks full arm posture,” which it needs to avoid them. The action space doubles and the observation grows faster: a two-hand benchmark carries action vectors in the tens and observations in the hundreds of dimensions [46], and a recent generalist emits a chunk of arm joints, end-effector poses, hand joints and fingertips together [84]. What the larger vector does not carry is any statement of who does what. Fig. 10 sets the four mechanisms side by side. Every one of the 28 carries exactly one architecture, read from its note: one policy over both hands, a network per hand, leader and follower, or a note that does not say. The fourth panel is not a fourth architecture but the relative frame, and it is the same single paper as the third. Twenty-one of the 28 put one policy over both hands, which is three quarters of the set: the trackers and tracking-adjacent methods [18], [142], [17], [141] and [26]; the reinforcement-learning tasks [110], [160], [15], [31] and [107]; the demonstra-tion pipelines [44], [126], [162], [45], [50] and [127]; and the generalist policies [84], [146], [163], [131] and [164]. In every one of them a single network takes a concatenated two-hand observation and emits a two-hand action. Two papers do not say which they are, Bunny-VisionPro [43] and DexImit [149], whose notes describe the rig and the data pipeline but never the policy’s own decomposition. The concentration is not the outcome of a comparison that was won. Four of the 28 give each hand its own network, and the two papers that compare the choice disagree. Bi-DexHands [46], a benchmark entry and so outside the 28, ships the MARL baselines and finds PPO over the full observation beats HAPPO and MAPPO “in most cases,” with the gap narrowing on tasks that need both hands, because PPO “can use all observations” where MARL sees only part. BiDexHD [159] concludes the opposite. Its independent PPO teachers score 74.59 percent stage-two tracking rate on trained tasks against 53.88 for a centralised policy over both observations. Both are simulation only, on different tasks and hands, so neither settles it. ArtiGrasp [158] trains one PPO policy per hand, and Dynamic Handover [19] and DyDexHandover [42] use MAPPO with one agent per arm-hand system. One paper assigns explicit leader and follower roles, and it is the same one that expresses its policy in a relative frame. AsymDex [40] gives the dominant hand full finger and wrist control, restricts the facilitating hand to a base pose, and writes both the dominant hand and the object in a frame attached to the object the other hand holds, which halves the observation and the action. It is also the only paper here that ablates the two mechanisms separately, and neither works without the other: relative frames without asymmetry and asymmetry without relative frames both collapse to near zero where the full method succeeds most of the time. Its real rig pairs an Allegro with a cheaper hand because only one Allegro was available, so the roles are confounded with the hardware. Two other papers use a relative quantity without giving either hand a role, one appending the inter-hand pose to its observation [162] and one applying a shared transform to both arms while generating data [45], which is the mechanism offline and not in a policy. One architecture carries three quarters of the field, and the choice behind it has been compared twice, with opposite outcomes, and ablated once. A decisive comparison would run one handover task on three architectures with the same hand and the same seeds, reporting separate giver and receiver returns. 

C. Benchmarks, datasets and the shared axes 

Two purpose-built bimanual dexterous suites exist in the corpus, four years apart. The two bimanual benchmarks in the corpus trade coverage against measurement, in opposite directions. Bi-DexHands [46] is 20 tasks on two Shadow Hands in Isaac Gym, ordered by the infant age at which humans acquire the skill, at 2048 environments and a reported 30,000-plus FPS. Its measurement discipline is weaker than its coverage. It reports reward and normalized score, never a success rate. Its only success flag in code tests the object-to-goal distance against 3 cm, which ignores orientation and exists only in the four catching tasks. Any success rate later work attributes to Bi-DexHands comes from that flag or its own definition. Bench2Dex [88] is the more instrumented of the two, running 26 long-horizon tasks across 12 arm-and-hand embodiments with thousands of teleoperated demonstrations in eight modali-ties, including a ray-cast tactile image that maps every hand geometry into one representation. Its headline result is the gap between conditions and not the leaderboard: the matched leader takes about half the rollouts and falls to a fifth under combined perturbation, which compresses the field until the ranking no longer holds. Tasks are not crossed with embodiments, so no contrast isolates the hand, and the authors state that their hand and tactile models are not calibrated against matching physical hardware. D. Handover, and what transfers from one hand 

Handover is the one bimanual task where the hands are unambiguously asymmetric, because one gives and one receives. All three corpus handover papers use a single shared reward across giver and receiver. None defines separate objectives for the two roles. Whether the two hands optimize the same objective is usually left implicit. Dynamic Handover [19] states one formula, r =

Lin et al. [107] stage their reward with a discrete variable switching which hand’s fingertips are scored, and still calls handover its hardest task at 52.5 percent real success. The training recipe transfers intact. PPO with thousands of parallel environments, an asymmetric critic on privileged state, domain randomisation and distillation into a vision policy is the same in [160] and [107] as in Section V-B. Success criteria compound, because ManipTrans [17] fails a trajectory “if either hand fails to meet these conditions.” And object-centric metrics cannot say which hand caused the failure. BiDexHD [159] scores the steps where both objects track their references, and DexMachina [18] averages ADD across object parts before an AUC. Three papers carry an inter-hand penetration term, and all three are static grasp synthesis: [41], [156] and [149]. VII. E VALUATION , AND A PROTOCOL FOR COMPARISON 

A. Reporting practice 

Most of this literature does test on hardware, which is the check that matters and the one a simulator cannot substitute for: 89 of the 113 papers report a real-robot experiment. What they report about those experiments is much less even. Fig. 13 draws six reporting axes, each against the denominator that belongs to it, and two of the six are the ones a reader most needs. Without a count of unseen test objects there is no denominator for a claim about generalisation; without some statement about contact there is no way to tell whether the hand passed through the object. Those two are the axes the field states least often. Every share in the figure is a lower bound, for reasons Appendix A gives. The plainest gap is the trial count. Nineteen of the papers that ran a real robot never say how many times they ran it, and a percentage with no denominator cannot be given an interval, so it cannot be compared with anything. Where a count is stated it is small, and it is not always the same quantity. Some papers report a per-cell count, a fixed number of trials on each task or each condition; others report a grand total over every cell; a few cannot be assigned to either basis. The two distributions are kept apart here and in Fig. 14, because pooling them produces a figure that describes nothing: Hora [75]’s 240 trials and Visual Dexterity [16]’s 20 are experiments of comparable size recorded on different bases. This is the single most consequential fact about evaluation in dexterous manipulation, and it is why Section VII-C proposes a protocol and not a league table. Reporting has improved a little and the experiments have not. About two thirds of papers from 2025 onward state a trial count, against three fifths before, but the counts themselves have not moved: the median has sat near twenty trials per cell every year since 2024, and earlier years rest on too few observations to read as a trend. A field can get better at disclosing a number without the number itself getting any better. The denominators also sit on different hardware. The papers that name their own hand describe it in 79 different ways, unnormalised, so that figure counts strings and not designs; Fig. 3 gives the distribution. A success rate on an Allegro and a success rate on an Inspire hand are not measurements of the same thing, because the two differ in actuated joints by nearly a factor of three. Nor are the criteria the same. DexVerse [89] counts a pick a success when the cube is “lifted at least 0.20 m above its resetting height”. Bench2Dex [88] requires its terminal predicate to hold for a continuous dwell time, to reject transient contacts. The Colosseum [169] counts an episode successful “if the model completes the task fully”. DexTrack [117] reports every success rate as a pair under two threshold sets, which can move the same rollouts by nearly twenty points. Among the papers that state no criterion at all, most have no success predicate to state: they report radians rotated or time-to-fall and never define a success, which is a fact about the paper rather than a gap in this survey. Where a criterion is stated it is usually a rubric, a staged partial credit or a deferral to a benchmark’s own definition rather than a threshold, and a rubric is reproducible inside a lab and not across two. Seven quantities dissociate in the published data, which is why they have to be reported separately. Task success is the first, and binary success discards the difference between a near-miss and inaction. Snyder et al. [170] put it plainly: “a policy that completes 90% of the task is clearly better than a policy that is frozen the whole time, yet their success rates would be identically 0%”. In [171] one policy scores in the teens overall on a pancake task while picking up the spatula and flipping the pancake in every attempt. Robustness to perturbation has been measured, though not yet on a hand. The Colosseum [169] reports a success drop of a third to a half under single perturbation factors and more than three quarters under all of them together. In [88] the leader of the matched condition is not the leader under combined shift. The ranking at the anchor is not the ranking under shift. Generalisation to unseen objects is stated by 40 papers, and half of those test on ten objects or fewer. Physical plausibility of the contact is the least reported of the six, at 12 percent of the papers whose notes settle the question. Section VII-B takes it up. Sample and wall-clock cost is reported by about a third of the corpus as an environment count and by fewer as an episode count. RoboPianist [29] is the exception that shows what disclosure looks like, giving samples per song and hours per run on named hardware. Real-robot transfer is the one that undoes the rest, because simulated rank order is not real rank order. AutoEval [172] scores one policy at 6 of 50 in simulation and 47 of 50 on the real arm for the same task. Badithela et al. [173] state the limit directly: “the simulation-to-real gap precludes rigorous statistical inferences about real-world outcomes from simulation results alone”. Reproducibility is the last, and releasing code is not the same as releasing something that settles a question. Eight papers state a training objective in their own repository that differs from the one in the paper, which Section V-F and Table II set out file by file. That is the figure this section uses. 

B. Physical plausibility 

One quantity is specific to hands and is almost never reported: how far the hand passes into the object it is holding. Outside closed-loop control it has been a standard comparative column for years. Grasp synthesisers fail a grasp that penetrates past a stated depth [41], retargeting methods report a maximum depth against a baseline [144], and hand–object datasets score a split on depth, intersection volume and simulation displacement [174]. Among learned closed-loop policies almost nobody reports it, and nobody reports it measured in the physics the policy ran in. D-Grasp [175] is the instructive case, because it does report a volume for a pose its own policy reached, and the way it does so shows where the quantity goes. Inside the simulator the paper reports zero penetration, since the meshes there are decimated for speed; the figure it prints instead comes from re-measuring a single frame against the original hand mesh and the full-resolution object mesh, with, in the paper’s words, “no physical simulation involved”. The number a reader wants, how deep the hand sits in the object in the physics that trained the policy, is zero by construction, and the number reported is a geometric re-measurement on geometry the simulator never used. The same paper supplies the reason to want the measurement. Its appendix observes that a deeply penetrating reference grasp can raise a success rate rather than lower it, because “the objects can become entangled within the hand mesh and will therefore not be able to fall down”, and concludes that “the success rate metric should always be interpreted in combination with the other metrics”. A success rate with no plausibility number beside it can be read two ways. There is a reason the gap persists, and it is not difficulty. A quantity a policy optimises cannot also judge it, because 22 PREPRINT, OCTOBER 2026 

the policy learns the measure rather than the property the measure stands for. PhysHOI [143] shows the failure in the clean direction: its imitation reward is maximised by not touching the object at all, because contact perturbs the reference trajectory. DexTrack [117] shows it from the other side, defining a maximum penetration depth, applying it only to its input references, and then reporting tolerance of “severe hand-object penetrations” as a result. The remedy is to measure with something the policy never optimised. Definitions are not shared either, which is the other half of why published figures do not compare. GRAB [176] reports an average penetration in millimetres without saying whether it is a maximum, a mean or a median; OakInk [174] supplies the fullest published vocabulary, separating penetration depth, solid intersection volume and simulation displacement. A number without its definition is not a number a second paper can be held to. What would close the gap is one line in a results table: the maximum and mean penetration depth over the evaluation rollouts, on a dense surface sample, computed by code that never entered the reward or the termination rule. That is the plausibility row of the protocol in Table III. What this survey supplies there is a measurement and a count; it does not supply a threshold, and Section VII-D says where the one in the table is borrowed from and why it does not bind. 

C. Statistical practice, and a proposed protocol 

Kress-Gazit et al. [171] give the field’s reference protocol, and it prescribes process rather than numbers: write the success criteria before the run, have someone other than their author score the runs, match initial conditions with image overlays, interleave the policies blind within one session, report counts and not percentages, and use a posterior over the success probability instead of a point estimate. All of that is right and none of it fixes the sample size. It prescribes no minimum trial count and no interval width, and its own worked example shows why one is needed: at twenty evaluations per policy, an eighteen-point difference in success rate still leaves better than a one-in-ten chance that the worse policy is the better one. Multiply every count in that example by ten and the same two rates separate. The TRI LBM Team [177] supplies the missing numbers by fiat rather than derivation: 50 rollouts per task per policy per condition on hardware, 200 in simulation, blind, with randomised policy order inside per-initial-condition bundles. It replaces confidence intervals with Beta-posterior violins and corrects its pairwise tests under Bonferroni, “unless otherwise noted”. Its own warning is the strongest sentence in this literature: “there is significant risk that many robotics papers are measuring statistical noise due to insufficient statistical power”. The rest fall short of their own advice. RoboArena [178] runs hundreds of double-blind pairwise comparisons across seven institutions and thousands of rollouts, and reports neither confidence intervals nor per-policy trial counts. The Colosseum [169] evaluates hundreds of test sets with “one training seed and one evaluation seed” per baseline and gives no intervals in simulation. AutoEval [172] calls ±10 percent “the natural variance of robot evaluations” without giving the formula behind the intervals it plots. Two papers do give usable numbers. Badithela et al. [173] pair real and simulated trials and de-bias the simulation with a rectifier, which saves up to a quarter of the hardware trials when the paired correlation is high and nothing at all when it is near zero; their decision rule is exact, that combining helps only when the rectifier variance is below the variance of the real evaluations. Snyder et al. [170] give the larger saving by scoring progress rather than success: a sequential test on graded scores cuts hardware trials by about 45 percent, and a gap that needs 80 binary trials to establish is established in 18 when the score is continuous. That is the strongest argument in this literature for grading partial progress. Every count below is derived for the statistic its axis actually reports, and not by one convention applied to all of them: a single rate takes a Wilson half-width, a matched comparison takes McNemar, a ratio takes the standard error of the log ratio, a correlation takes the Fisher-z interval. Appendix E derives the counts in Table III, and they come to this. Reporting a single rate takes about 100 real rollouts per cell, which is where the worst-case half-width of (1) falls to ten points. Comparing two policies is a harder question: the design is paired, so the count follows McNemar and depends on how often the two policies disagree and not on the two rates alone, and separating a twenty-point difference at conventional power takes 57 matched pairs per arm, fewer rollouts than two independent arms would need. Simulated cells are cheap enough to run in the hundreds. The other axes follow the same logic. Perturbation axes are screened cheaply, which ranks them but cannot establish that any one of them hurt, and certified at the cost of a single rate. Unseen-object claims resample the object rather than the trial, so the count that matters is the number of objects and not the number of rollouts. The transfer axis needs real and simulated trials paired, and separating two correlations takes several hundred pairs, which is why it belongs in a benchmark and not in a paper. Every count there is a cap and not a bill: a sequential test on a graded score can decide in a fraction of the trials [170], and a cell that stops early reports a confidence sequence and not a fixed-sample interval, never both. 

D. Feasibility and limitations 

The proposed protocol has not been run. In the twelve-method comparison assembled for this survey, no method supplied all the measurements needed for a complete protocol row. DeXtreme [14], for example, reports a success criterion and a confidence interval, but uses ten trials. This limits retrospective comparison; it does not imply that the reported experiments failed their own stated objectives. The protocol is expensive. For each policy and task it calls for 57 matched anchor trials and 100 unseen-object trials. Comparing two policies on three tasks is then 342 real rollouts on the matched set and 600 on the unseen-object set, 942 in all, or about 16 hours at one minute per rollout before repairs and scoring. What is unprecedented is paying that cost for a single dexterous-hand paper, where the modal cell is ten trials. Four things would have to change. Reviewers would have to reward 57 matched trials on one task over 20 unmatched trials on five, and nothing in the corpus suggests that is happening. The loop would have to be automated, and AutoEval [172] shows it is buildable for a gripper at one to three hours of setup, while stating that it supports binary success only and no robustness axes. Somebody would have to run the penetration measure on their own rollouts, which is a choice rather than a capability: Section IV-B shows the depth is computable from the poses and the meshes in a few lines of Warp, and IsaacGymEnvs already ships a task that does it every step, so the engine is not the barrier. The comparison would also have to be sequential, because the savings in [170] are the only reason 100 is a cap and not a cost. The proposal has four limits. It has not been run, and its sample sizes come from power and interval calculations and not from dexterous-hand experiments. The worst-case calculation assumes a success rate of 0.5, while the paired design assumes a discordance rate of 0.3 that the literature does not report. Its perturbations come from parallel-jaw benchmarks [169], [179], where contact is simpler. Finally, the 2 mm penetration threshold follows [144]; it is a simulator convention, not a physical limit. Captured human grasps in GRAB [176] average 3.25 mm under the same kind of measurement. Establishing a meaningful threshold will require measurements across hands, engines, and accepted behaviors. Seven papers in this corpus are evaluation protocols, a small denominator, and not one of them uses a dexterous hand: Badithela et al. [173] run a parallel-jaw gripper and the other six state no hand. Everything proposed above is therefore assembled from work on grippers and on whole-arm tasks. A modest first test would run the protocol in Table III once, on one in-hand reorientation task with one 16-DoF hand, and release the rollouts and per-trial outcomes for independent analysis. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

This corpus describes a field that has converged. Most of it trains with reinforcement learning on privileged state in a GPU simulator and distills the result into a vision-only student; a second branch, growing faster, learns from teleoperated demonstration instead; and almost all of it runs on four hands. What the field cannot currently do is compare its own results, and most of the seven conclusions below follow from that. 1) Eight hands that can be bought today or built from published designs take zero papers between them, while the corpus’s experiments concentrate on four designs (Section III-D, Fig. 3). 2) Human data does not port across hands, and the map is usually unstated: 33 of the 53 method papers that use human data never say how the human motion reached the robot hand (Section V-C). 3) Twenty-one of the 28 papers that put a learned closed-loop controller on two multi-fingered hands run one policy over a concatenated two-hand observation, a choice compared twice with opposite outcomes and ablated once (Section VI-B, Fig. 10). 24 PREPRINT, OCTOBER 2026 

4) The generalist and vision-language-action policies that do evaluate on a multi-fingered hand run it at a median of 6 actuated degrees of freedom, against 16 across the reinforcement-learning papers that state a count, and the papers reaching the larger band on paper never say whether the hand was in the evaluation (Section V-D). 5) The evaluation-methodology literature this survey builds its protocol on contains no dexterous hand at all: of its seven papers in this corpus, one runs a parallel-jaw gripper and the other six state no hand (Section VII-D, Table III). 6) Eight of the 63 papers that released parseable code state, in a named file at a named commit, something other than the value their paper prints. Table II gives each of the eight as a repository, a commit, a file and two values, and all of their authors were contacted before this was posted (Section V-F). 7) Penetration is almost never measured where it would matter. No paper here reports how far its hand sank into the object, measured in the physics its own policy ran in. The one that does report a volume for a pose its own policy reached measures it outside the simulator, on meshes the simulator never used, and reports zero inside it. On the reference-versus-rollout split that frames this survey, every paper that handles penetration at all is still measuring a reference (Section VII-B). Six concern gaps in the literature. The first concerns publishing practice; the corrections made during this review are recorded beside the result in Section V-F. Three of those claims carry a case. A reward table in a paper is a claim about a document, not about a run, and PhysHOI [143] is the instance to keep in mind: the term its table weights at 0.1 is set to zero in the file at the commit this survey fetched, and the success criterion the paper reports is itself position-only. Contact is what separates a hand from a gripper, and the obstacle to measuring it is not the engines. IsaacGymEnvs ships a task that computes a per-environment maximum interpenetration depth against meshes and gates the policy update on a 1 mm threshold, and Tactile Genesis [180] offers penetration depth on Genesis geometry as a sensor. The tooling sits in the field’s own benchmark repository and the number is still not reported. DexTrack [117] has the formula and points it at its inputs. And hardware and software have come apart on a narrower claim than the hand count first suggests, because 11 of the 19 hands that take no paper are neither sold nor open and take none for that reason, which is why the fifth claim is eight hands and not nineteen. What this survey cannot establish is which method is better than which. It re-runs nothing, and Section VII argues that the published numbers do not compare. Six works are cited by metadata only, and a seventh, Ma and Dollar 2011, is on disk but unread; no claim rests on any of them. Every coverage figure here is a lower bound, for the reason Appendix A sets out. Several practical changes require little additional computa-tion. Authors can generate reward tables from the configurations used for the reported run, cite the exact revision, state the trial count and success criterion, and release per-trial outcomes. Evaluations can report penetration depth over policy rollouts as a diagnostic, without optimizing the policy against that measure. TopoRetarget [144] shows how informative the result can be. Hardware choices also deserve a broader base: the Allegro, Shadow, Inspire, and LEAP hands account for most of the published use in this corpus, while every other listed hand appears in eight method papers or fewer. Better reporting would make results easier to reproduce; broader hardware coverage would show which results survive a change of embodiment. APPENDIX AMETHOD 

No claim about a paper here is made from memory. Each claim traces to a note taken from that paper, or from the code released with it, and each note to the version of the source it was read from. What follows is how the papers were selected, read and coded, and what the method cannot do. 

Selection. Six topic-specific bibliographies were assembled in parallel (simulators and physics, hands and vendors, re-inforcement learning, imitation and human data, bimanual, benchmarks and evaluation), seeded with the canonical works in each area and extended by search up to 2026-09-17. Every arXiv identifier was checked by fetching the abstract page and matching the title, and entries that could not be checked that way are marked. The six lists were merged with deduplication on arXiv identifier and normalized title, giving 223 entries. One of those 222 was added after the search. 

dgrasp_2022 entered the corpus on 2026-10-06, three weeks after the cutoff, and it turned out to be the clearest instance of the finding in Section VII-B. It was not hard to find: a corpus paper’s own note cites it as that paper’s baseline, and the search still did not surface it. A search that missed the work most relevant to one of this survey’s two findings has probably missed others, and that is the plainest statement available of what this corpus is worth as a sample. Seven further bibliography entries are not part of that corpus and are not counted anywhere in this survey. They are the prior audits and case studies Section I positions this survey against, they sit in a bibliography of their own as prior work from outside the corpus, and none of them carries a structured row. No source for them was parsed either, so each is quoted only from its abstract and its stated method. 

Acquisition. PDFs were obtained from arXiv or, for work without a preprint, from the publisher or vendor page cited. Vendor pages for hands with no paper behind them were kept as dated copies, so that a specification quoted here is quoted from the page as it read on a stated date rather than as it reads now. Where a paper released code, that repository was read beside it at a recorded revision: the README, the task and reward configuration files, and the bodies of the reward and observation functions. 

Conversion. PDFs were converted to text, falling back to raw text extraction where the layout parse returned too little, and the source, its page count and the converter are recorded per paper. Two consequences matter for reading this survey. Equations rendered as images do not survive conversion, so where a reward weight exists only inside a figure it is recorded ABUELSAMEN: LEARNING DEXTEROUS MANIPULATION 25 

as unreadable rather than guessed. Tables are flattened, so a number taken from a table is quoted with the table it came from and, where the flattening is ambiguous, the ambiguity is stated. 

Reading and coding. Each entry was read into a structured note under a fixed template: embodiment, learning method, objective, contact handling, evaluation, reproducibility, stated limitations, and quotable claims. Notes were written only from the converted sources. Where the source is silent the note says “not stated”, and nothing was inferred from the reviewer’s prior knowledge of the work. For method papers the reward or loss was quoted from the paper and, separately, from the released code, so that disagreements between the two are visible. Those disagreements turned out to be common enough to become a finding in their own right. The reading into notes was done with language-model assistance, from the parsed files only. The template’s conventions are the safeguard against what that can get wrong: a quotation rather than a paraphrase, “not stated” rather than an inference, and a named source beside every value, so a note can be checked against the file it came from. Four fields carrying a headline claim were re-read against their sources afterwards, the trial counts, the success criteria, the unseen-object counts and the penetration field, and all four audits are below; the coverage statistics are reported as floors for the same reason.

What this method cannot do. Mention counts over the corpus are counts of mentions, not of use: a related-work sentence counts the same as an experiment. Vendor specifications are manufacturer claims and are labeled as such throughout, and where a page has since gone offline the note says so. The corpus is large but not exhaustive, and selection by search favours work that is indexed, in English, and posted as a preprint. It also favours recent work. Of the 223 bibliography entries, 142 are dated 2024 or later and 17 predate 2018, so this is a corpus of the learned era and any claim here about a trend over time is a claim about 2022 onward. Every coverage statistic in this survey measures what this survey’s extraction captured, not what the literature reported. A structured row holds a scalar. A paper that reports a per-task count, a rubric, or a total spread across several tables produces a null, and a null is then counted as silence. The bias runs one way. Every miss converts a reporting paper into a silent one, so the field is made to look worse at reporting than it is. The size of the effect was measured on the statistic the survey leads with. For unseen objects the resampling unit is the object and not the trial, so 20 objects at 5 trials each gives 100 trials and an object-level half-width near 20 points. That 20 points is the Wilson width at n = 20 and it treats each object’s outcome as a single Bernoulli draw, which the five within-object trials are not. It is the right order of magnitude and the assumption belongs in the cell. A 10-point claim about an object distribution needs about 93 objects. Seven of the 39 rows that state an unseen count reach that: 225 in [41], 241 in [150] and [151], 360 in [10], 500 in [133], 2029 in [210] and 503409 in [47].
