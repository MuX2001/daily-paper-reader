Abstract— Collecting high-quality demonstrations remains a
central bottleneck for learning dexterous manipulation. Tele-
operation records executable robot actions, but it requires a
complete robot setup and places the burden of coordinating
many finger joints on the operator. Human demonstrations
are easier to collect, but their motions must be estimated and
retargeted to the robot, and they do not directly provide robot
actions. In this paper, we introduce DEXPORT, a portable
data-collection interface for high-DoF robot hands. It uses the
same hand hardware for collection and deployment, avoiding
a hand embodiment gap, and records both commanded joint
targets and measured joint states. Our key observation is that
a small set of skills can support a wide range of dexterous
tasks. During collection, the operator moves the hand-held
interface to specify the robot’s wrist motion and selects or
adjusts skills that coordinate the finger motion. This removes
the need to control each joint independently while retaining
behaviors such as precise grasping and in-hand manipulation.
We collect over 2,500 demonstrations across several real-
world tasks in laboratory and in-the-wild settings without a
robot arm. We then use behavior cloning to train visuomotor
policies directly on the recorded wrist motions and hand
commands, without skill labels. We evaluate on four tasks:
pick-and-place with diverse objects, screwing with in-hand tool
use, drawer stowing that requires simultaneous grasping and
pulling, and bimanual barcode scanning. Using recorded robot
commands instead of measured joint states improves average
final-task success from 11% to 64% across the three tasks
evaluated by success rate. Co-training with in-the-wild data
improves average pick-and-place success from 36% to 78%
across the three evaluation conditions. Videos are available at
https://amberxie88.github.io/dexport/.
I. INTRODUCTION
Multi-fingered hands allow robots to use different grasps,
manipulate tools, and reorient objects in hand. Learning these
†Amazon FAR team co-lead.
behaviors from demonstrations requires data that capture
detailed finger motion and contact-rich interactions. Tele-
operation records executable robot actions, but it requires
a complete robot setup. Collecting in-hand manipulation
demonstrations through teleoperation is particularly chal-
lenging because the operator must coordinate many finger
joints [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. On the other hand, human hand
demonstrations are easier to collect, but their motions must
be estimated and retargeted to the robot, and they do not
directly provide robot actions [6], [7], [8]. We therefore
need a method that scales beyond a fixed robot setup while
preserving executable hand actions.
To address this issue for parallel-jaw grippers, the Uni-
versal Manipulation Interface (UMI) [9] uses a portable,
hand-held interface to collect demonstrations naturally and
at scale. However, extending this paradigm to multi-fingered
hands has not been trivial. Early efforts, including Dex-
UMI [10] and DEXOP [11], can collect dexterous and
contact-rich behaviors using wearable hand exoskeletons.
However, designing a high-DoF exoskeleton involves a diffi-
cult trade-off between dexterity and wearability: supporting
more independent finger motions increases both mechanical
complexity and the operator’s control burden. In addition,
these systems record exoskeleton configurations rather than
the commands and resulting states of the deployed robot
hand. RealDexUMI [12] preserves this information by col-
lecting executable commands with the physical robot hand,
but demonstrates the approach on a hand with only six active
DoFs. It remains unclear how to preserve these benefits for
a hand with many independently controlled joints without
making the interface cumbersome.

interface should use the same end effector as the deployed
robot (Same Emb.), so that hand motions need no retargeting
and no embodiment gap is introduced. Second, it should
record the commands sent to the hand (Action Data) rather
than relying only on actions inferred from future measured
states. This distinction matters during contact, when the
commanded target and measured joint state may differ. Third,
it should expose enough controllable degrees of freedom
(DoF) to support tasks that require multi-fingered dexterity;
otherwise, a simpler gripper would be sufficient. Reusing
the deployment hand satisfies the first two requirements, but
creating an intuitive and comfortable interface for high-DoF
control is challenging, especially when the operator must
control each joint directly.
In this paper, we introduce DEXPORT, a portable data-
collection interface built around these requirements. Our
key design principle is to collect demonstrations with the
same hand hardware used at deployment while controlling
it through a small set of skills. Because the collection hand
shares the deployment hand’s hardware and control interface,
each demonstration records native commanded joint targets
and resulting states. The operator moves the device to specify
the robot’s wrist motion and selects or adjusts skills to
control the hand. Parameterized skills produce power, pinch,
and two-finger grasps, while a learned closed-loop skill
generates the finger gaiting needed for in-hand rotation. This
skill-level interface leaves task decisions to the operator
while the robot hand produces the coordinated finger motion.
We evaluate DEXPORT on four tasks that require different
forms of dexterity: selecting grasps for objects of different
shapes, opening a drawer while holding an object in the
same hand, rotating a tool in hand, and coordinating two
hands to scan a barcode. We collect demonstrations both
in the lab and in unstructured environments, without a robot
arm or external tracking infrastructure. We then use behavior
cloning to train visuomotor policies directly on the recorded
wrist motions and hand commands, without skill labels. Our
policies complete all four tasks and outperform a state-as-
action baseline trained without commanded hand actions on
each task. Across the three tasks scored by success rate,
final-task success averages 64% versus 11%. Co-training
with in-the-wild demonstrations further improves pick-and-
place in every condition, including unseen objects and scenes
with distractors. DEXPORT also collects demonstrations at
roughly 1.4× the rate of glove teleoperation.
To summarize, our contributions are as follows:
• A portable and ergonomic data-collection interface that
uses the same hand hardware and control space as the
deployed robot. It avoids a hand embodiment gap and
records native commanded actions and measured states.
• A skill-level control scheme that makes a high-DoF
hand practical to operate during portable data collection.
A compact set of parameterized and learned skills re-
places joint-by-joint control while preserving the ability
to perform dexterous tasks.
• An evaluation across four real-world tasks, including
data collection in unstructured environments and visuo-
motor policies trained directly on the recorded wrist
motions and hand commands, without skill labels.
II. RELATED WORK
Hand-Held Grippers for Robot Learning. Hand-held inter-
faces offer a middle ground between robot teleoperation and
unconstrained human video: they can be carried into diverse
environments while retaining an end-effector-centered obser-
vation and action space. Early systems used instrumented
reacher-grabbers or other portable tools and recovered their
motion using structure from motion, visual odometry, or
smartphone odometry [17], [18], [19]. UMI [9] makes
this approach more accurate and broadly useful through a
hand-held parallel-jaw gripper, a wide field-of-view camera,
visual-inertial pose tracking, and a policy interface that
accounts for deployment latency. Matching the collection
and deployment grippers further reduces the embodiment
gap. UMI demonstrates that portable collection can support
precise and long-horizon behaviors, but its gripper has only
one actuated degree of freedom. Extending these benefits to
a multi-fingered hand requires a practical way to control its
higher-dimensional action space.
Learning Dexterous Manipulation. Dexterous manipula-
tion skills are commonly learned from simulation, human
demonstrations, or robot teleoperation. Simulation can gen-
erate robot actions at scale and has enabled complex in-
hand behaviors, but transferring policies that rely on contact
dynamics and tactile observations to real hardware remains
difficult [20], [21], [22]. Human demonstrations collected
from videos, hand tracking, or motion-capture systems pro-
vide natural and diverse behaviors [23], [24], [3], [6], [7],
[8]. However, hand motion must be estimated and retargeted,
and the resulting data do not directly contain robot actions.
Teleoperation instead records executable actions by mapping
human-hand motion to a robot hand [1], [2], [3], [4], [5],
[25]. Immersive visual feedback and haptic devices can
improve control [26], [27], [28], but these systems generally
require a complete robot setup and ask the operator to
coordinate many finger joints. Lower-dimensional interfaces

Portable Data Collection for Dexterous Hands. Recent
systems enable dexterous data collection without a fixed
robot setup. DexUMI, DEXOP, and DexEXO use wear-
able exoskeletons or passive hand mechanisms to constrain
and measure the operator’s motion [10], [11], [14]. These
systems efficiently collect demonstrations of contact-rich
behaviors and reduce the human-to-robot kinematic gap.
However, these passive collection mechanisms record hand
configurations rather than both the commands and resulting
states of the actuated hand used at deployment. Related
systems improve kinematic alignment or tactile sensing
through mechanically isomorphic and modular exoskeleton
designs [30], [16]. Another line of work uses robot hands
that operators carry or wear, reducing the contact-geometry
gap and allowing robot-aligned data collection [31], [15].
RealDexUMI [12] follows the UMI principle of using the
same end effector for collection and deployment. It also
records robot commands, rather than inferring actions from
measured states, but demonstrates this approach on a hand
with six active DoFs. In contrast, DEXPORT targets a 20-
DoF hand, for which joint-by-joint operation would be
cumbersome. We use a compact set of skills to make the
hand practical to operate while still recording the raw hand
commands used to train the policy.

A. Hardware Design
Transferable Hand-Camera Module. Our portable data-
collection interface comprises a transferable hand-camera
module and a collection handle built around a Meta Quest
2 controller, as shown in Fig. 2(a). The module combines a
five-fingered, 20-DoF Wuji robot hand with a wide-angle
fisheye camera through a rigid 3D-printed flange. During
collection, it attaches to the collection handle; during de-
ployment, the same physical module mounts to the robot
wrist. Reusing the module preserves the hand embodiment
and camera-to-hand extrinsics across the two settings, so
recorded hand targets transfer to the robot without retar-
geting. This satisfies the Same Emb. requirement. The
mechanical design can be adapted to other hand-camera pairs
by replacing the flange. We design flanges for both the Wuji
1 and Wuji 2.
Recorded Signals. During data collection, the Quest 2
controller provides the wrist’s 6-DoF trajectory, from which
we construct end-effector motion targets. The fisheye camera
captures the hand-object interaction and surrounding task
context, while the hand controller records both the com-
manded 20-DoF joint targets and the resulting joint states.
Each demonstration therefore carries the actions actually
sent to the hand, rather than actions inferred from measured
states, satisfying the Action Data requirement. The rigid
connections keep the controller-to-hand and camera-to-hand
transforms fixed, so the tracked motion and visual observa-
tions remain expressed relative to the same hand frame.
Ergonomic
Design. The complete hand-held assembly
weighs 0.955 kg, of which the non-hand hardware contributes
0.220 kg. To support this load while leaving the thumb and
index finger free to operate the controls, we extend the Quest
2 battery cover into a collection handle that connects the
controller to the transferable module. A strap across the back
of the operator’s hand carries part of the load, reducing the
sustained grip force.
Our initial design placed the controller behind the robot
hand, offsetting the operator’s grip from the assembly’s
center of mass and creating a long moment arm, as shown
in Fig. 2(b). The final design positions the controller above
the hand so that the grip lies near the center of mass. This
shortens the moment arm and reduces the wrist torque that
the operator must counteract.


B. Dexterous Control via Parameterized Skills
Control via skills is an effective way to operate high-
dimensional robotic systems. For example, a humanoid can
be commanded through a desired velocity rather than by
specifying the motion of every joint. We apply the same
principle to dexterous hands by separating the operator’s
intent from the finger motions required to realize it. To this
end, we design a compact set of skills that we find effective
across a wide range of dexterous tasks. The skill interface is
parameterized instead of discrete, allowing more wholistic
control. For instance, the user controls the strength of the
grasp by using the joystick to incrementally close or open
the grasp, instead of snapping to a single fixed grasp.
Together, the skills keep all 20 joints of the hand available
during collection without asking the operator to command
each one, satisfying the DoF requirement that separates a
dexterous interface from a simpler gripper. We implement
this interface using a Meta Quest 2 controller, which provides
two buttons, two continuous triggers, and a two-axis joystick.
As illustrated in Fig. 2(c) and detailed below, these inputs
allow the operator to select and modulate the skills.
• Thumb Control. The two joystick axes control thumb
flexion and extension along one axis and abduction and
adduction along the other, following HATO [29]. This
allows the operator to position the thumb independently
for different grasp geometries.
• Power Grasp. One joystick axis controls the coordinated
closure of the four non-thumb fingers. Moving the joystick
incrementally loosens or tightens the grasp, while releas-
ing it holds the current target. Unlike continuous trigger
control [29], this persistent command does not require
the operator to maintain pressure throughout a grasp. It
is primarily used to wrap the hand around larger objects.
• Pinch Grasp. The same incremental command controls
only the index finger, which closes against the indepen-
dently positioned thumb. This skill supports small objects
and grasps that require precise fingertip contact.
• Two-Finger Grasp. The index and middle fingers open
or close together while the remaining fingers retain their
targets. This skill supports multi-stage behaviors such
as multi-object grasping and two-finger grasp-and-pull
motions, without requiring the operator to coordinate the
two fingers individually.
• In-Hand Rotation. A button invokes a learned closed-
loop policy that generates the repeated finger gaiting
required to rotate a grasped tool in hand. The policy
controls the fingers while the operator continues to control
the global motion of the hand.
Learning the In-Hand Rotation Skill. We learn the in-
hand rotation skill in simulation. This relieves the operator of
difficult low-level, in-hand manipulation, especially without
finger-level haptic feedback. Similar to [21], we train a
closed-loop screwing policy in simulation with PPO [32] in
Isaac Lab [33].
We model the task as rotating a cylindrical handle on a
revolute joint. In the real-world, the operator can make wrist
adjustments, but we simplify the simulation environment so
hand base is fixed, with the policy commanding fingers only.
The policy learns delta actions over 12 actuated joints, with
the ring and pinky finger fixed. The policy observes a three-
step history, including measured joint positions, commanded
targets, and the previous action. We learn a privileged critic
which has access to the handle’s rotation angle.
We follow rewards and domain randomization similar to
[21], which includes a handle rotation, fingertip proximity,
bounded action smoothness, initial grasp proximity, joint
torque, and mechanical work rewards. We randomize object
mass, center of mass, friction, restitution, finger PD gains,
initial grasp, and random disturbance forces. We train for
1.18 × 108 environment steps across 4096 environments.
During collection, the actor, a three-layer MLP, runs at 20 Hz
on the hand’s joint encoders while the operator retains control
of the wrist.
C. Policy Learning
Raw-Action Supervision. The skills are used only to assist
the operator during data collection. The learned policy di-
rectly predicts wrist poses and 20-DoF hand joint targets. At
each time step, we record the raw 20-DoF target produced
by the active skill and sent to the hand controller, together
with the resulting measured joint state. The policy receives
no skill identity, skill parameter, or task-phase label. Instead,
it is trained to predict the commanded joint targets directly.
Thus, the skills structure how demonstrations are collected
without constraining the representation learned by the policy.
Switching the action label from the commanded target to
the measured joint state is the single change that defines our
state-as-action baseline in Sec. IV; every other part of the
recipe below is shared.
Observation and Action Spaces. We train a diffusion
policy [34] with a convolutional U-Net denoiser and a fine-
tuned DINOv2 visual encoder. Images are augmented with
a random crop and resize, color jitter, random grayscale,
and Gaussian blur. The vision encoder is optimized with
a reduced learning rate, one-tenth that of the remaining
network. The policy observes the two most recent time steps,
each consisting of a 224 × 224 RGB image, the end-effector
position represented relative to the current chunk start [9],
and the measured 20-DoF hand state.
The policy predicts a 16-step action chunk containing end-
effector poses relative to the start of the chunk and absolute
20-DoF hand targets. Each action step is 29-dimensional: 9
for the wrist (3 translation and a 6D rotation) and 20 for the
hand. For bimanual tasks, we concatenate the observations
and actions of the two hands without otherwise changing
the architecture. We train 50 DDIM steps and sample with
10 steps. Following HATO [29], policy inference runs asyn-
chronously to maintain smooth control.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate DEXPORT through three research questions:
• Q1: Do the hand commands recorded by DEXPORT enable
policies that succeed on diverse dexterous tasks?
• Q2: Does co-training with demonstrations collected in
unstructured environments improve robustness to unseen
objects and cluttered scenes?
• Q3: How does the collection throughput of DEXPORT
compare with teleoperation and human-hand demos?
A. Experimental Setup
Robot Platform. We deploy policies on Tianji Marvin, a
bimanual robot with two 7-DoF arms. The hand–camera
modules used for collection (Sec. III-A) mount directly to
its wrists, so the hand embodiment and camera-to-hand
extrinsics are identical across collection and deployment.
Hand targets therefore need no retargeting, and the only cor-
rection at deployment is the fixed controller-to-end-effector
transform (Sec. III-C).
We showcase the modularity of our system by using
different hands: the Wuji 1 hand for Pick-and-Place and
Screwing, and the Wuji 2 hand for Drawer Stowing and
Bimanual Barcode Scanning.
Tasks. We evaluate four tasks that exercise complementary
forms of dexterity. Fig. 4 shows a successful rollout of each,
annotated with the phases we use to score partial success
and with the grasp types that emerge:
• Vegetable Pick-and-Place. The robot sequentially picks
and places two vegetables into a plate. Variations in object
size and shape require the policy to select between pinch
and power grasps without explicit grasp labels.
• Screwing. The robot maintains a stable grasp while rotat-
ing a screwdriver in hand, testing sustained contact and
repeated finger gaiting.
• Drawer Stowing. Without releasing a grasped object, the
robot opens a drawer with the same hand, places the object
inside, and closes the drawer. This task couples object
retention with articulated-object manipulation.
• Bimanual Barcode Scanning. One hand grasps and
presents an object while the other positions and operates
a barcode scanner. Success requires coordinated bimanual
positioning of the object and scanner.
Observations. Policies observe the fisheye view from the
hand–camera module, shown for every task in Fig. 3. For
drawer stowing we add a third-person camera, because the
grasped object occludes much of the scene while the drawer
is pulled open. The operator is occasionally visible in the
recorded frames, and we apply no post-processing.
Datasets. We collect 1,605 laboratory demonstrations across
the four tasks: 700 for vegetable pick-and-place, 200 for
screwdriver manipulation, 380 for drawer stowing, and 325
for bimanual barcode scanning. We additionally collect 1,000
pick-and-place demonstrations in unstructured apartment and
office environments. The co-trained policies use both the
laboratory and in-the-wild pick-and-place datasets.
Training Variants and Baselines. To answer Q1, we
compare policies trained on the commanded hand targets
recorded by DEXPORT against a state-as-action baseline that
instead uses measured hand states as action labels. To answer
Q2, we compare the laboratory-only pick-and-place policy
with two co-trained variants. To answer Q3, we compare
the number of trajectories collected per 30 minutes using
DEXPORT, teleoperation, and human-hand demonstrations.

C. Co-Training with In-the-Wild Data (Q2)
We collect a large in-the-wild pick-and-place dataset
across several sites, including an apartment room and a range
of office tables. DEXPORT requires neither a robot arm nor
external tracking infrastructure, thus enabling this portable
data collection. We use the same training objects, with the
difference being diversity in scene, background, and lighting
rather than in object identity, as Fig. 3 illustrates.
Fig. 6 shows that full success improves in every condition
and grows with the amount of in-the-wild data. With 1,000
in-the-wild demonstrations, full success rises from 40% to
87% under distractors and from 20% to 73% on unseen
objects, so the benefit transfers to new objects. It also
improves on seen objects, from 47% to 73%. App. A reports
a lower data regime.

source of training data, as human hand data provides neither
the deployed hand’s embodiment nor its commanded actions.
V. CONCLUSIONS
We presented DEXPORT, a portable interface for collecting
demonstrations with high-DoF dexterous hands. The system
uses the same hand hardware for collection and deploy-
ment, preserving its contact geometry while recording its
commanded targets and resulting joint states. A compact set
of skills makes the hand practical to operate by separating
task-level intent from detailed finger coordination. These
skills assist only the demonstrator; the learned policy directly
predicts the raw hand commands without skill or task-phase
labels. In our experiments, DEXPORT collects 122 trajecto-
ries per 30 minutes, roughly 1.4× the rate of teleoperation.
Policies trained on the resulting demonstrations succeed on
all four tasks and consistently outperform a state-as-action
baseline, which never completes a bimanual barcode scan.
With 700 laboratory demonstrations, co-training with in-the-
wild data further improves pick-and-place success in every
evaluation condition. Together, these design choices provide
a practical and scalable solution for collecting portable,
executable demonstrations for dexterous robot learning.
Limitations. Despite its ergonomic design, the weight of the
device limits uninterrupted use and rotation-heavy motions.


As the weight of the hand is the primary bottleneck, we
expect that future hardware developments for lighter and
smaller hands can address this. Our skills also cover only
part of the space of dexterous behaviors, and future work
may explore extending them with additional skills learned in
simulation, such as other in-hand manipulation skills, learned
grasps, and more.

D. Policy Learning
Architecture. Table VI lists the hyperparameters of the
diffusion policy in Sec. III-C. The end-effector pose is
represented relative to the current end-effector frame, with
a 3-D translation and a 6-D rotation. Each predicted wrist
action is a relative pose with respect to the start of the chunk,
and each hand action is an absolute 20-D target. Bimanual
policies concatenate the observations and actions of both
hands and additionally observe the relative pose between
them. All inputs and targets are normalized using their 2nd
and 98th percentiles.
E. Deployment
Mount Calibration. To estimate the controller-to-flange
transform M in (1), we bolt the module to each robot
flange and jog the arm through about 30 poses that tilt the
flange axis by at least 30◦, pausing 1.5 s at each pose before
recording the paired flange and controller poses. We initialize
M with a closed-form hand–eye solution and refine it by
nonlinear least squares.
Control. The policy runs at 30 Hz, matching the collection
rate. Following HATO [29], inference runs asynchronously:
each new 16-step chunk is aligned to the control tick at
which its observation was taken, the first 8 steps are kept,
steps whose time has passed are dropped, and the hand and
wrist commands at each tick average all chunks that cover
it. We sample each chunk with 10 DDIM steps. Predicted
wrist motions are converted to flange targets with the mount
transform and solved for the 7-DoF arm with Levenberg–
Marquardt inverse kinematics in PyRoki [40].
