Abstract —Force-sensitive manipulation requires contact and load information that is not provided explicitly by visual observations. We present CW-WAM, a world action model that jointly predicts video, motion, and contact wrench. Inspired by hybrid motion–force control, it contains a unified interaction module that treats both motion and contact wrench as modeling targets and predicts time-aligned motion–wrench chunks as control references. A pair-causal attention mechanism structures the motion–wrench dependencies, while a long fixed-length wrench memory retains past interaction information. The predicted wrench chunk acts as a force reference for a 20-Hz outer-loop admittance controller within a slow–fast control system that maintains force feedback and smooth motion between model queries. CW-WAM improves contact prediction and increases the insertion success rate from 25% to 70% for square pegs with 1-mm clearance per side and from 40% to 95% for power adapters, compared with WAM without wrench input. It also improves lifting-height accuracy across different loads. Our framework with an admittance controller reduces force-reference RMSE from 4.8 to 2.7 N for peg insertion and from 2.0 to 1.3 N for wiping, respectively, with smaller force fluctuations during successful execution. Project website: cw-wam-project.github.io .

I. I NTRODUCTION 

World action models (WAMs) jointly predict future visual observations and actions, providing coherent motion plans for manipulation [1]. Yet a square peg jammed during insertion may appear fully seated, and visually similar objects may differ substantially in weight. Such force-sensitive tasks require physical interaction information that wrist force–torque sensing can provide. Force feedback improves contact-rich manipulation by complementing visual observations [2], [3] and enabling rapid motion correction [4]. Joint action–wrench prediction extends these benefits: predicted forces guide learned feedback correction [5] or help evaluate contact outcomes [6]. These advances motivate WAMs that predict the wrench associated with each action and use it to guide execution. Contact depends on commanded motion, prior interaction, and the environment. To capture these dependencies, we design an interaction module inspired by hybrid motion–force control. The module pairs past commands with measured wrenches (Fig. 1) and predicts future motion and wrench using separate tokens, prediction heads, and training losses. High-rate wrench encoding captures contact changes within motion intervals, while longer memory retains earlier wrench measurements. CW-WAM integrates this module into a DreamZero-style video–action backbone [1]. During execution, paired predictions provide nominal motion and an expected wrench reference for admittance feedback. A slow–fast system applies corrections and tracks continuous motion between model queries. During contact, this keeps force feedback active while the model computes the next motion–wrench chunk. a) Visuomotor policies and WAMs: Visuomotor policies generate actions from visual or language-conditioned obser-vations [7], [8], [9], [10]. Alongside direct action genera-tion, world models support policy learning from imagined trajectories [11]. Video-based approaches recover actions from generated videos [12], [13], [14], while world action models such as DreamZero jointly model video and actions [1]. Cosmos Policy uses predicted states and values for planning [15], and Fast-WAM omits future-video generation at inference [16]. Building on this foundation, CW-WAM incorporates interaction history and wrench prediction. 

b) Contact-aware policies and action–wrench prediction: 

Contact-rich manipulation requires interaction signals that vision does not explicitly provide. Prior work incorporates tactile feedback for reactive correction [4] or visual–tactile– action modeling [17], [18], [19]. ForceVLA and ManipForce condition actions on F/T observations [20], [3], while FoAR modulates force feedback using predicted contact [2]. Gripper-force feedback also supports learning delicate grasping [21]. Beyond using force as input, FAWAM and Facet-0 jointly predict actions and wrenches. They use the predicted wrenches in a learned residual corrector and an action–wrench critic, respectively [5], [6]. CW-WAM pairs past motion commands with measured wrenches and retains longer wrench histories to capture prior contact. It predicts future motion and wrench using separate tokens, prediction heads, and losses. The predicted wrenches then serve as references for admittance feedback control, without a learned corrective policy. 

c) Slow–fast execution and contact feedback: Contact can change within an action chunk, requiring a fast response. To respond between planning updates, slow planners can guide learned residual or reflex policies [22], [23], while learned motion targets and compliance parameters guide high-rate force control [24], [25]. Force Policy combines global visual planning with fast local force control [26]. In CW-WAM, predicted wrench trajectories guide online admittance correction between model queries. III. B. Continuous action-chunk execution 

On a single NVIDIA RTX 5090, four-step model queries typically take 0.5–0.6 s, including observation processing, inference, and postprocessing, but excluding network commu-nication. Chunks execute sequentially (Fig. 3). While awaiting the next prediction, the robot holds the final nominal pose with admittance active during contact. An adaptive-duration 

C2 bridge joins each new chunk to the current commanded pose, matching velocity and acceleration. At 20 Hz, we interpolate nominal poses with C2 continuity and predicted wrenches linearly. All experiments select the wrench reference by matching the measured TCP pose to the planned motion trajectory. Matched progress ϕw cannot move backward or exceed scheduled progress ϕ, accommodating execution lag while preserving reference order. Every 50 ms, we replan a 0.5-s piecewise-quintic joint trajectory, matching the active trajectory’s position, velocity, and acceleration for C2 continuity. A 1-kHz joint-impedance controller tracks it with gravity and friction compensation. V. E XPERIMENTS 

We evaluate prediction accuracy through open-loop ablations, force-sensitive manipulation through real-robot tasks, and exe-cution quality with and without predicted-wrench admittance. 

A. Tasks and data collection 

We select four tasks to test contact-guided alignment, precise seating, force regulation, and adaptation to payload (Fig. 1, left). 

Square peg insertion. The robot inserts a 38-mm square peg into a 40-mm hole with 1-mm clearance per side and an insertion depth of about 50 mm. The tight clearance and long engagement make misalignment and jamming challenging. Demonstrations include smooth first-attempt insertion, failed attempts followed by recovery, and light jamming followed by alignment. Insertion success requires full seating; release success additionally requires the peg to remain seated after gripper opening. 

Power-adapter insertion. The robot grasps a loosely placed adapter and seats it in another socket. This tests precise positioning and insertion with contact. Compared with the long peg, the adapter is less prone to jamming once insertion begins. Demonstrations therefore emphasize successful first-attempt insertion with minimal lateral adjustment. The success criteria are the same as for peg insertion. 

Whiteboard wiping. The robot picks up an eraser and removes a marker trace through sustained surface contact. The traces vary in orientation and curvature, requiring both spatial coverage and sustained force regulation. Success requires complete marker removal after automatic eraser pickup. We also quantify ink removal in additional patterned-wiping recordings and evaluate contact-force accuracy separately. 

Spring-scale transport. The robot grasps a spring scale attached to a box and transfers the box between bins. The boxes weigh either 30 g or 830 g but look identical. The heavier box stretches the spring farther, requiring a higher lift to clear the rim. This tests whether contact and load information guide motion beyond visual appearance. Success requires rim clearance, transport, and placement in the destination bin. Training uses task-specific language instructions and 450 demonstrations: 213 peg insertions, 97 adapter insertions, 64 whiteboard wipes, and 76 spring-scale transports. B. Model variants 

WAM has no wrench input or output. WAM + Wrench 

encodes pooled wrench history and predicts concatenated 16-D action–wrench vectors. It uses the current robot state without explicit past motion-command tokens. CW-WAM w/o LTM 

introduces the interaction module: explicit paired command and wrench history, separate future streams, and PC attention. Its recent history pairs 24 motion commands with wrench intervals over 1.6 s. Full CW-WAM adds LTM. CW-WAM w/o LTM and PC replaces only PC with bidirectional attention, isolating attention structure within the no-LTM module. All variants share training data, backbone initialization, and a 50k-step schedule with task-balanced sampling. WAM omits wrench loss. WAM + Wrench uses either padded-width normalization, dividing masked squared-error sums by 32, or CW-WAM’s valid-channel normalization (Sec. III-C), with architecture and other settings unchanged. The latter tests the interaction module under the same loss normalization. PC and LTM ablations retain the CW-WAM objective: PC is tested without LTM, and LTM with PC retained.

E. Task completion and contact behavior 

The interaction module improves insertion and release (Fig. 4). Relative to WAM + Wrench, CW-WAM w/o LTM raises adapter insertion from 9/20 to 16/20 and release from 0/20 to 13/20. Peg release improves from 1/20 to 6/20, with insertion at 7/20 versus 8/20. Longer wrench memory improves both insertion tasks: peg insertion rises from 7/20 to 14/20 and release from 6/20 to 11/20; adapter insertion rises from 16/20 to 19/20 and release from 13/20 to 16/20. It also lowers force-reference error in adapter insertion and wiping, demonstrating the task value of longer interaction context (Fig. 4). In successful peg trials, CW-WAM reduces position-tracking RMSE from 12.5 to 2.8 mm and force-reference RMSE from 15.2 to 4.8 N relative to WAM + Wrench (Fig. 4). Force profiles also show less variation during search and seating (Fig. 5(b)). For adapter insertion, CW-WAM improves completion and reduces position-tracking RMSE from 25.3 to 8.8 mm. Its higher force-reference RMSE and contact-force variation relative to WAM + Wrench (Figs. 4 and 5(c)) are consistent with the observation that most successful WAM + Wrench trials rely on precise, one-shot alignment without adjustment, followed by full insertion. In contrast, CW-WAM’s learned search and firm insertion behavior improves insertion success while producing higher contact forces. In the whiteboard-wiping task, all methods remove the ink in 20/20 trials with simple patterns. For more complicated patterns, such as circles and letters, the average removal rate is 85.9% across the six selected trace patterns. For contact-force quality, force-reference RMSE and contact-force variation both decrease from WAM + Wrench to CW-WAM with admittance, showing more accurate force tracking and smoother contact (Figs. 4 and 5(a)). F. Load-dependent lifting 

CW-WAM reproduces load-dependent lifting heights more accurately than the other models, even when the boxes look identical. Its median peak heights are 423.6 and 481.8 mm for light and heavy loads, respectively, close to the demonstration medians of 420.9 and 492.7 mm (Fig. 6). CW-WAM w/o LTM shows similar lifting behavior (not plotted). WAM reaches 464.4 and 447.3 mm, showing no higher lift for heavier loads. WAM + Wrench gives an intermediate response at 431.3 and 467.9 mm: its absolute differences from the demonstration medians are 10.4 and 24.8 mm, compared with 2.7 and 10.9 mm for CW-WAM.

VI. C ONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION 

In this work, we presented CW-WAM, a world action model that jointly predicts video, motion, and contact wrench. The interaction module combines paired action–wrench history, separate prediction streams, and longer wrench memory. Across four real-robot tasks, CW-WAM improves insertion and release success rates and adapts the lifting height to different loads. Compared with the WAM baseline without wrench input, the peg insertion success rate increases from 25% to 70%, and the adapter insertion success rate increases from 40% to 95%. The slow–fast execution system uses predicted wrenches as references for admittance feedback control between model queries. During successful execution, this feedback mechanism reduces force-reference RMSE from 4.8 N to 2.7 N for peg insertion and from 2.0 N to 1.3 N for wiping, while also reducing contact-force variation. Our evaluation covers four tasks within the demonstration dis-tribution, using manually tuned feedback gains and predefined compliant directions. Future work will examine how controller settings affect performance and explore learning additional contact parameters such as the feedback-weighting matrix and contact frame, alongside motion and wrench prediction for end-to-end closed-loop control.
