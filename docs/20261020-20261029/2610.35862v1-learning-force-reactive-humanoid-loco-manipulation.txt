Abstract— Many everyday tasks require controlling contact
force as well as motion. On a humanoid, this force must often
be coordinated across both hands and the whole body, and
cannot be specified by kinematic trajectories alone. There-
fore, we present FLORE, a framework for learning force-
reactive humanoid loco-manipulation. We collect robot-free
demonstrations using two force-sensing UMI-style handheld
grippers for hand motion and contact wrenches, and wearable
trackers for body motion. A force-reactive diffusion policy
takes vision, proprioception, and measured wrenches as input,
then predicts actions and per-hand force commands. A hybrid
compliance whole-body controller with force tracking executes
these commands while maintaining balance: an integral of the
force error measured by real force/torque (F/T) sensors lets each
hand track force accurately along the commanded direction
and remain compliant along the others. In simulation, the
controller tracks per-hand force commands of varied magnitude
and direction, with little loss in whole-body motion accuracy.
Across three contact-rich tasks on the robot, the force-reactive
policy succeeds in 80% of rollouts, compared with 26.7% for
a position-only policy trained on the same demonstrations and
executed by the same controller. For more details, please visit
our website: https://louis-leee.github.io/FLoRe/.

I. INTRODUCTION
Humanoid robots offer a general-purpose embodiment for
working in environments built for people. Many tasks in
these environments, such as pushing a cart, moving a heavy
box, and wiping a surface, require the robot to control contact
force as well as motion. However, learned humanoid loco-
manipulation is commonly formulated as generating and
tracking motion references [1]–[3]. Tracking a demonstrated
hand trajectory alone does not reproduce the demonstrated
force, which also depends on contact geometry, stiffness,
and object motion. Unlike a fixed-base arm, a humanoid
must stay balanced under these contact loads, coordinating
its hands, torso, and legs while regulating force.
Two difficulties stand in the way. The first is data: imi-
tation learning requires scalable demonstrations that record
contact force alongside motion. Existing robot-free interfaces
for whole-body demonstration collection record motion but
not force [4], [5]. The second is execution: the robot must
accurately apply the desired force. Current learned humanoid
whole-body controllers track motion alone, or treat force
as a disturbance to reject or a stiffness to lower [6], [7].

Tracking a commanded contact force has been shown only
on quadrupedal manipulators, using estimated rather than
measured force [8], [9], which is not accurate enough. A
humanoid instead needs to track force at both hands together
with whole-body motion while maintaining balance.
We present FLORE, a framework for learning force-
reactive humanoid loco-manipulation. Its robot-free force-
annotated data collection system records contact force with
each demonstration; the policy learns to predict this force,
and the controller tracks it. The system pairs two UMI-style
handheld grippers [10] with a chest tracker and two ankle
trackers [4], [11]. Each gripper carries a wrist camera, a pose
tracker, and a six-axis F/T sensor [12], [13]. The applied
wrench is thus recorded alongside hand, torso, and foot
motion. The robot uses the same gripper–sensor assembly.
For the high-level planner, we propose a force-reactive
diffusion policy [14]. It observes wrist images, robot state,
and the measured wrench at each hand, then predicts a
chunk [15] of action, gripper widths, and per-hand desired
force. Force enters as both an input and an output. The input
lets the policy react to interactions that images do not reveal.
The output is commanded to the controller [13], rather than
used only as an auxiliary target [16]. This connects the force
measured during demonstration to the deployed force.
To track the commanded forces, we develop a whole-
body controller with hybrid compliance. It outputs only joint
positions, so each hand’s force command becomes a shift of
its position target. In contact, an integral of the force error
measured at the wrist adjusts this shift until the hand exerts
the commanded force, whatever the contact stiffness. Along
uncommanded axes, the same integral lets the hand yield
to external pushes. Each hand thus tracks force along the
commanded direction and complies along the others, without
switching modes. We train the controller in simulation [17]
and deploy it on the robot using wrist F/T measurements.
We deploy FLORE on a Unitree G1 and evaluate three
whole-body contact-rich tasks: pushing a gantry hanging
another robot, bimanual box rotation, and board wiping.
In simulation, the controller realises in-contact force error
of about 1 N per hand while tracking whole-body motion.
On the real robot, FLORE succeeds in 80% of rollouts,
compared with 26.7% for a position-only policy trained on
the same demonstrations and executed by the same controller
with force commands disabled. Withholding only the force
command reduces success to 36.7%, while removing only
the force observation reduces it to 66.7%. Both contribute to
the final performance. Our key contributions are:
• A robot-free pipeline for learning force-reactive humanoid
loco-manipulation from demonstrations that record both
motion and contact wrench.
• A learned whole-body controller that tracks per-hand force
together with keypoint motion while remaining compliant
to external forces and maintaining balance.
• Evaluation in simulation and on three contact-rich tasks on
a Unitree G1, where force commands and force observa-
tions both improve task success over position-only control.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Force Sensing for Loco-Manipulation Data Collection
and Policy Learning
Robot-free data collection builds on UMI [10]. Force-
aware variants add wrist or fingertip sensing, but deploy
on fixed-base arms with analytic impedance or admittance
control [12], [13], [18]. Humanoid extensions instead collect
whole-body motion and execute it with learned trackers, but
do not record contact force [4], [11]. WT-UMI is the closest
interface for humanoids, although it measures only a scalar
normal force at each palm and relies on paired teleoperation
data [19]. Our interface records a six-axis wrench at each
hand together with torso motion, and its demonstrations
require no robot-side data.
Measured force has been used to condition visuomotor
policies [20], [21]. Other work predicts stiffness, admittance
gains, or virtual position targets that are executed by an
impedance controller [12], [22]–[24]. More closely related
methods predict a wrench trajectory together with pose,
but execute it on fixed-base arms through analytic force
controllers [13], [25], [26]. On humanoids, WT-UMI passes
a scalar normal-force prediction to an admittance rule [19].
UniFP is the closest formulation. It predicts 3-D force
commands, but deploys end-effector force control only on
a quadrupedal manipulator. Its humanoid interface exposes
force only at the base [9]. Our planner instead predicts per-
hand pose, gripper, and wrench trajectories from handheld
demonstrations, then sends the predicted force to a learned
humanoid whole-body controller.
B. Force for Low-Level Control
Learned legged and humanoid controllers usually either
withstand external force or yield to it through commanded
stiffness or damping [6], [7], [27]–[30], but neither approach
directly specifies how hard to press. Direct force tracking
has mainly been studied on quadrupedal manipulators, as
a separate control mode or through a target-shifting spring
law, both using force estimated from proprioception [8], [9].
WT-UMI regulates a scalar palm force on a humanoid, but
through an analytic admittance rule and inverse kinematics
above a separate locomotion controller [19]. In contrast, our
hybrid compliance whole-body controller with force tracking
integrates the force error measured at each wrist. One learned
policy tracks position in free space and force along the
commanded direction, while remaining compliant along the
other directions and maintaining balance.
III. METHOD
In FLORE (Fig. 2), a hybrid compliance whole-body
controller with force tracking executes motion references and
per-hand force commands (Sec. III-A); a robot-free force-
annotated data collection system records vision, motion, and
contact wrenches with handheld devices (Sec. III-B); and a
force-reactive diffusion policy, trained on these demonstra-
tions, predicts keypoint motion and force commands for the
controller (Sec. III-C).

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate whether a humanoid can learn force-reactive
loco-manipulation from robot-free demonstrations. Experi-
ments in simulation and on the robot test four capabilities:
• Whole-body force tracking: tracking per-hand force com-
mands and whole-body motion with one learned controller.
• Sustained contact: making contact and holding an appro-
priate force while the hands move with or along the object.
• Bimanual and whole-body coordination: applying force
with both hands while the torso and legs balance and walk.
• Robustness to force variation: completing tasks when
resistance changes within or across episodes but is not
visible in the images, e.g., a heavier load or box mass.
We first compare our hybrid compliance whole-body con-
troller with force tracking (Sec. III-A) to three baselines:
• SONIC [3]: a large-scale whole-body motion tracker, run
from its released checkpoint. It tracks motion references
and accepts no force command.
• SONIC + admittance: SONIC followed by an ana-
lytic force servo that adds α J⊤
h(f cmd
h
+ f meas

joint targets, a correction that SONIC does not observe.
• FLORE w/o Force: our controller trained with the force
channel removed (f cmd ≡0, no Ih, ∆xh, ch observed).
The second comparison isolates how the high-level policy
uses force. FLORE observes the measured wrench (F-in),
learns to predict the demonstrated force (F-out), and sends
this prediction to the controller (F-cmd). w/o F-cmd and
w/o F-in remove one pathway each, and DP all three:
• w/o F-cmd: the FLORE policy with its predicted force
withheld (f cmd ≡0), which turns off the force channel
(ch, Ih, ∆xh ≡0) so that the controller tracks pose only.
• w/o F-in: the FLORE policy trained without the mea-
sured wrench in its observation. It still commands force
but cannot sense whether contact is made or lost.

at most 8.0 mm of wrist position error, while its wrist
orientation error roughly halves; it also tracks orientation
better than SONIC, at a higher root error. When a force is
commanded, the hand target is shifted by design (f cmd/K
alone is 12.5 cm at 25 N), and the hand departs from the
motion reference only as far as needed to realize the force.
Hybrid compliance controller tracks commanded force
accurately. Neither SONIC nor FLORE w/o Force ac-
cepts a force command. SONIC + admittance does, but
its servo acts outside the balance policy and realizes only
0.67–0.70 of the commanded force, with an in-contact error
of about 7.9 N. FLORE tracks 5–25 N commands with a gain
of 1.03–1.06 and about 1 N of in-contact error per hand;
lateral and downward commands reach a lower gain and
leak partly into the other axes. With nothing commanded
the sensor still reads 1.25–1.50 N, so at deployment we zero
commands below 1.5 N and cap them at 25 N. Integrating
the measured force error removes the dependence on the
unknown contact stiffness: Spring-Law, the target-shift
law of [9] without its force-feedback term, cannot detect
contact, and its force scales with that stiffness relative to the
assumed K. The same integral makes the hand compliant:
while it tracks a force, a push along an uncommanded axis
is read by the wrist sensor and builds up the integral, which
moves the target with it, so the hand gives way instead of
resisting, with no mode switch (real robot, Fig. 5 bottom).
Real-world force tracking. We verify force tracking on the
robot against an external reference: a rigidly mounted digital
force gauge that the hand presses along each of ±x, ±y, and
±z while the humanoid stands freely. The command follows
a staircase of 4, 6, 8, and 10 N, repeated across directions
and hands. Along the x axis, the measured steady-state force
reaches approximately 91% of the commanded magnitude,
with a mean absolute steady-state error of about 1.1 N. The
response enters a ±1 N band in 1.8 s on average, while the
steady-state fluctuation remains approximately ±0.3–0.5 N.
The controller consistently recovers the commanded contact
force without loss of stability.

D. Rotate Box
Task. As shown in Fig. 1c, the robot pinches the rim of a
box with both hands. It rotates the box by about 50◦, drags it
about 0.5 m across the table, and releases it. The box remains
on the table, so the measured wrench reflects table friction,
box inertia, and the internal squeeze between the hands. A
rollout succeeds if the box rotates by more than 40◦and
remains upright after release.
Capabilities. Bimanual coordination: the hands share an
internal force and rotate about a common axis. In the
demonstrations, the right hand applies about 1.4× the
force of the left. If one hand leads the other, the box
tips. Sustained contact: the pinch must hold through the
drag and the yaw without slipping or lifting the box.
Robustness to force variation: friction changes with the box
mass and when the box begins to slide.
Test scenarios. We run 10 rollouts per policy over two box
masses and five initial yaw angles.
Performance. Ours succeeds in 9/10 rollouts and has an
offline pose error of 0.70 cm. DP succeeds in 3/10 and
exhibits two failure modes (Fig. 6, bottom left). It either
fails to drag the heavy box or leans into it and loses balance.
w/o F-cmd succeeds in 4/10 and fails similarly because its


predicted squeeze is not executed. w/o F-in succeeds in
7/10 but cannot react when the box begins to slide.
E. Wipe Board
Task. As shown in Fig. 1d, the robot presses an eraser against
a whiteboard and wipes a marked strip. Success depends on
normal force, which is not visible in the images. A rollout
succeeds if the strip is erased without a stop.
Capabilities. Sustained contact: the robot must maintain
normal force during each stroke and re-establish it after
losing contact. Images alone do not distinguish hovering
from pressing. Robustness to force variation: we displace
the board by ±3 cm along its normal. The demonstrated
trajectory then produces either no contact or excessive force
if tracked directly.
Test scenarios. We run 10 rollouts per policy over two board
offsets (±3 cm along the normal) and two surfaces.
Performance. Ours erases the strip in 8/10 rollouts. DP
succeeds in 3/10. It usually hovers above the board (Fig. 6,
bottom right). This directly shows that observed motion can
be valid supervision in free space but not in contact [41].
w/o F-cmd succeeds in 4/10. It detects contact but cannot
increase a force that is too low. w/o F-in succeeds in 7/10
but continues wiping in free space when the board is farther
away. This task also has the largest offline errors: 2.02 cm
for pose and 1.59 N for force.
F. Findings Summary
F1: The force channel costs little motion tracking. Relative
to FLORE w/o Force, FLORE adds 1.3–3.2 cm of root
error and at most 8.0 mm of wrist position error while
halving wrist orientation error (Table I); in return it tracks
commanded force with a gain of 1.03–1.06 and about 1 N
of in-contact error. The 1.25–1.50 N F=0 floor bounds
the smallest verifiable command, and lateral and downward
commands remain weaker.
F2: Force-reactive policies resolve failures that motion
alone cannot distinguish. With the same demonstrations and
controller, DP succeeds in 26.7% of rollouts and FLORE
in 80% (Table II). The position-only policy stalls when a
reached pose does not move the object, hovers or presses
too hard when the board is displaced, and loses balance
against the heavy box (Fig. 6). Force also composes with
coordination: both hands hold the internal force on Rotate
Box, and the push is sustained while walking on Push Gantry.
F3: Force commands give the larger, significant gain;
force observations show a smaller trend. Withholding the
predicted force from the controller reduces success from 80%
(24/30) to 36.7% (11/30; Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.001): the
policy senses resistance but can respond only through pose.
Removing the force observation reduces success to 66.7%.
V. CONCLUSION
We
presented
FLORE,
a
framework
for
learning
force-reactive humanoid loco-manipulation from robot-free
demonstrations that record both motion and contact wrench.
Our hybrid compliance whole-body controller tracks the
learned force commands, allowing the humanoid to sustain
contact, coordinate both hands, and apply force while walk-
ing and balancing. On three tasks, the force-reactive policy
succeeds in 80% of rollouts, compared with 26.7% for a
position-only policy. By making force a shared interface
between demonstrations, policies, and whole-body control,
FLORE extends robot-free imitation learning to contact-rich
skills that motion alone cannot specify.
