## ABSTRACT 

Dexterous multi-finger hands hold promise to unlock diverse skills like functional grasps, in-hand reorientation, and dynamic handover that the human-designed world assumes, yet existing learning recipes force a trade-off. Goal-reaching re-inforcement learning generalizes to arbitrary object poses but, lacking any hu-man prior, converges to unnatural and unreliable finger strategies; motion track-ing reproduces human-like behavior from a single clip but cannot deviate from it. We present ADA DEX , a goal-conditioned RL framework that turns a single hand-object trajectory of a few seconds—from mocap data or HOI reconstruc-tion from in-the-wild RGB(D) video—into an adaptable dexterous skill. At the core of ADA DEX is Hybrid Dexterous Mimicking : a shared policy is trained on two populations of massively parallel environments: motion tracking environ-ments trained with dense motion tracking objective, which instill the human prior from human motion; and generalization environments with noised initial states and goals and a sparse object-success reward, which force the skill to work far off the reference. From seconds of human motion per skill, the resulting policies can learn diverse adaptable dexterous skills, such as functional grasping, object reposing, in-hand reorientation with finger gaiting, and dynamic behaviors like throw-and-catch. Our method outperforms baselines in terms of generalization and functionality, and we demonstrate transfer across simulators and to the real world. Our built environment is designed around the human hand: tools, handles, lids, and switches all presuppose a multi-fingered end-effector. Many of the skills this design affords, including func-tional tool use, in-hand reorientation, and dynamic behaviors such as throwing and catching, re-main difficult for parallel-jaw grippers, which makes dexterous hands a natural embodiment for general-purpose manipulation. Despite this promise, learning dexterous skills is challenging. Amulti-fingered hand must act in a high-dimensional continuous action space with 20+ degrees of freedom, and each skill must regulate contact forces, friction, and gravity across many simultaneous and often intermittent contacts under fast dynamics. Two paradigms currently dominate learning-based dexterous manipulation, and they fail in comple-mentary ways. Consider picking up a hammer lying on a table and striking a nail. Goal-reaching reinforcement learning (Kuang et al., 2026; Kedia et al., 2026) trains a single policy to move an object from any initial pose to any target pose, achieving broad coverage. Given target hammer poses, such a policy can bring the hammer to the nail. However, an object-centric reward specifies only where the object should end up, not how to get it there. Without explicit shaping, the policy could seize the hammer by its head or pinch it mid-shaft. These grasps might satisfy the goal but are not functional for hammering. Additionally, behaviors that require finger gaiting or regrasping, such as reorienting the hammer in hand from a lying-flat pickup to a striking grip with functional thumb bracing, are rarely discovered at all. As a result, the intricate motion the task demands remain largely out of reach. Motion tracking (Li et al., 2025; Mandi et al., 2025), in contrast, imitates a hand-object trajectory under dense supervision and thereby inherits the demonstrator’s functional grasp, in-hand regrasp, and swing. The resulting policy, however, is bound to the reference. If the nail sits at a different location or angle, the policy has no mechanism to adapt, and it typically requires the dense reference as input at inference time. In short, tracking supplies the human prior and goal reaching supplies generalization, but neither alone yields a skill that is both human-like and adaptable. To that end, we present ADA DEX , which treats the two paradigms as complements rather than alternatives. At the core of ADA DEX is Hybrid Dexterous Mimicking , built on the observation that motion tracking and goal reaching are the same goal-conditioned problem: both ask a policy 

πθ (a | s, g ) to bring the object and wrist to a target pose g, and differ only in where g comes from and how progress is rewarded. We therefore train one policy across two kinds of massively parallel environments: Tracking Environments (TrackEnv) initialize along a human hand-object reference ˆτ ,take goals from ˆτ , and apply a dense tracking reward on object, wrist, fingers, and contacts, teaching the policy how a human grasps, gaits, and releases. Generalization Environments (GenEnv) perturb the initialization with large noise, offset the goal by a random SE(3) transformation that can be far from any frame of ˆτ , and reward only sparse object success plus wrist guidance, with no finger terms. The two are mutually reinforcing. TrackEnv supplies the prior and the exploration: skills that sparse reward almost never discovers, such as throwing an object and catching it mid-flight, are learned under dense tracking and, because g is the only channel of task information, become directly addressable by novel goals. GenEnv supplies the generalization: it forces success from off-reference states toward off-reference goals so the skill can be adaptable to new scenarios and does not collapse into replaying one clip, while leaving the finger strategy to the human prior. Neither suffices alone; together they yield a skill that is both functional and adaptable. The recipe needs no discriminator, phase variable, or distillation stage, and the dense reference is not required at test time: at deployment the policy consumes only proprioception, object pose, object shape, and a goal pose, so it can be driven by any goal source, such as VLM Agent planner’s output waypoints. We evaluate ADA DEX on functional grasping of diverse tools and household objects, in-hand reori-entation requiring finger gaiting and contact switching, and dynamic behaviors like throw-and-catch. Every skill is learned from a single human motion trajectory of a few seconds. The learned policies preserve the grasp type, in-hand manipulation, and dynamic behaviors from human motion, while generalizing to unseen trajectories and randomized object goals far from the reference, and achieve various downstream tasks with VLM Agents under multiple embodiments. Additionally, we demon-strate that our recipe can be embedded in between real-to-sim and sim-to-real, where a few seconds of human motion from in-the-wild RGB(D) videos is converted into a simulation-ready hand-object reference via our advanced HOI reconstruction pipeline. And then the reference is broadened into an adaptable skill in simulation as described above, and the resulting simulation rollout is transferred to a real arm-hand robotic system with real-time dynamics. In summary, our contributions are: 2• Hybrid Dexterous Mimicking. A goal-conditioned RL formulation in which dense reference tracking and sparse goal reaching are trained as parallel environment types under a single shared actor, so that the human prior from tracking and the coverage from goal reaching reinforce one another. The formulation requires no dense reference input at inference. • Adaptable dexterous skills. Human-like functional grasping, in-hand reorientation with finger gaiting, and dynamic behaviors like throw-and-catch, each learned from seconds of human motion and generalized to novel states and goals. A reconstruction pipeline that converts a clip of in-the-wild human video into a simulation-ready hand-object reference, outperforming existing HOI reconstruction baselines. This pipeline allows us to learn adaptable dexterous skills from human data and transfer their simulation rollouts across simulators and to the real world. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 DEXTEROUS SKILL LEARNING 

Endowing robots with human-level, generalizable dexterity is a long-standing goal toward general-ist agents that operate across diverse real-world scenarios. A large body of prior work formulates dexterous manipulation as contact-based optimization (Liu et al., 2020; 2021; Wang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024; Yin & Abbeel, 2025), synthesizing static grasps and planning motions. These approaches are largely confined to grasping and, lacking closed-loop feedback, remain brittle un-der disturbances. An alternative line of work collects teleoperated demonstrations for behavior cloning (Wang et al., 2024a; Lin et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025a). The resulting policies are bound to the distribution of in-domain demonstrations and generalize poorly; moreover, existing teleoperation interfaces (e.g., gloves and exoskeletons) struggle to capture behaviors that demand high dexterity or dynamic motion. More recently, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising route to generalizable dexterity. Task-specific methods have been developed for dex-terous grasping (Wan et al., 2023; Lum et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2025) and in-hand reorientation (Qi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024b; Liu et al., 2025b), while two more general paradigms have gained traction: motion tracking (Liu et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025; Mandi et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025b; Liang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2026) and generalized goal reaching (Kuang et al., 2026; Kedia et al., 2026). Each paradigm exhibits a complementary limitation: motion tracking faithfully reproduces a reference but cannot generalize beyond it, whereas goal reaching handles arbitrary goals but yields motions that lack human-like functionality. Our method unifies the two so that they reinforce each other, learning adaptable dexterous skills that span functional grasping, in-hand manipulation, and dynamic behaviors from only seconds of human motion. 2.2 ROBOT LEARNING FROM HUMAN MOTION 

Human data constitutes the most scalable source of supervision for robot learning, and human mo-tion in particular has been widely exploited (Wu et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2023). Motion tracking has become the dominant recipe for humanoid control and loco-manipulation (Liao et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026), and the same idea has been extended to dexterous manipulation (Liu et al., 2025a; Xu et al., 2025b; Lum et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2026). These methods, however, typically offer limited generalization and require a dense reference trajectory as the goal at inference time. We are inspired by recent work on hybrid imitation learning, including HIL (Wang et al., 2026b) and GfR (Wang et al., 2026c), which relax this requirement, en-abling humanoid parkour from sparse goals while still generalizing beyond the reference. Building on this insight, our method couples motion tracking with goal reaching RL to learn dexterous skills that are simultaneously human-like and generalizable from seconds of human motion.

Pure motion tracking and pure goal-reaching RL sit at opposite ends of a trade-off. Tracking a single clip (Li et al., 2025; Mandi et al., 2025) yields human-like, dexterous motion but a policy that can only replay that clip. Goal-reaching with a sparse success reward, as in Dex4D (Kuang et al., 2026) 4and SimToolReal (Kedia et al., 2026), generalizes to arbitrary goals but has no prior on how to move and converges to unnatural, unreliable finger strategies, and it rarely discovers behaviors such as finger gaiting on its own. Hybrid Dexterous Mimicking, inspired by hybrid imitation for humanoid control (Wang et al., 2026b;c), obtains both by running the two settings as different environments 

that share one policy. Concretely, a fraction p of the N parallel environments are Tracking Environments (TrackEnv) and the remaining 1 − p are Generalization Environments (GenEnv). The two differ only in their initial-state distribution, their goal distribution, and their reward; the observation space, the action space, and the actor πθ are identical. Because the goal gt is the only channel through which task information reaches the actor, behaviors learned by tracking ˆτ in TrackEnvs become directly addressable by novel goals in GenEnvs. The joint objective is

Adaptive sampling and termination. Initial reference frames t0 are grouped into B bins along the motion. We maintain an exponential moving average of the tracking success sb of each bin and perform reference state initialization on bins with probability P (b) ∝ σ(sb) + ϵ, where σ(·) is the success variance, so that both solved and hopeless bins receive little probability and the frontier receives most, facilitating learning harder initial states. We embed our Hybrid Dexterous Mimicking recipe in between real-to-sim and sim-to-real, con-verting everyday videos of human manipulation to physically grounded robot execution. 

Real2Sim: from video to HOI reference. When ˆτ is not available from a mocap dataset, we reconstruct it from a few seconds of RGB(D) video, recorded in the lab or in the wild (Fig. 7). We obtain a watertight object mesh O, per-frame object poses ˆxot , and per-frame MANO hand parame-ters ˆqht and wrist poses ˆxwt . These independent estimates are then aligned and optimized post hoc, including depth-based hand motion alignment, gravity axis estimation, and table plane alignment. The output is a HOI reference that is consistent and smooth enough to be tracked in simulation. Mo-cap datasets plug into the pipeline directly at this output. Through a series of careful design choices, our reconstruction pipeline outperforms existing HOI reconstruction baselines (Paliwal et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a) in both reconstruction accuracy and runtime efficiency, as shown in Table 3 and Fig. 8. More details can be found in §B. 

Sim: retargeting and Hybrid Dexterous Mimicking. The HOI reference is kinematically retar-geted to specific hand embodiments to obtain initial wrist and finger configurations for reference state initialization (more details in §D). In this work we present ADA DEX , a framework for learning adaptable dexterous skills from seconds of human motion. At the core of ADA DEX is Hybrid Dexterous Mimicking : a goal-conditioned reinforcement learning framework in which a single policy is trained simultaneously in tracking en-vironments, which supply dense supervision from the human reference, and generalization environ-ments, which pose noised, off-reference goals under a sparse object reward. Extensive experiments in simulation and the real world verify the effectiveness of our framework, demonstrating dexterous functional grasping, in-hand reorientation, and dynamic behaviors. We further showcase our poli-cies performing various downstream tasks with different embodiments, highlighting our recipe’s generality and adaptability. Finally, we show transfer across simulators and to the real world. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

B.2.1 EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

Evaluation Setup. We evaluate on DexYCB and HO3D dataset. For DexYCB, we evaluate on the 

s0 test subset, using only camera sequence 836212060125 , downsampling the sequences to 10 FPS. For HO3D, we evaluate on the official evaluation set, using HO3D v3 version, downsampling to 6 FPS. Our method and Do-As-I-Do require the user to specify the target object; Do-As-I-Do additionally requires a reference frame to be selected. We therefore evaluate all methods under a common ini-tialization protocol: the target object is specified by its ground-truth mask in frame 0, subsequent masks are propagated using SAM 2, and frame 0 is used as the object reconstruction reference. (For EgoInfinity, this replaces its automatic object identification and reconstruction-frame selection.) To ensure sufficient visibility for first-frame object reconstruction and subsequent tracking, we retain only sequences where at least 70% of the target object’s rendered silhouette is visible in the first frame. This retains only 135 sequences in DexYCB s0 test , while all sequences are kept in HO3D. For Do-As-I-Do, we use the middle frame of each sampled clip as the preferred alignment reference frame and, if it is invalid, select the nearest valid frame. This avoids fixing the reference to frame 0, where the hand is often absent.

We use non-squared bidirectional Chamfer distance with up to 3,000 sampled vertices per mesh. MPJPE is reported in millimeters (mm), while CD h is reported in centimeters (cm). Sequence-level success is the percentage of evaluation sequences for which a method produces a valid final reconstruction: Do-As-I-Do may fail to produce a final result when it cannot find a valid reference frame for hand–object alignment. Frame-level success is the percentage of ground-truth-valid frames with a valid predicted reconstruction, taking frames from failed sequences into account. A frame may fail when, for example, the hand is not detected or the method produces no valid reconstruction for that frame. We additionally report runtime measured by the average seconds needed to reconstruct a single frame.

## C IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

C.1 STATE AND ACTION SPACE 

As mentioned in 3, our state st includes proprioception roprioception qt, robot wrist pose xwt ∈

SE(3) and its linear and angular velocity ˙xwt , object pose xot ∈ SE(3) , and a fixed-size shape code given by Basis Point Set (BPS) distances to the object mesh (Prokudin et al., 2019). There is no privileged information in st.For critic observation, besides the state above, it also has privileged information including hand joint velocities ˙qt, object velocity, fingertip contact forces, object COM, and object weight. For TrackEnvs, the critic also gets the HOI reference ˆτ , which is zero masked out in GenEnvs. Regarding action space, for the floating Arti-Mano setting, the action at consists of 6-DoF delta wrist command and 22-DoF absolute finger commmand. We first apply a exponential moving average (EMA) filter with smoothing factor α = 0 .4 to the raw action, and then scale the action to joint ranges. For the arm, the scaled action is interpreted as delta wrist pose ∆xwt with respect to current wrist pose wwt and converted into 6D force/torque by a PD controller, which is then applied to the floating hand base. For the finger, the scaled action is the absolute finger joint targets for the PD controller. For physical xArm6+XHand training, instead of outputing 6-DoF delta wrist command, the first 6-DoF of at is the delta joint angles for the 6 xArm6 joints, which is filtered and scaled to generate PD targets for the xArm6 PD controller. The hand action remains the same. C.2 NETWORK ARCHITECTURE AND TRAINING DETAILS 

We train the policy using PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) with asymmetric actor-critic. We initial-ize both actor and critic networks as MLP with hidden layers 1024 , 1024 , 512  with ELU activa-tions (Clevert et al., 2015). The policy action is sampled from a Gaussian distribution with learnable action std. Our training is divided to two stages for a curriculum purpose. In stage I all parallel environments are TrackEnvs to give a good human prior for the policy, where in stage II we initialize from stage I actor checkpoint, and train under 50/50 TrackEnv and GenEnv to provide the policy with stage/goal coverage and generalization. The policy is trained using Isaac Gym (Makoviychuk et al., 2021) with 8,192 parallel environments per GPU. Each stage is trained till convergence, with a 20k-iteration hard cap. It takes around 1-2 days for each stage to converge on a single GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. More training details and hyperparameters are reported in Table 4. C.3 REWARD SHAPING

## D KINEMATIC MOTION RETARGETING 

Inspired by Yang et al. (2025); Feng et al. (2026), we adopt a interaction mesh-based kinematic retargeting method to turn the MANO (Romero et al., 2022) parameters into embodiment-specific joint positions and wrist pose. Note that the retargeted joint positions are only used for reference state initialization. The tracking reward, termination, etc., all depend on raw MANO links from the HOI reference ˆτ , where each embodiment maintains a one-to-one MANO-to-robot mapping based on its kinematic structure. 

## E BENCHMARK MOTIONS 

Our benchmark motions are reported in Table 6. All motions are in 60 Hz. 18 F BASELINE DETAILS 

In this work, we compare our method with representative baselines from motion tracking and general goal reaching methodologies. We compare with ManipTrans (Li et al., 2025) for motion tracking baseline and SimToolReal (Kedia et al., 2026) for goal reaching baseline. We also compare with two ablation baselines w/o TrackEnv and w/o GenEnv to show necessity and synergy between the two sets of environments. For ManipTrans, we utilize their pretrained first-stage imitator checkpoint and learn a residual policy for each trajectory, aligned with our observation and action space. For SimToolReal, we use their original Kuka+Sharpa embodiment, and finetune their pretrained checkpoint to train object-specific policies for each object. Note that due to different interfaces of the baselines and our method, for tracking seen and similar motions, we only give object goal poses for SimToolReal, while for goal reaching, we give ManipTrans hand references from contact frames where candidate frames are restricted to frames at/after the handoff where at least two fingertips are within 4 cm of the object. For each trajectory, the candidate with the lowest deterministic contact cost is selected. Therefore, the random goal gets a plausible, source-recorded grasp configuration that is transported with the object. 

## G DOWNSTREAM TASK SPECIFICATIONS 

In total, we have five downstream tasks: Hammer , MugPour , Marker , Sweep , and PanToss . Their task specifications are: • Hammer : grasp the hammer on the table and hammer two nails of distinct locations. • MugPour : grasp the mug by the handle, and pour the cube inside to a container on the table. • Marker : in-hand spin the marker, and draw ADA DEX on a whiteboard. • Sweep : use the sweep to sweep cubes on the table to a dustbin. • PanToss : grasp the handle of the pan, and toss the egg around. We also demonstrate other robot hands (e.g., Sharpa Hand, XHand, Wuji Hand v1) to show that our recipe can be transferred to real robot embodiments and make them do actual work rather than animation purposes only. 

## H LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

Our method currently learns one policy per object and per reference trajectory. Scaling to a single policy that covers many objects and motions is a natural next step, and would also enable skill com-position across tasks. In addition, we represent object state as a 6-DoF pose plus a BPS shape code, chosen as a minimal recipe.
