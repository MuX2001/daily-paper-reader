Recent advances in visuospatial understanding and agentic reasoning open the possibility of using general-purpose multimodal models for direct robotic manipulation without specialized action policies; however, their embodied capabilities and limitations remain poorly understood. This paper presents a systematic evaluation of GPT-6 Astra for closed-loop robotic manipulation, built on a lightweight agent execution framework. We first probe three fundamental embodied capabilities—depth perception, embodied in-context learning, and tactile understanding—and demonstrate that the model can exploit depth information, acquire simple manipulation strategies from contextual demonstrations, and use tactile feedback to correct its actions. We then evaluate GPT-6 Astra across five established manipulation benchmarks along six capability dimensions. Despite competitive performance on several benchmarks—26.92% success on RoboDojo and 76.0% on RoboTwin 2.0—our analysis reveals a pronounced gap between semantic reasoning and physical execution, particularly in consequence-aware action planning, precision, and complex motion coordination. To further examine these limitations, we introduce LIBERO-GeoMotion, a new benchmark of geometry-constrained tasks that require precise pose alignment, coordinated translation and rotation, and anticipation of physical action consequences. GPT-6 Astra achieves only 11.1% zero-shot success, exposing the difficulty of translating spatial understanding into physically feasible motion. Real-world experiments corroborate these findings: zero-shot execution is feasible for simple tasks, yet persistent deficits remain in motion feasibility, precise control, and physical safety, while substantial inference latency further hinders practical deployment. Overall, our results characterize the emerging embodied capabilities of general-purpose multimodal models and delineate the key challenges toward safe, efficient, and reliable robotic manipulation.

## 1 Introduction 

Robot manipulation requires a tightly coupled set of capabilities: understanding language and visual scenes, reasoning about spatial relationships, generating physically grounded actions, and adapting to environmental feedback [2, 52]. Although general-purpose LLMs and VLMs excel at semantic understanding and high-level planning [1, 16], their limited spatial grounding [9] and closed-loop control preclude reliable action genera-tion [14, 25, 32, 35]. LLM/VLM-based coding agents have likewise been explored for robotic manipulation [35]; however, they specify only high-level action logic, whereas precise manipulation points and target poses are still delegated to hand-engineered geometric computations and control primitives, which limits their flexibility. In contrast, specialized action models, such as Vision-Language-Action (VLA) [3–6, 20, 30, 55, 57] models and World Action Models (WAMs) [7, 23, 51], directly generate actions, but remain constrained by their embodied training distributions [56], which limits their generalization to unseen tasks and environments [17]. 1arXiv:2610.28259v1 [cs.RO] 20 Oct 2026 (a) End -to -end closed -loop control

Agentic hybrid architectures [46, 48, 63] aim to combine the strengths of both families, using general models for planning and specialized action models for execution. However, these systems still rely on post-trained action policies, even for relatively simple manipulation tasks, and therefore inherit the same generalization and flexibility bottlenecks. Recently, GPT-6 Astra [45] has emerged as a promising candidate, with strong visuospatial understanding, 3D reasoning, and agentic reasoning. These capabilities suggest that a general-purpose model could directly generate executable actions through spatial reasoning and sustain closed-loop control through agentic reason-2ing—without the hand-engineered primitives or post-trained action policies required by existing hierarchical architectures. Preliminary robotics studies [13, 37, 50, 62] offer initial evidence for this potential, reporting promising manipulation performance without specialized VLA or WAM policies. Yet the boundaries of GPT-6 Astra’s embodied capabilities remain insufficiently characterized. Existing studies focus on benchmark performance and selected failure cases, without establishing which capabilities the model executes reliably and where its limitations emerge across diverse physical demands; in particular, real-world evaluations have not systematically probed precise geometric constraints, complex motion coordination, or safety-critical inter-actions. These gaps raise a fundamental question: What can GPT-6 Astra reliably achieve in manipulation, and where do its capabilities break down, particularly in the real world? 

To answer this question, we conduct a comprehensive, capability-driven investigation of GPT-6 Astra spanning more than 2,000 trials across simulation and real-world environments. We first introduce GPT-Generalist End-effector Action Reasoning ( GPT-GEAR ), an agent execution framework that equips GPT-6 Astra with minimally engineered general-purpose tools for direct closed-loop robotic manipulation (Figure 1 (a)). Using GPT-GEAR, we isolate three capabilities that underlie successful manipulation—depth perception [24, 26], embodied in-context learning [13, 37], and tactile understanding [8, 18, 33, 39] (Figure 1 (b))—and show that the model can estimate and exploit depth, acquire simple motor skills from in-context demonstrations, and use tactile feedback to correct its actions during execution. We then evaluate GPT-6 Astra on five established benchmarks—LIBERO-Plus [17], RoboTwin 2.0 [10], RoboDojo [11], RoboCasa GR1 [3, 43], and UniVTAC [8] (Figure 1 (c))—providing a broad empirical baseline for general-purpose models in robotic manipulation. Characterizing performance along six capability dimensions reveals an uneven profile: without task-specific policy training, GPT-6 Astra exhibits strong task understanding, promising generalization, and superior memory and attains competitive results on several benchmarks, yet these strengths do not consistently translate into reliable physical execution—particularly in long-horizon manipulation, precision-sensitive tasks, and complex motion coordination—while substantial inference overhead further underscores the gap between general-purpose reasoning and efficient robotic control [31]. Geometry-constrained manipulation exposes this gap most clearly, as it requires precise pose control, co-ordinated translation and rotation, and anticipation of physical interactions. To probe these demands, we introduce LIBERO-GeoMotion , a targeted benchmark built on LIBERO [36] assets that emphasizes complex motion execution while minimizing semantic complexity (Figure 1 (d)). On LIBERO-GeoMotion, GPT-6 Astra exhibits substantial difficulties in satisfying geometric and sequential constraints, exposing a critical disconnect between understanding desired spatial configurations and generating physically feasible action sequences. We finally extend the evaluation to real-world zero-shot deployment: GPT-6 Astra reliably completes basic pick-and-place tasks, but its overall zero-shot success rate reaches only 40%, with some constrained tasks failing entirely, and persistent failures involving unsafe grasp configurations, motion feasibility, and precise alignment reveal substantial challenges in safety-aware planning and reliable physical execution. General-purpose models thus already exhibit meaningful zero-shot manipulation capabilities, but remain far from delivering consistently safe and reliable robotic control. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We introduce GPT-GEAR , a lightweight agent execution framework that equips GPT-6 Astra with minimally engineered general-purpose tools, enabling direct closed-loop robotic manipulation with no specialized action policies .• We systematically investigate three fundamental embodied capabilities—depth perception, embodied in-context learning, and tactile understanding—revealing how diverse forms of embodied information support action generation and execution. • We conduct comprehensive evaluations across five established manipulation benchmarks and characterize performance along six capability dimensions, establishing a broad empirical baseline and identifying the strengths and limitations of general-purpose models in robotic manipulation. • We introduce LIBERO-GeoMotion , a targeted benchmark for geometry-constrained manipulation that probes precise pose control, coordinated rotational and translational motion, and consequence-aware action planning, revealing critical limitations that conventional pick-and-place benchmarks cannot reveal. • We validate GPT-6 Astra on real robotic hardware, demonstrating its potential for zero-shot execution 3of simple manipulation tasks while identifying critical limitations in safety-aware planning, motion feasibility, and reliable physical control. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 LLM and VLM-Based system 

Large Language Models (LLMs) [1, 25, 35] and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) [12, 16, 24, 26, 34, 38, 40, 41, 58, 61] support robotic manipulation through semantic reasoning and task planning. SayCan [1] grounds language-based skill selection in learned affordances, while Inner Monologue [25] incorporates environmental feedback into language-based planning. PaLM-E [16] integrates visual and state observations for embodied reasoning, and Code as Policies [35] generates executable programs over perception and control APIs. VoxPoser [24] and ReKep [26] further ground model outputs through 3D value maps and relational keypoint constraints, respectively. More recently, Guava [38] enables iterative interaction through semantic manipulation tools, while Show-Harness [12] translates fine-grained semantic action units into robot movements, supporting closed-loop execution without a separate VLA policy. Building on these studies, we introduce an agent execution framework with minimally engineered, general-purpose interaction tools for closed-loop control. This framework supports a comprehensive evaluation of the general model’s native manipulation capabilities, revealing its potential and limitations across diverse tasks and environments. 

2.2 VLA and WAM-based system 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [4–6, 30, 47] connect visual observations and language instructions to robot actions through embodied training. RT-1 [6] demonstrates scalable language-conditioned visuomotor learning, while RT-2 [5] transfers knowledge from vision-language pretraining to robotic control by representing actions as tokens. OpenVLA [30] provides an open-source VLA trained on diverse robot demonstrations, and 

π0 [4] combines a pretrained VLM with flow matching for continuous action generation. π0.5 [47] further improves open-world generalization through co-training on heterogeneous robot and non-robot data. These advances demonstrate that learned action models can inherit semantic knowledge while acquiring increasingly diverse manipulation skills. World Action Models (WAMs) [7, 51] further connect action generation with predictive modeling of the environment. WorldVLA [7] jointly models actions and future images within an autoregressive framework, enabling action learning and visual prediction to support each other. More recently, OpenWAM [51] system-atically studies how video-generative priors, world–action information exchange, and embodied pretraining contribute to manipulation and generalization. Despite their different architectures, both VLAs and WAMs acquire action-generation capabilities through embodied training, and their transfer to unfamiliar settings remains dependent on data coverage and learned control representations. Our work evaluates the native manipulation capabilities of a general-purpose model through closed-loop interaction with general-purpose tools, without relying on a separately trained VLA or WAM. 

2.3 Hybrid Architectures 

Hybrid architectures [46, 48, 63] combine the semantic reasoning of general LLMs or VLMs with the execution capabilities of learned action models. Hi Robot [48] uses a high-level VLM to interpret complex instructions and user feedback, and a low-level VLA to execute the resulting commands. Cortex [46] further aligns high-level planning with low-level VLA execution through a structured subtask interface and execution-aware training. Harness VLA [63] exposes a frozen VLA as a callable primitive for contact-rich manipulation and combines it with analytic primitives and memory-guided planning. These systems illustrate how reasoning and learned control can complement each other, including settings where the action model remains frozen during deployment. However, their performance reflects the joint contributions of the reasoning model, action policy and interface, making it difficult to attribute successful manipulation to the general model alone. 2.4 GPT-6 Astra Driven Policy 

Recent technical reports [13, 21, 37, 50, 62] explore GPT-6 Astra as a closed-loop manipulation policy. GPT-as-Policy [50] compares direct control with VLA-assisted execution, while Zhang et al. [62] evaluate Astra on RoboDojo, identifying semantic strengths and limitations in precise control. GPT-Policy [13] investigates adaptation from demonstrations and interaction feedback, while RoboICL [37] combines demonstration context with persistent interaction memory to support in-context robot learning. RoboDawn [21] further explores control through a discrete action interface guided by in-context demonstrations. These studies demonstrate Astra’s embodied potential, but their emphasis on particular benchmarks or settings provides only a partial account of its manipulation capabilities and boundaries. Furthermore, we provide a more comprehensive capability-level evaluation by jointly examining depth perception, embodied in-context learning and tactile understanding within a common execution framework.

As shown in Figure 2, GPT-Generalist End-effector Action Reasoning (GPT-GEAR) separates model-level decisions from low-level execution [35]. GPT-6 Astra interprets observations and generates commands, while the tool interface provides environmental information and executes the specified actions. Each tool call and its result zt are appended to the interaction history:

3.3 Closed-loop Execution 

We organize five complementary tools into a closed-loop workflow, as illustrated in Figure 2. Observe retrieves current or historical observations [22] and robot states; Reference supplies optional demonstration context; and Act executes motion and gripper commands, returning updated observations and execution feedback. 

Status checks the environment’s task-completion predicate, while Finish terminates the interaction. Specifically, Algorithm 1 summarizes the workflow. Starting with the initial Observe and a Reference call, the model uses feedback from each action to continue execution, retrieve historical evidence or verify completion. 6Successful environment verification leads to Finish ; otherwise, execution resumes until completion or budget exhaustion.

3.5 Embodied In-context Reference 

Reference supplies an optional, prevalidated demonstration package to support embodied in-context learn-ing [13, 37]. The package may contain video keyframes, textual step descriptions, action trajectories, or a combination of these modalities. It is provided after the initial observation and before the first Act call, and incorporated into the interaction history Ht. Let R denote the demonstration context. We make its role explicit in the tool-selection policy: 

u(a)t ∼ πθ (· | H t, ℓ, R ) , (5) where R is written separately to highlight the demonstration context already included in Ht. It provides guidance on manipulation order and contact strategies, while the model determines motion parameters from current observations and execution feedback. This interface enables us to evaluate how demonstration context supports action generation and closed-loop adaptation. 

3.6 Action Execution Interface 

Act converts a model decision into a bounded, synchronous control command. In the dual-arm interface, both arms are specified explicitly. For arm k ∈ { L, R }, the action arguments at contain 

akt =  ∆pkt , ∆qkt , g kt , M kt

 , (6) where ∆pkt ∈ R3 is a translation in meters in the controller’s reference frame, ∆qkt is a unit quaternion specifying a rotation increment in that frame, gkt ∈ [0 , 1] is the gripper target, and M kt is the arm’s independent limit on solver steps. Let (pkt, start , q kt, start ) denote the measured end-effector pose in the same frame at the start of the call. The target pose is 

pkt, goal = pkt, start + ∆ pkt , qkt, goal = ∆ qkt ⊗ qkt, start , (7) where ⊗ denotes quaternion composition, with the increment applied by left multiplication. The controller executes these targets through bounded motion increments, synchronizing the two arms while allowing each to have an independent execution limit. An arm that reaches its target maintains that target as the other continues. Each synchronized step is checked against kinematic and joint constraints and rejected for both arms if either command is invalid. After execution, the tool returns updated observations and feedback rt as part of zt. Motion completion and residual pose errors provide evidence for the model to assess the attempted action and revise subsequent commands. This feedback connects model-generated geometric decisions to their physical outcomes, enabling closed-loop correction. Task-dependent motion planning remains the model’s responsibility; the controller’s feasibility checks do not guarantee collision-free motion or successful grasping. 74 Experiments 

We evaluate GPT-6 Astra on robot manipulation along decomposed complementary dimensions. Our systematic experiments cover four aspects: depth perception, embodied in-context learning, tactile understanding, and integrated manipulation performance. For depth perception, we test whether the model performs metric reasoning about 3D space or only relies on 2D visual cues. For embodied in-context learning, we examine whether additional demonstrations transfer task intent or support fine motor skills. For tactile understanding, we determine whether the model interprets tactile signals and adjusts force-dependent actions. Finally, we evaluate integrated manipulation performance for comparison, which probes the capability ceiling in complex and diverse scenes. 

4.1 Datasets  

> LIBERO-Plus

[17] extends LIBERO [36] with a large-scale robustness evaluation suite for vision-language-action models. It contains 10,030 perturbed tasks based on LIBERO assets spanning 7 dimensions: object layout, camera viewpoint, robot initial state, language instruction, lighting, background texture, and sensor noise.  

> RoboDojo

[11] is a unified simulation-and-real benchmark for generalist manipulation policies. It includes 42 simulation tasks and 18 real-world tasks. The simulation suite evaluates generalization, memory, precision, long-horizon execution, and open-vocabulary instruction following, while the real-world suite tests deployment under physical interaction and standardized hardware protocols.  

> RoboTwin 2.0

[10] is a bimanual manipulation benchmark built with generative digital twins. It provides 50 bimanual tasks with strong domain randomization and supports scalable expert-data generation. The benchmark emphasizes dual-arm coordination, object interaction, and robustness to scene and embodiment variation.  

> UniVTAC

[8] is a simulation and benchmarking platform for visuo-tactile manipulation. It supports multiple tactile sensor types and provides scalable generation of paired visual and tactile interaction data. Its benchmark contains 8 tasks covering pose reasoning, shape perception, and contact-rich insertion.  

> RoboCasa GR1

[3] is a tabletop manipulation benchmark built on the RoboCasa simulation framework [43]. It comprises 24 tasks performed by a Fourier GR-1 humanoid robot equipped with dexterous hands, covering object placement and interactions with household fixtures. 

4.2 Implementation Details 

GPT-6 Astra is the only planning component for evaluation. No other robot-specific models are adopted. All experiments are conducted through Codex with the reasoning effort set to high. Each Act call specifies a relative pose target that the controller tracks over multiple native control steps. The translation and rotation increments are limited to 0.01 m and 0.05 rad per step, respectively. The requested displacement norm and shortest rotation angle are bounded by 0.20 m and π/ 2 rad, respectively. By default, each Act call permits up to 10 native control steps.

Dense Depth Improves Success and Efficiency. Providing dense ground-truth depth maps alongside RGB observations achieves the highest success rate (67.5%) and the fewest average action rounds (24.23) among the tested configurations, with only a modest increase in token usage over RGB alone. Figure 3 illustrates this benefit through the task open the middle drawer of the cabinet , where the model must locate and approach the drawer handle. Without additional depth cues, the model commands an excessively large movement toward the handle. The arm collides with the cabinet corner, leaving the wrist misaligned. In contrast, access to scene depth helps the model select a suitable approach distance and reach the handle accurately for grasping. This example suggests that depth information supports more accurate approach movements and can reduce the need for corrective actions, consistent with the lower average action rounds observed across the evaluated tasks. 

Point Queries Underperform Dense Depth Maps. Compared with the depth-map setting, the back-projection tool yields a lower success rate of 60.0% and higher average token usage of 1.38M per episode. This is 31.6% higher than the RGB-only baseline and 25.1% higher than the depth-map setting. These results suggest that accurate point coordinates alone are less helpful than dense depth maps in the tested settings. A depth map shows the depth of surrounding surfaces across the image, whereas each tool query returns only the position of a selected point. The model must still choose useful points and relate their coordinates to the objects and available space in the scene. This may require additional queries and reasoning, helping explain the higher token usage.

Overall, the depth ablations suggest that GPT-6 Astra can use camera parameters to improve manipulation, while explicitly estimating depth provides a modest additional gain. Camera parameters help the model relate visual observations to the robot workspace, and ground-truth depth maps further improve success and reduce action rounds. Dense depth maps also outperform the back-projection tool in the tested settings, suggesting that how depth information is provided matters. 4.5 Main Results 

Evaluation setting. Following the capability-level analyses, we evaluate GPT-6 Astra on complete manipulation tasks across five benchmarks to examine how its embodied capabilities jointly support closed-loop execution. For consistency with subsequent real-world experiments in section 7, we provide only RGB observations and camera parameters for depth perception, requiring the model to explicitly estimate target object depths before acting. For embodied in-context learning, we provide demonstrations combining sampled video frames, textual step descriptions, and action trajectories. Tactile feedback and ground-truth depth map are additionally provided only on UniVTAC. These capabilities are integrated within our proposed agent execution framework as shown in Figure 2. We evaluate full tasks in each of the five benchmarks, covering 164 tasks in total: 40 base tasks from LIBERO-Plus, 50 from RoboTwin 2.0, 42 from RoboDojo, 24 from RoboCasa GR1, and 8 from UniVTAC. Given the computational cost of closed-loop evaluation, we use a reduced set of trials rather than the full official 15 evaluation protocols. Each task is evaluated with 20 trials on RoboCasa GR1 and UniVTAC, and 10 trials on the other three benchmarks. To assess the consistency of this protocol, we reevaluate a subset of selected existing methods. For these methods, the resulting success rates remain close to their officially reported values, which suggests that performance estimates are reasonably stable despite the reduced evaluation budget. Figure 8 compares GPT-6 Astra with existing methods, using our reevaluated results for a subset of baselines and officially reported results for the others. These comparisons provide an empirical reference for GPT-6 Astra’s integrated manipulation capabilities across the evaluated benchmarks.  

> Competitive integrated performance.

On LIBERO-Plus shown in Figure 8 (a), GPT-6 Astra achieves an 83.50% success rate, showing competitive performance relative to the baselines included in our comparison. This is close to the officially reported 83.90% of π0.5 [47]. Our reevaluated starVLA-OFT and starVLA-π [49] achieve slightly higher success rates of 84.75% and 85.50%, respectively. LIBERO-Plus introduces variations in scene layout, camera viewpoint, language, and other observation conditions. This result suggests that GPT-6 Astra can adapt task interpretation and spatial grounding to varied inputs within a closed loop. On RoboTwin 2.0 shown in Figure 8 (b), GPT-6 Astra reaches 76.00%, the highest success rate among the methods shown. It exceeds our reevaluated OpenWAM-α [51], which ranks second in this comparison, by 9.20 percentage points. The benchmark emphasizes bimanual manipulation under scene variation. This result suggests that GPT-6 Astra can translate task understanding into coordinated actions across two arms, extending its utility beyond single-arm manipulation. On UniVTAC shown in Figure 8 (c), GPT-6 Astra achieves 72.50%, markedly outperforming the reported 62.90% of FTP-1 [59] and 56.10% of starVLA. The absolute gains are 9.60 and 16.40 percentage points, respectively. This benchmark includes pose reasoning, shape perception, and contact-rich insertion. Together with our tactile analysis, this result suggests that GPT-6 Astra can use visual and contact evidence to guide interaction when visual localization alone is insufficient. On RoboDojo shown in Figure 8 (e), GPT-6 Astra obtains a 26.92% success rate and a score of 35.17. Both are the highest values in the comparison, exceeding the reported 19.34% and 24.90 of DM0.5 [15]. RoboDojo spans generalization, memory, precision, long-horizon execution, and open-vocabulary instruction following. Its favorable aggregate performance suggests that GPT-6 Astra’s capabilities remain useful across a diverse set of manipulation demands. The score further indicates meaningful intermediate progress, even when tasks are not completed. Despite its favorable relative performance, both absolute metrics remain low. GPT-6 Astra therefore has substantial room for improvement on this highly challenging benchmark.  

> Limits of cross-benchmark transfer.

The excellent results above do not extend uniformly to RoboCasa GR1 shown in Figure 8 (d). GPT-6 Astra achieves only 18.96% success on this benchmark. It exceeds the reported 13.60% of π0 [4], but falls well below the reported 37.00% of π0.5. The gap also persists against our reevaluated starVLA-OFT and reported OpenWAM-α, which reach 57.29% and 60.50%, respectively. The other evaluated settings use grippers, whereas RoboCasa GR1 uses dexterous hands. These hands have substantially more degrees of freedom, making manipulation a higher-dimensional reasoning problem. The model must account for hand pose, finger configuration, and multiple contact relationships to form and maintain stable grasps. This places greater demands on GPT-6 Astra’s physical and spatial understanding, as well as its ability to translate that understanding into coordinated control. Policies trained on demonstrations from this embodiment can acquire corresponding motor priors, while GPT-6 Astra must rely on general reasoning and online feedback. We hypothesize that these additional demands contribute to its weaker performance, although aggregate results alone cannot establish the cause. Overall, GPT-6 Astra demonstrates strong integrated manipulation capabilities across several benchmarks, with particularly notable zero-shot transfer. The compared policies learn manipulation from robot data within their respective training distributions before evaluation. In contrast, GPT-6 Astra operates without benchmark-specific policy training or parameter updates. Its competitive performance under zero-shot setting highlights the potential of general-purpose models for embodied control. This strength coexists with clear limitations. Success rate remains low on RoboDojo, and performance falls substantially behind trained policies on RoboCasa GR1. Although general reasoning provides a strong basis for manipulation, it doesn’t ensure reliable embodiment-specific motor control. ## 6 LIBERO-GeoMotion 

Our systematic analysis reveals a recurring gap between GPT-6 Astra’s spatial understanding and its ability to execute physically feasible manipulation motions. While the model performs well on straightforward pick-and-place tasks, it struggles with precise pose control, coordinated translation and rotation, and anticipating the physical consequences of actions. To further investigate these limitations, we introduce LIBERO-GeoMotion, a targeted benchmark designed to evaluate geometry-constrained manipulation while minimizing semantic complexity.

These findings suggest that GPT-6 Astra’s manipulation limitations extend beyond isolated errors in spatial perception or target-pose estimation. In particular, complex rotational control remains a major bottleneck when orientation changes must be coordinated with translation, contact, and subsequent actions. 7.4 Real-World Performance Analysis 

We further evaluate GPT-6 Astra on five real-world manipulation tasks under zero-shot and one-example in-context learning (ICL) settings, with five trials per task. Despite its promising performance on several simulation benchmarks, GPT-6 Astra achieves only 40.0% overall success in real-world zero-shot execution. While basic pick-and-place succeeds in all five trials, performance drops substantially on more demanding tasks. Block-in-cup insertion and pouring achieve only 40.0% success, shelf rearrangement reaches 20.0%, and constrained mango grasping fails in all five trials. These results indicate that although certain basic manipulation capabilities can be directly deployed without task-specific training, reliable execution remains challenging under more complex physical and geometric constraints. In contrast, providing a single contextual demonstration increases overall success from 40.0% to 80.0%, with improvements across all four challenging tasks. This substantial gain highlights the potential of embodied ICL to improve real-world manipulation without parameter updates. Nevertheless, the remaining failures reveal persistent limitations in motion planning, geometric precision, and physical interaction, which we examine below. 30 Rotation and Consequence-aware Motion Planning. The real-world experiments reinforce the limitations in rotational control and consequence-aware action planning identified in our systematic analysis and LIBERO-GeoMotion. In the block-in-cup task shown in Figure 18 (a), GPT-6 Astra can establish an appropriate grasp but subsequently rotates its wrist inward, reaching a joint limit that prevents the required placement. Although the initial grasp is successful, the selected wrist configuration does not preserve sufficient motion range for the next operation. This reveals a failure to anticipate how an immediate action constrains subsequent physical execution. A similar limitation appears in the mango-grasping task shown in Figure 18 (c), which extends the rotation-dependent grasping problem examined in LIBERO-GeoMotion to a more constrained physical environment. The mango’s major axis exceeds the gripper opening, requiring the model to select a suitable grasp orientation across its shorter axis. However, GPT-6 Astra sometimes fails to perform the necessary reorientation and instead attempts an infeasible grasp. In other attempts, it lowers the gripper before establishing a collision-free approach, causing the arm to encounter the nearby peach. A feasible strategy requires coordinating grasp orientation with an oblique approach that avoids the obstacle. These cases expose a common limitation: GPT-6 Astra can propose locally plausible actions but does not consistently anticipate their downstream kinematic and geometric consequences. The difficulty lies not merely in determining the desired object pose, but in planning a sequence of physically feasible motions that preserves the ability to complete subsequent operations.  

> Precision under Tight Geometric Constraints.

Precise manipulation remains another significant challenge in real-world execution. The block-in-cup task requires inserting a block into a closely fitting container, leaving little tolerance for positional and angular errors. As shown in the right-hand sequence of Figure 18 (a), even when the model successfully grasps and transports the block toward the opening, residual pose misalignment prevents successful insertion. This failure is consistent with the precision limitations observed in simulation. GPT-6 Astra can often identify the correct target and approximate the required motion, yet struggles to satisfy the fine-grained geometric constraints needed for physical engagement. Although ICL improves block-in-cup success from 40.0% to 80.0%, accurate pose alignment remains a challenge in the remaining unsuccessful executions.  

> Safety Limitations of Direct Pose-based Control.

Beyond task success, the experiments reveal potential safety concerns when directly translating model-generated end-effector pose increments into robot movements. In the shelf-rearrangement task shown in Figure 18 (d), GPT-6 Astra attempts to manipulate objects on the shelf but unintentionally knocks over the shelf during execution. This failure demonstrates that an action directed toward a semantically correct goal can still produce undesirable physical consequences through unintended contact with the environment. The joint-limit issue in block insertion and obstacle interference in mango grasping further illustrate the importance of motion feasibility. Although low-level controllers can enforce kinematic and joint-limit con-straints, these checks do not guarantee collision-free trajectories or safe object interactions. Directly generating end-effector pose changes without sufficiently considering the robot’s configuration, surrounding geometry, and future motion requirements therefore remains insufficient for reliable physical execution. While these cases do not constitute a comprehensive safety evaluation, they expose practical risks that must be addressed before deploying general-purpose models as autonomous robotic controllers.  

> Embodied ICL Enables Action-level Adaptation.

The real-world results further demonstrate that embodied ICL can improve not only semantic task understanding but also manipulation strategies and motor execution. Quantitatively, contextual demonstrations improve constrained mango grasping from 0.0% to 60.0%, block-in-cup insertion from 40.0% to 80.0%, pouring from 40.0% to 100.0%, and shelf rearrangement from 20.0% to 60.0%. These improvements are particularly notable because the tasks involve different physical requirements, including grasp orientation, precise alignment, object reorientation, and multi-step manipulation. The execution trajectories in Figure 18 provide further insight into these improvements. In the mango-grasping task (c), contextual demonstrations help the model select a more appropriate grasp orientation and approach direction, overcoming some of the geometric constraints that prevent successful zero-shot execution. Similarly, demonstrations provide useful rotation and grasping references for block-in-cup insertion, allowing the model to adopt more feasible manipulation motions. 31 The pouring task (b) provides particularly interesting evidence of action-level adaptation. Without demon-strations, GPT-6 Astra sometimes repeatedly attempts to grasp inaccessible objects inside the cup. With ICL, the model more consistently selects and executes the appropriate pouring strategy. Notably, one successful execution transfers two objects into the drawer in a single pour, although this specific operation was not demonstrated. This suggests that the model can adapt a learned manipulation strategy to a new physical configuration rather than simply reproducing the demonstrated action sequence. Together, the quantitative improvements and execution behaviors provide evidence that embodied ICL extends beyond semantic guidance to the adaptation of grasping strategies, rotation patterns, and manipulation procedures. However, the remaining failures, particularly in constrained grasping, precise insertion, and long-sequence execution, indicate that contextual demonstrations cannot fully compensate for limitations in physical planning and control. 

7.5 Discussion 

Overall, our real-world experiments further corroborate the capability boundaries identified in our systematic analysis and LIBERO-GeoMotion. Although GPT-6 Astra demonstrates the potential for zero-shot deployment on basic manipulation tasks, its limited overall success and recurring failures in constrained environments reveal substantial weaknesses in consequence-aware action planning, fine-grained precision, and continuous motion coordination. Unintended contacts, including knocking over surrounding objects and reaching joint limits, further expose safety concerns associated with directly generated robot actions. Meanwhile, the substantial improvements achieved through ICL reinforce our earlier finding that general-purpose models can acquire and adapt simple manipulation strategies from contextual demonstrations without additional training. Nevertheless, even with such adaptation, robust physical execution is not guaranteed. These findings highlight a fundamental distinction between the emerging ability of general-purpose models to perform robotic manipulation and the safety, efficiency, and reliability required for practical real-world deployment. 

## 8 Conclusion 

In this work, we presented a systematic evaluation of GPT-6 Astra for direct closed-loop robotic manipulation through GPT-GEAR, a lightweight agent framework that requires neither specialized action policies nor task-specific parameter updates. Spanning five established benchmarks, six capability dimensions, our new LIBERO-GeoMotion benchmark, and real-robot experiments, the study provides the community with a broad empirical baseline for general-purpose models as robotic controllers. GPT-6 Astra exhibits emerging embodied capabilities : it exploits depth information, adapts manipulation strategies from contextual demonstrations, and uses tactile feedback for corrective actions—capabilities whose effectiveness depends on how sensory evidence and demonstrations are presented, and which support competitive benchmark results. However, we identify a persistent gap between semantic reasoning and physical execution : precise alignment, coordinated motion, consequence-aware planning, and safety remain unreliable, and while embodied in-context learning enables adaptation of strategies and motions, contextual guidance does not fully resolve these deficits, with substantial inference and interaction overhead further impeding deployment. The strengths of general-purpose models in task understanding, generalization, and memory point toward a promising pathway for general-purpose robotic control; the remaining challenges in translating flexible reasoning into safe, efficient, and reliable action delineate where future advances are needed.
