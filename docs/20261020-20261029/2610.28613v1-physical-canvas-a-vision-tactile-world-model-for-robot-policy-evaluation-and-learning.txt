ABSTRACT
Evaluating and post-training robot policies through physical interaction is costly
and difficult to scale, motivating action-conditioned world models as interactive
simulators. However, visually plausible predictions can misrepresent contact dy-
namics, providing unreliable feedback for policy evaluation and learning. We
introduce PHYSCANVAS, a state-consistent visuo-tactile world model for contact-
rich robot manipulation. PHYSCANVAS unifies multi-view RGB, tactile images,
marker displacement, task state, and actions within a unified vision space. All
modalities share a video VAE and pretrained generative backbone, allowing op-
tical touch to reuse visual and temporal priors. Motion images derived through
forward kinematics and camera projection provide spatially aligned action con-
ditioning, while an initial RGB anchor and updated reference observations guide
sequential generation. Our consistency-aware post-training uses the frozen Scene
Reward Model, Modality Reward Model, and State Reward Model to supervise
scene preservation, visual–tactile compatibility, and agreement with real task out-
comes. The resulting world model supports closed-loop policy rollouts for eval-
uation and provides imagined interactions and rewards for GRPO policy updates.
Across four real-world manipulation tasks, when policies are rolled out in the
world model, State Reward Model supervision increases the arithmetic mean of
task-wise Pearson correlations between world-model and real-world success rates
from 0.602 to 0.885. Policy post-training in the reward-guided world model fur-
ther increases π0.5’s mean real-world success rate from a 59.1% SFT baseline to
70.8%. These results demonstrate the value of unified multimodal modeling and
consistency-aware post-training for robot policy evaluation and learning. Project
page: https://zebinx.github.io/PhysCanvas_HomePage/.

INTRODUCTION
Robot world models predict how environments evolve in response to actions, offering low-cost,
reproducible interactions for policy evaluation and learning (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Wu et al.,
2023; Wang et al., 2026). Contact-rich manipulation requires both visual observations of scene
dynamics and tactile feedback about contact changes (Yuan et al., 2017; Lambeta et al., 2020; Lee
et al., 2020; Guzey et al., 2024). A world model that captures these complementary signals and their
associated task outcomes could reduce the need for repeated physical trials.
Recent action-conditioned video models combine pretrained generative priors with action represen-
tations to support interactive prediction, policy evaluation, and learning in imagination (Wu et al.,
2026; Wang et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a). Existing visuo-tactile models, however, typically re-
quire a dedicated tactile encoder and a custom interface for injecting tactile features into a general
backbone (Zheng et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026). Adapting to new tactile data or sensor configura-
tions may therefore require retraining, limiting generality and complicating the pipeline. Meanwhile,
token-wise reconstruction supervision emphasizes local fidelity and can miss high-level semantics
such as task success. Reliable rollouts thus require visual observations, tactile feedback, and pre-
dicted task outcomes to describe the consequences of the same action; inconsistencies can mislead

predicting stable tactile contact or eventual success is semantically inconsistent despite visually
plausible frames. The challenge is to build a reusable multimodal model while preserving scene,
cross-modal, and task-outcome consistency for policy evaluation and learning.
We investigate two questions: (1) Can a simple, general mechanism encode heterogeneous
modalities in a shared space? (2) Can high-level semantics be injected into the world model
without additional networks?
To address this challenge, we introduce PHYSCANVAS, a world model with state consistency for
visual and tactile prediction, robot policy evaluation, and learning. PHYSCANVAS places multiple
modalities in a shared generative space and uses consistency supervision during post training to
preserve the interaction dynamics and task outcomes induced by actions, providing interactive,
coherent observations.
Specifically, PHYSCANVAS places multiview RGB, tactile images, marker displacement, and task
state on a unified canvas predicted by a shared video VAE and generative backbone, allowing optical
touch to reuse pretrained visual and temporal priors (Wan Team et al., 2025). Actions are encoded
as spatially aligned motion images through forward kinematics and camera projection (Wu et al.,
2026), while an initial RGB anchor and updated references guide sequential generation. During post
training, the frozen Scene Reward Model, Modality Reward Model, and State Reward Model enforce
scene preservation, visual and tactile compatibility, and agreement with real outcomes, including
both success and failure. The resulting model supports evaluation in a closed loop and supplies
imagined interactions and rewards for GRPO policy updates (Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2026).
We evaluate PHYSCANVAS on four real-world manipulation tasks: test-tube placement, bottle-cap
unscrewing, block relocation, and Rubik’s Cube manipulation (Figure 2). When policies are rolled
out in the world model, post-training improves the Pearson correlation between their world-model
and real-world success rates on every task, increasing the arithmetic mean of task-wise correlations
from 0.602 to 0.885. Each task includes nine evaluation batches spanning ACT, FM, and π0.5 (Zhao
et al., 2023; Lipman et al., 2023; Black et al., 2025); the batches are evaluated at different times,
and each task receives equal weight in the summary. Policy optimization through reward-guided
imagined interactions further improves the mean real-world success rate of π0.5 by 11.7 percentage
points over SFT, from 59.1% to 70.8% across the four tasks. These results demonstrate utility for
reliable evaluation and policy improvement.
Our contributions are threefold: (1) a unified canvas space that jointly models vision, touch, task
state, and actions using a shared video generation framework; (2) consistency-aware post-training
that constrains generated interactions through scene, modality, and task-state supervision; and (3) a
framework for policy evaluation and learning in imagination, evaluated on real robots.

RELATED WORK
2.1
ACTION-CONDITIONED WORLD MODELS FOR ROBOTICS
Action-conditioned video models have advanced robot planning and interactive prediction (Finn &
Levine, 2017; Yang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; 2023; Micheli et al., 2023; Bruce et al., 2024). Pre-
trained video priors (Ho et al., 2022; Blattmann et al., 2023; Wan Team et al., 2025) and embodied
action conditioning (Jiang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026) improve controllability, while unified archi-
tectures connect video prediction with policy modeling (Bi et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026). Extending
these visual simulators to contact-rich manipulation also requires predicting the tactile observations
on which policies act.
Imagined rollouts also support policy evaluation and learning (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner
et al., 2020; Hansen et al., 2024; Hafner et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026), mak-
ing reliable action consequences and task outcomes essential. Recent work studies long horizon
consistency and simulator validity (GigaWorld Team et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026b), or improves
generation through reward based optimization (Bardhan et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025). PHYSCAN-
VAS brings these requirements to joint visual and tactile prediction through scene and task state
consistency supervision, and evaluates generated rollouts against real policy outcomes.
2.2
VISUO-TACTILE WORLD MODELING
Touch exposes contact, deformation, and slip that can be ambiguous in RGB, motivating joint
visual and tactile prediction (Yuan et al., 2017; Lambeta et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2020; Guzey
et al., 2024). OmniVTA (Zheng et al., 2026) combines two stream dynamics with contact aware
action generation; FeelWorld (Ma et al., 2026) predicts contact and slip states through contact
gated attention.
DexTouch-WM (Qin et al., 2026) couples video and tactile experts, whereas
DexTacWAM (Yuan et al., 2026) integrates tactile latents specific to each finger into video diffusion.
TACO (Liu et al., 2026) uses generated visual and tactile corrections for policy post training.
Rather than add a separate tactile predictor, PHYSCANVAS renders tactile appearance and marker
displacement as regions of the same visual canvas as RGB and task status. The shared visual VAE
and video denoiser then model aligned sensory futures, with explicit supervision of visual and tactile
agreement.

3.3
CONSISTENCY-AWARE POST-TRAINING
Closed-loop simulation requires a reliable and consistent world model, beyond pixel-level similarity
to observed trajectories. Pixel token wise supervision is insufficient to enforce these requirements;
we therefore introduce reward-based post-training to improve scene, state, and modality consistency.
3.3.1
REWARD MODELS
We use a separate lightweight reward model for each consistency criterion. Given criterion-specific
input uq = hq(x0, xt+h), a ResNet-18 encoder (He et al., 2016) ϕq and decoder Dq produce a logit
and score:
Rq(uq) = Dq(ϕq(uq)) ,
ρq(uq) = σ(Rq(uq)) ,
(6)
where Dq may be an MLP or Transformer decoder and ρq ∈[0, 1]. Each model is trained beforehand
and remains frozen while supervising the generator.
Scene consistency.
The Scene Reward Model takes an episode-initial RGB reference and a future
RGB frame, assigning (1) to preserved scene identity and objects under valid motion and (0) to
unintended changes. For training, we construct a class-balanced dataset using ground-truth obser-
vations and scene-consistent world-model generations as positive examples, and scene-inconsistent
generations as negative examples. Each example is paired with its episode-initial RGB reference.
State consistency.
The State Reward Model takes a future RGB observation and predicts whether
the task’s success condition holds (1) or remains unsatisfied (0). Its judgments provide labels for
the canvas state region, whose color identifies the task and texture encodes completion. We train it
exclusively on real trajectories, which naturally contain abundant negative examples: frames before
task completion are labeled (0), while frames from completion onward are labeled (1).
Modality consistency.
The Modality Reward Model takes paired RGB, tactile-image, and marker-
displacement regions, assigning (1) when their contact evidence is compatible and (0) when it is
mismatched. Negative training pairs are constructed by keeping the RGB observation fixed and
replacing the paired tactile observations with a different tactile state, such as one with a different
contact texture. This supervises cross-modal agreement rather than the presence of contact.

3.4
POLICY EVALUATION AND POLICY POST-TRAINING
Policy evaluation.
PHYSCANVAS provides a multimodal environment for evaluating robot poli-
cies without executing every trial on hardware. Starting from a real initial observation, each policy
is rolled out in the world model in a closed loop: the policy consumes the generated observation
and issues the next action, and the world model returns its predicted visual, tactile, and task-state
consequences. Repeating this process produces imagined policy trajectories under controlled initial
conditions. We assess simulator reliability by comparing the success rates of these world-model
rollouts with those of the corresponding real-world policy executions under the same success crite-
ria.
Policy post-training.
Beyond evaluation, PHYSCANVAS supports policy post-training without
collecting additional robot trajectories. From real initial observations, a policy explores alterna-
tive behaviors through imagined closed-loop interactions, while the frozen State Reward Model
scores their task outcomes. Group-relative policy optimization (Shao et al., 2024) converts differ-
ences among these outcomes into learning signals for the policy. Throughout this process, the world
model and reward model remain fixed, so only the policy is updated from generated experience.

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate action-conditioned multimodal prediction, the effect of consistency-aware post-training,
and the use of imagined rollouts for policy evaluation and learning.
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Platform and data.
We use an ARX AC One dual-arm robot with three cameras and four finger-
tip Xense tactile sensors (Appendix A). Our study covers four tasks—test-tube placement, bottle-
cap unscrewing, block relocation, and Rubik’s Cube manipulation—spanning handover, constrained
contact, and object rotation.
Models and training.
We initialize PHYSCANVAS from Wan 2.2 and train the unified canvas on
mixed-task clips. Training uses four recursive chunks of eight future frames each. Unless otherwise
specified, models are trained for 300 epochs. Reward models use ResNet-18 encoders with criterion-
specific decoders. We evaluate ACT, FM, and π0.5 with visual and tactile inputs, and post-train FM
and π0.5.
Baselines and evaluation.
We compare with EVAC (Jiang et al., 2025), Open-Sora (Zheng et al.,
2024), and WoVR (Jiang et al., 2026). RGB prediction is evaluated with standard reconstruction and
distributional metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, FID, and FVD), VBench consistency scores (Subject,
Bg., and Smooth) (Huang et al., 2024), and EEF/object tracking errors. For touch, we report tactile-
image PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, Contact MAE, Onset/Offset, and Event F1, together with tactile-flow

4.5
PHYSCANVAS AS A LEARNING SIMULATOR FOR POLICY IMPROVEMENT
We next ask whether interaction with a visuo-tactile world model can improve policies on the
real robot. Starting from supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we optimize FM and π0.5 with GRPO in
PHYSCANVAS and evaluate the resulting policies on real tasks. In Table 4, RL in WM uses M1 as
the simulator, whereas RL in WM+RM uses the consistency-post-trained M4; both use the frozen
State Reward Model to score task outcomes. We omit ACT because it does not use the diffusion or
flow-matching formulation assumed by our GRPO implementation.
RL in M1 raises mean real-world success from 11.2% to 16.6% for FM and from 59.1% to 67.5%
for π0.5, averaging equally across the four tasks. Using M4 as the simulator further raises these
means to 19.1% and 70.8%. For FM, gains occur on Bottle and Blocks, while Test Tube and Ru-
bik’s Cube remain at zero. For π0.5, M4 yields its largest additional gains over M1 on Blocks and
Rubik’s Cube (6.6 and 10.0 percentage points); Bottle decreases from 90.0% to 86.6%. Thus, imag-
ined interactions in the consistency-post-trained world model improve average real-world success,
although the benefit varies by task.
5
CONCLUSION
We introduced PHYSCANVAS to study whether heterogeneous modalities can share a simple rep-
resentation and whether high level task semantics can be injected without an additional prediction
network. It places multiview RGB, tactile images, marker displacement, task state, and actions in
a unified vision space, while consistency supervision aligns scene, modality, and task outcomes.
Experiments across four real world tasks show that task state supervision improves the agreement
between world model rollouts and real policy outcomes, while reward guided imagined interactions
support policy improvement on real robots. These results demonstrate the value of unified multi-
modal representations for robot policy evaluation and learning.

BIMANUAL TASKS AND DATA
B.1
TASK DESIGN AND OBJECT SELECTION
All four tasks require coordinated use of both arms. We select objects that expose complementary
contact phenomena: reorientation of a slender object, separation of interlocking parts, threaded
rotation, and changing surface textures. Figure 11 pairs object photographs with robot rollouts from
our data and synchronized tactile observations.
Test Tube.
The left gripper picks up a test tube and passes it to the right gripper. The right gripper
then uses contact with the table to reorient the tube in-hand before inserting it into the rack. The
slender, transparent tube makes grasp stability and orientation difficult to infer from appearance
alone, motivating tactile feedback during handover and reorientation.
Blocks.
The left gripper lifts two connected blocks. Both grippers separate them and place each
block into the bag of the matching color. Interlocking studs and edges create localized contact
patterns, while separation produces a distinct change in contact that must agree with the visible
motion.
Bottle.
One gripper stabilizes the bottle while the other unscrews its cap; the cap and bottle are
then placed separately on the table. The ridged cap and compliant bottle provide different contact
geometries. This task couples rotational motion with sustained grasping and a final contact-release
event.
Rubik’s Cube.
The robot restores a Rubik’s Cube after a single face-turn perturbation. Its colored,
textured faces change the local contact pattern during rotation, making it a challenging test of visual–
tactile alignment. The restricted scramble isolates coordinated manipulation and sensory consistency
rather than general Rubik’s Cube solution planning.

C
WORLD MODEL TRAINING AND INFERENCE
C.1
WORLD-MODEL DATA COLLECTION
To expose the world model to a broad action distribution, we execute stochastic rollouts of task
policies on the real robot. The resulting data contain successful executions as well as naturally
occurring failures, including missed grasps, premature release, inaccurate handover, misalignment,
and incomplete insertion or placement. This diversity is important because a policy evaluator must
model not only expert demonstrations but also the off-nominal states visited by imperfect policies.
Figure 15 summarizes the data loop. Robot demonstrations are first converted to the LeRobot format
and used to train task policies. Stochastic policy execution then provides rollouts for world-model
learning. After collection, these rollouts undergo offline multimodal preprocessing: (1) numerical

robot actions are rendered as view-aligned action images; (2) camera images are cropped and re-
sized to the fixed world-model layout; and (3) each raw tactile record is separated into its tactile
image and tracked marker positions, from which marker displacement is computed. We time-align
these processed signals and assemble them into the unified canvas before world-model training.
The trained world model subsequently interacts with the policy in closed loop, providing generated
visual–tactile observations for either policy evaluation or RL-based policy improvement.

C.2
TRAINING CONFIGURATION
Initialization and action conditioning.
We initialize PHYSCANVAS from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, a
5-billion-parameter latent video flow-matching transformer pretrained for text/image-to-video gen-
eration. We retain its visual VAE and spatiotemporal transformer, freeze the VAE, and fine-tune the
transformer for multimodal future prediction. As described in Section 3.2, robot configurations are
rendered into view-aligned action images using camera calibration and the robot URDF. Their la-
tent tokens are fused position-wise with the noisy canvas tokens, converting the pretrained generator
into an action-conditioned world model without a dedicated action encoder beyond the shared video
VAE.
Optimization and compute.
Training uses 64 PPU-ZW810E accelerators (four nodes with 16
devices each) for 300 epochs and takes approximately three days. We use the 8-bit CAME optimizer
with a constant learning rate of 2−14.5 = 4.316 × 10−5 and weight decay 10−2. The per-device
batch size is 2 with one gradient-accumulation step, giving an effective batch size of 128 clips.
Training uses BF16, DeepSpeed ZeRO-2, and activation checkpointing of Wan transformer blocks.
No explicit gradient clipping is applied.

C.3
INFERENCE AND RECURSIVE ROLLOUT
At inference, the same episode-initial RGB anchor is fixed across all chunks. Each chunk starts
from Gaussian noise at σ = 1 and is denoised with 5 flow-matching steps to generate eight future
multimodal frames; its last frame updates r for the next call. In interactive evaluation, the policy
consumes the generated RGB and tactile observations and supplies the next action sequence; the
action is rendered into the aligned conditioning canvas before the following chunk is generated. We
repeat this process until the full episode horizon is reached, so rollout length is not limited to the
four chunks used during training.
D
POLICY ARCHITECTURES AND TACTILE INTEGRATION
We evaluate three policy families: Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) (Zhao et al., 2023),
a task-specific flow-matching policy (FM) (Lipman et al., 2023), and a tactile-augmented π0.5 pol-
icy (Black et al., 2025). All three receive three synchronized RGB views, the current bimanual
robot state, and four tactile pads, and output continuous dual-arm joint commands at 10 Hz. Be-
cause tactile-to-policy fusion is not the primary focus of this work, we deliberately adopt a simple
modality-concatenation strategy rather than designing a specialized fusion architecture. The three
policies nevertheless differ in their pretraining, action parameterization, and data sources. FM and
ACT are trained separately for each task from successful real-robot executions, whereas π0.5 shares
one multitask checkpoint across language instructions. We therefore use them as deployed policy
families rather than as a controlled architecture comparison.
D.1
COMMON OBSERVATION AND CONTROL INTERFACE
The RGB observation contains one base-camera view and two wrist-camera views, each resized to
224×224. FM and ACT encode each view with a frozen DINOv2-small backbone and use its 384-
dimensional CLS feature. The tactile input uses marker displacement rather than the tactile image:
for each of four Xense pads, 700 tracked markers form a 35×20 grid with an XYZ displacement
vector relative to the episode reference. FM and ACT concatenate the four pads along the channel
dimension into a 35×20×12 tensor; π0.5 retains four separate 35×20×3 grids. Each policy observes
only the current synchronized frame and the current 14-dimensional dual-arm joint state. The robot
interface consumes the first 14 output dimensions as absolute joint targets.
Figure 16 summarizes how the same sensory interface is specialized by the three policies. FM
compresses vision, touch, and state into one global condition for a one-dimensional U-Net velocity
field. ACT exposes the modality features as Transformer memory tokens and decodes a complete
action chunk with learned queries. π0.5 appends four tactile tokens to its vision–language prefix and
predicts actions with a Gemma action expert.
D.2
FLOW-MATCHING POLICY
The FM policy encodes the three RGB views with frozen DINOv2-small features and uses
lightweight encoders for the 14D robot state and the concatenated tactile-displacement grid. The
three modalities are fused into a 256D condition ct. A conditional 1D U-Net then learns a flow
field from Gaussian noise to a normalized relative 32 × 14 joint-action chunk using the standard
flow-matching objective. At inference, we integrate the learned flow field with 32 Euler steps. The
predicted chunk is unnormalized using the checkpoint action normalizer and converted, relative to
the current robot state, into absolute 14D joint targets. All 32 actions are executed at 10 Hz before
the policy replans.

ACT
Our ACT variant uses frozen DINOv2-small features for the three RGB views and compresses the
four tactile grids into a single token. These modality tokens, together with the current robot state and
a CVAE latent, condition a Transformer decoder with learned action queries. The model is trained
to reconstruct normalized relative joint-action chunks with KL regularization on the CVAE latent.
At deployment, the latent is fixed to zero and the complete action chunk is decoded in one forward
pass. The predicted relative actions are unnormalized using the checkpoint action normalizer and
converted, relative to the current robot state, into absolute 14D joint targets before execution. For all
four tasks, ACT predicts and executes a 32-step action chunk at 10 Hz before replanning, without
temporal ensembling.
D.4
TACTILE-AUGMENTED π0.5
The π0.5 policy combines SigLIP visual tokens, a PaliGemma language–state context branch, and a
Gemma action expert for flow-based generation. A shared CNN independently encodes each tactile
pad into a token, and the four tactile tokens are appended to the language–vision prefix through a
learned gate. The model predicts a 16-step action chunk in a 32D internal space, of which the first
14 dimensions control the robot. Deterministic evaluation and real-robot deployment use a ten-step
Flow-ODE sampler from Gaussian noise to action. During GRPO rollout, one of the ten denoising
steps is selected uniformly and replaced by a stochastic Flow-SDE transition, while the remaining
steps use Flow-ODE integration.
E
REWARD MODEL TRAINING
E.1
ROLES AND SUPERVISION
We train reward models to provide supervision for world-model post-training and rewards for pol-
icy reinforcement learning inside the trained world model. Three models address complementary
aspects of the generated interaction. The State Reward Model scores whether an RGB observation
satisfies the task outcome; its scores guide world-model post-training and provide the binary reward
for policy GRPO. The Scene Reward Model scores whether a generated frame preserves the ini-
tial scene while allowing intended robot and object motion. The Modality Reward Model scores
whether visual and tactile observations describe a compatible physical state. The latter two provide
scene and cross-modal supervision during world-model post-training.
All three models output a binary logit and are optimized with BCEWithLogitsLoss; a sig-
moid is applied only when a score in [0, 1] is needed. Table 6 summarizes their supervision. The

E.2
NETWORK ARCHITECTURE
Following the image-based reward-model design in RLinf Yu et al. (2026), we use ImageNet-
initialized ResNet-18 encoders and a scalar scoring head. The State Reward Model shares one
encoder across three RGB views and scores their concatenated features with a 1536 →256 →1
MLP. The Scene Reward Model shares an encoder between the initial GT and current WM views; a
two-layer Transformer decoder compares their 7 × 7 spatial tokens, using the reference as memory.
The Modality Reward Model uses separate encoders for RGB, tactile image, and marker displace-
ment, shared across views or pads within each modality. Its four-layer decoder lets visual tokens
query tactile tokens. Both decoders use eight attention heads and a 512→256→1 scoring head.
E.3
TRAINING CONFIGURATION
We train all three reward models for 100 epochs with AdamW on PPU or H20 accelerators. The State
Reward Model and Scene Reward Model use balanced positive and negative batches, whereas the
Modality Reward Model retains the mixture of aligned observations, controlled tactile mismatches,
and world-model-generated failures described above. We use a learning rate of 10−4 for task state
and scene consistency and 2×10−4 for visual–tactile consistency. Data are split by rollout to keep
observations from the same trajectory together. Once trained, the reward models are frozen during
world-model post-training and policy GRPO.
F
POLICY–WORLD MODEL INTERACTION ROLLOUT
F.1
CLOSED-LOOP INTERACTION
A policy rollout in PHYSCANVAS treats the world model as an action-driven virtual environment. A
trajectory begins from one synchronized real observation, after which the policy predicts an action
chunk from the current observation. We use a common task specification ξ: it identifies the task
for task-specific FM and ACT, and includes the language instruction required by the shared π0.5
policy. For all policy families, the corresponding task prompt is independently supplied to the text-
conditioned world model. The world model executes each chunk in short segments and generates the
corresponding future observations. Its latest prediction is converted back into the policy observation
interface, closing the loop until the target horizon is reached. Except for the initial state, the policy
therefore acts on generated rather than recorded future observations.
This interaction extends visual world-model rollout to the sensory interface required by contact-
rich policies. In addition to multi-view RGB, the generated canvas carries tactile images, marker

nized signals together with the current robot state. Consequently, policy behavior can respond not
only to visible geometry but also to predicted contact changes, while the same simulator provides a
common interaction loop for policies with different action horizons.

G
POLICY REINFORCEMENT LEARNING IN THE WORLD MODEL
G.1
RL IN WORLD MODEL
We optimize a policy by treating the frozen world model as the interaction environment and the
frozen State Reward Model as the outcome evaluator. Starting from one real reset observation,
the policy samples multiple action trajectories. The world model converts each trajectory into a
closed-loop multimodal rollout, and the State Reward Model evaluates the generated three-view
RGB observations. Trajectory rewards are normalized only against alternatives generated from the
same initial condition. The resulting group-relative advantages supervise a clipped policy update;
gradients update the policy while the world model and State Reward Model remain fixed.
Figure 17 summarizes the GRPO sampling process. FM uses 32 integration steps and π0.5 uses 10.
Each policy call selects one step uniformly for a Gaussian Flow-SDE transition; the remaining steps
use deterministic Flow-ODE integration. We store the selected index, action latents before and after
that transition, current RGB, robot state, and tactile inputs, the tokenized prompt for π0.5, and the

whereas π0.5 recomputes the selected Flow-SDE denoising transition. The resulting current-policy
log-probability forms the GRPO likelihood ratio. The generated rollout supplies the reward and the
stored transition supplies actor gradients without differentiating through the world model.
