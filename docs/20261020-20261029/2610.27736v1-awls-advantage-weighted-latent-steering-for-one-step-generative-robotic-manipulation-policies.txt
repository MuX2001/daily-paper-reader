Abstract: Human-collected real-world robotic manipulation demonstrations of-ten contain mixed-quality trajectories, including hesitant, indirect, or inefficient action segments. While diffusion- and flow-based policies can model such multi-modal data, standard behavior cloning (BC) assigns equal likelihood to all demon-strated actions and may therefore reproduce suboptimal modes. Offline RL value estimates offer a natural signal for biasing policies toward higher-value behav-ioral modes, but directly fine-tuning the generative policy or using critic gra-dients for policy steering can amplify critic errors and induce prior drift. We propose Advantage-Weighted Latent Steering (AWLS), an offline post-training method for improving frozen one-step generative manipulation policies without updating their decoders. AWLS learns a residual noise shaper, an observation-conditioned residual correction to the policy’s latent noise, and trains it with an advantage-weighted action reconstruction objective. The advantages are detached, scale-normalized, and used only as supervised weights on demonstrated actions, avoiding critic-gradient policy updates while preserving fast single-step infer-ence. We further introduce a rollout-free decoder-sensitivity criterion for select-ing pretrained checkpoints that remain responsive to latent-noise perturbations. Experiments on simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks with clean and mixed-quality demonstrations show that AWLS improves over the frozen base policy while limiting drift from the pretrained behavior prior. ## 1 Introduction 

Generative policies based on diffusion and flow matching [1, 2, 3] have become a standard approach to imitation learning for robotic manipulation. They are typically trained by behavior cloning (BC), which fits the policy to human-collected demonstrations and therefore inherits their quality. Yet even successful demonstrations are rarely uniformly optimal. Some trajectories are smooth and efficient, while others are hesitant, indirect, or unnecessarily long [4, 5]. Standard BC does not distinguish between these quality levels, so it reproduces hesitant behavior as faithfully as efficient behavior (Figure 1, left). Can a policy trained on such data instead be steered toward the better behavior in those demonstrations? Offline reinforcement learning (RL) offers an answer: value estimates learned from the same demon-strations can identify higher-advantage demonstrated actions in their respective contexts. The ques-tion then becomes how to inject this signal into a pretrained generative policy. Guiding the sam-

This makes frozen-decoder latent steering an appealing interface: the pretrained de-coder is kept fixed, and improvement is attempted by reshaping the latent noise. Yet this inter-face exposes two hidden failure modes. First, critic-gradient (Q-maximization) steering in latent space [11, 12, 13, 14] can still chase extrapolated value artifacts rather than demonstrated high-value behavior (Figure 1, middle). Second, latent steering is only effective if the frozen decoder remains sensitive to its latent noise, a property that behavior cloning may weaken during training. We propose Advantage-Weighted Latent Steering (AWLS) to mitigate both failure modes simulta-neously. AWLS post-trains the policy with the pretrained decoder frozen, learning only a residual noise shaper that applies observation-conditioned corrections to the latent-noise input. These cor-rections are initialized near zero to bias post-training toward the original latent prior. Critically, the offline critic never provides a gradient to the policy: advantages estimated via implicit Q-learning (IQL) [15] are detached and enter training only as scalar weights on demonstrated actions [16] (Fig-ure 1, right)—critic errors may change which demonstrated actions are emphasized without directly providing gradients toward potentially unsupported actions. Two additional mechanisms help make this interface practical across tasks: scale-normalized advantage weighting bounds weight concen-tration independently of reward magnitude, and a rollout-free decoder-sensitivity diagnostic selects frozen checkpoints that remain responsive to latent-noise perturbations. Our contributions are: (i) we identify two reliability issues in frozen latent steering for one-step generative manipulation policies, critic-gradient failure modes and decoder-sensitivity degradation, and address them with detached advantage weights and decoder-sensitivity checkpointing; (ii) we propose AWLS, a residual noise shaper trained by advantage-weighted reconstruction with scale-normalized weights while keeping the decoder frozen; (iii) we evaluate AWLS on simulation bench-marks, controlled mixed-quality demonstrations, and real-world robot tasks. 

## 2 Related Work 

Generative policy learning. Diffusion and flow policies learn expressive action distributions for manipulation [17, 18], using RGB and depth perception [1, 2, 19] to condition action generation. Few- and one-step variants reduce sampling cost for fast closed-loop control [20, 21]. Building on these policy classes, offline RL uses learned value functions to guide policy training and action selection [22, 23, 24]. Pretrained policies can also be adapted through offline-to-online RL, policy-2gradient updates, or generative-model optimization [10, 25, 9, 26, 27, 28]. In offline settings, value-gradient updates can exploit critic extrapolation errors and move the policy away from its behavior-cloning prior [29]. Behavior-cloning regularization can reduce this drift, but the policy update still depends on critic gradients. 

Frozen-decoder post-training. A complementary line keeps the pretrained decoder fixed. Inference-time guidance steers multi-step denoising with value functions, return conditioning, or dynamics guidance [6, 7, 8, 30], but needs an iterative sampler that one-step generators do not expose. Noise-space steering instead adapts the latent noise of a frozen decoder through critic gradi-ents, rollout search, or external feedback [11, 12, 13, 14]; critic-gradient variants still depend on the learned value landscape, and search- and feedback-based variants need additional evaluation signals. Latent-action offline RL [31, 32] trains a VAE-style latent policy with value gradients or advantage weights, and learned noise priors [33] guide an iterative diffusion sampler. AWLS differs from all of these in how it uses the latent interface. Following advantage-weighted imitation [16, 34, 35], offline advantages estimated with IQL [15] serve only as detached weights for supervised reconstruction of demonstrated actions. AWLS therefore trains no new generative model, keeps single-step inference, and needs neither value gradients nor critic-defined targets.

When Jθ has low gain or is concentrated in a few directions, this gradient provides little leverage for a latent-only update. We observe that such loss of latent sensitivity can occur late in BC training even after task performance has plateaued (Appendix E), so the final BC checkpoint is not necessarily the best frozen base for latent steering. We therefore score the checkpoints {θk} saved during BC training with two rollout-free diagnostics computed on held-out demonstrations. Best-of-M reconstruction error LBoM proxies BC quality by the best action-space match over M latent-noise samples. Directional sensitivity SJ is a finite-difference estimate of the gain of Jθ : the decoded action change under small random latent-noise perturbations. 4.3 Real-World Experiments 

We further evaluate AWLS on real robots to test whether the same offline post-training recipe trans-fers to policies trained from human teleoperated demonstrations, the intended setting of AWLS. 

Setup. For real-world experiments, the single-arm tasks use a UR5e manipulator, whereas the dual-arm tasks use two UR3e manipulators. The perception setup combines one third-view Intel RealSense D455 camera with wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D405 cameras on each arm, pro-viding a shared scene view together with close-range hand-centric observations. Policies consume these two RGB streams plus proprioception and output end-effector pose-and-gripper commands in action chunks at 10 Hz; the offline critic uses the same binary task-success reward as the evaluation criterion. Hardware, observation encoding, and evaluation details are given in Appendix I. 

Tasks and demonstrations. Figure 4 shows five real-world tasks: three single-arm tasks and two bimanual tasks. All methods are trained from the same offline teleoperated demonstrations: each task uses 70 demonstrations collected by SpaceMouse teleoperation, with commands expressed in the robot base frame. ## 5 Conclusion 

We propose AWLS, a decoder-preserving post-training method for one-step generative robotic ma-nipulation policies trained from mixed-quality demonstrations. AWLS freezes the pretrained de-coder and learns a residual noise shaper with detached, scale-normalized advantage weights, using the critic only to emphasize higher-quality demonstrated actions rather than to directly optimize ac-tions. Across simulation, controlled mixed-quality data, and real-world manipulation tasks, AWLS improves over the frozen base policy and Q-maximization baselines while preserving fast single-step inference and limiting drift from the behavior-cloning prior. 

## 6 Limitation 

AWLS is intended as a conservative post-training method: it can emphasize better behaviors al-ready present in the offline demonstrations and reachable through the frozen decoder, but it is not designed to synthesize entirely new skills absent from the dataset. Its performance also depends on the offline critic providing a useful relative ranking of demonstrated actions. While detached and scale-normalized weighting reduces sensitivity to critic scale and avoids critic-gradient policy updates, extending AWLS to broader task families, more diverse visual settings, and less clearly ranked datasets remains an important direction for future work.

## A One-Step Decoder Background 

AWLS treats the one-step decoder Gθ as a frozen black box: the only property the rest of the paper relies on is that Gθ (o, ϵ) maps an observation o and a Gaussian latent ϵ ∼ N (0 , I ) (of the same shape as the action chunk) to an executable action chunk in a single network evaluation. Both flow-based backbones we use, MeanFlow and FlowPolicy, provide this interface; we refer to the original papers for their training objectives. 

A common one-step interface. Although MeanFlow and FlowPolicy differ in parameterization (interval-average velocity uθ vs. instantaneous velocity vθ ) and training signal (an exact JVP-based stop-gradient identity vs. a finite-difference EMA consistency objective), they yield the same inter-face for downstream use. In both cases the trained network collapses, at its boundary evaluation (the full interval r = 0 , t = 1 for MeanFlow; t = 1 for FlowPolicy), to a single frozen map 

Gθ : ( o, ϵ) 7 −→ x0, ϵ ∼ N (0 , I ), (8) which is the interface of Eq. (1); AWLS is therefore agnostic to which backbone—MeanFlow or FlowPolicy—produced the decoder.

Remark 2 (Tightness and scope) . The bound is tight in the worst case : 4nR/ (1 + R)2 is the largest critic-independent floor possible, attained (in the continuous relaxation of Steps 2–4) by the two-point split with a fraction p∗ = 1 /(R + 1) of weights at w = R. Why decoder sensitivity decays. The decay of SJ follows directly from behavior cloning sharp-ening the conditional action distribution. Early in training the flow decoder still maps a broad region of the latent-noise prior onto a spread of plausible actions, so a latent perturbation z +hu produces a sizeable change in the decoded action and SJ is large. As BC proceeds, the decoder concentrates the mass it places on each observation onto the demonstrated action mode, driving the conditional map toward a near-deterministic function of the observation; the latent Jacobian ∂G θ /∂ z corre-spondingly shrinks and SJ falls. This is the same property that makes latent steering both attractive and fragile: AWLS can only reshape behavior that the frozen decoder still exposes through its latent input, so post-training gains are largest at an intermediate checkpoint where the decoder remains re-sponsive yet has already acquired competent behavior, and shrink once over-training has collapsed that degree of freedom.

## H Mixed-Quality Demonstration Construction 

For the DexArt mixed-quality study, we generate noisy demonstrations by executing the RL expert with temporally correlated OU action perturbations. At each control step, the perturbation evolves as 

ηt+1 = ηt + κ(μ − ηt)∆ t + σ√∆t ξ t, ξt ∼ N (0 , I ), (17) with zero mean μ = 0 , mean-reversion rate κ = 0 .15 , and control timestep ∆t = 0 .1 s (the 10 Hz control period). The perturbed action is clipped to the valid action range before execution. We tune the per-task noise scale σ (Bucket 0.15 , Laptop 0.4, Toilet 0.6, Faucet 0.6; also annotated in the subplot titles of Figure 8) so that the expert rollout success rate falls from near 100% to below 20% ,then use the resulting rollouts as the low-quality pool. Mixed datasets are constructed by replacing a chosen fraction of clean expert demonstrations with noisy expert-derived demonstrations while keeping the total number of demonstrations fixed. 

Noisy-pool calibration and statistics. The per-task σ above is set by a coarse sweep so that the OU-perturbed expert reaches the target low-quality regime; the resulting noisy-pool rollout success rates are 18% (B UCKET ), 16% (L APTOP ), 14% (T OILET ), and 19% (F AUCET ), all below the 20% 

ceiling. For each task we roll out the perturbed expert for 600 episodes across the three environ-ment seeds ( 200 per seed) and keep only usable trajectories: time-limited rollouts are retained, but episodes that abort on a simulator fault or leave the valid workspace ( 3–6% per task) are filtered out, leaving ≥ 500 trajectories per task before sub-sampling to the requested mix fraction. The OU pro-cess and the clean/noisy assignment use fixed seeds {0, 1, 2} (shared with the three training seeds), so the mixed datasets are reproducible. Both clean and noisy demonstrations come from the same 

RL expert and differ only in the injected action noise, so the two pools share state coverage and differ only in execution quality. 

Limitations of the OU proxy. Ornstein–Uhlenbeck action perturbation is a controllable but im-perfect stand-in for genuine human mixed-quality behavior: it injects temporally correlated, zero-mean noise around the expert action rather than the structured, goal-directed suboptimality of real teleoperators (hesitation, re-grasps, indirect paths, and pauses). It therefore captures the noisy-execution axis of mixed quality but not higher-level strategic suboptimality. ## I Real-World Experiment Details 

This section expands the real-world setup of Section 4.3; all real-world policies use the MF-AWLS variant, and the robot is used only for the final evaluation rollouts. 

Hardware and control. The single-arm tasks use a Universal Robots UR5e manipulator and the bimanual tasks use two Universal Robots UR3e manipulators. Perception combines one third-view 26 Intel RealSense D455 camera with one wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D405 camera per arm. Un-like the simulation benchmarks, which consume point clouds, the real-world policy operates directly on RGB images: a third-view stream ( img ) and a wrist stream ( rgb hand ), each 256 × 256 and center-cropped to 224 × 224 . Policies run in closed loop at 10 Hz. 

Observation and action spaces. Each RGB stream is encoded by an independent ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 backbone (global average pooling followed by a linear projection to a 128 -dimensional feature); the two camera features are concatenated with a 64 -dimensional embedding of the 7-dimensional proprioceptive state, giving a 2 × 128 + 64 = 320 -dimensional observation feature. The action space is the 7-dimensional end-effector command (pose and gripper) for single-arm tasks. The frozen MeanFlow decoder predicts an action chunk of horizon 4 from 2 observation steps and executes the 4 predicted steps per inference. The encoder ( ≈ 22 .5M parameters) and the MeanFlow ConditionalUnet1D decoder (down dimensions [512 , 1024 , 2048] , ≈ 267 M parame-ters) total ≈ 289 M parameters. 

Demonstrations and BC training. All methods are trained from the same offline teleoperated demonstrations: every real-world task is collected with 70 demonstrations teleoperated with a Space-Mouse, with commands expressed in the robot base frame. The MeanFlow decoder is behavior-cloned for 3000 epochs with AdamW (learning rate 10 −4, β = (0 .95 , 0.999) , weight decay 10 −6), batch size 128 , a cosine schedule with a 500 -step warmup, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, and an EMA of the weights; checkpoints and evaluations are taken every 200 epochs. Even when success-ful, these demonstrations vary in speed, contact timing, pauses, corrections, and final alignment, producing the within-success mixed-quality conditions that advantage weighting is designed to ex-ploit. 

Post-training on real data. For each task we behavior-clone the MeanFlow decoder, select the frozen base checkpoint with the rollout-free SJ criterion of Eq. (7), train the offline IQL critic on the same demonstrations, and run AWLS on top of the 320 -dimensional ResNet-18 observation fea-tures using the residual shaper and critic of Appendix F with detached, scale-normalized advantage weights ( β = 3 ). Critic gradients are never backpropagated into the decoder, and no real rollouts are collected during this stage. 

Baseline parity. FQL and DSRL are matched to MF-AWLS in every shared component so that the real-world comparison isolates the post-training method, not the representation. All four poli-cies (frozen MeanFlow base, FQL, DSRL, MF-AWLS) use the identical perception stack described above and the same demonstrations. FQL and DSRL reuse the same offline IQL critic (archi-tecture and hyperparameters of Appendix F.4) trained on those demonstrations, are checkpoint-selected by the same rollout-free SJ rule (Eq. (7)), and receive the same post-training compute and hyperparameter-tuning budget—a single shared sweep over learning rate and steering strength with an equal number of gradient updates. The methods differ only in the generative decoder and steer-ing module each one intrinsically requires: DSRL steers a 10 -step diffusion decoder, whereas FQL and MF-AWLS use a one-step decoder of matched size (Table 15); none of the baselines uses real rollouts during post-training, exactly as for AWLS.
