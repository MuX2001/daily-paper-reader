Abstract — Large pretrained robot foundation models provide strong priors for manipulation, yet adapting them to target tasks with offline teleoperated demonstrations often fails to cover the contact-rich, recovery, and precision-critical states encountered during deployment. Existing methods mitigate this issue by augmenting offline demonstrations with human-in-the-loop data or online reinforcement learning. Despite this, offline post-training adapts slowly because it cannot immediately ab-sorb deployment experience, while online RL typically relies on value functions or auxiliary actor-critic models, increasing both training complexity and instability. We present TIDE (Train-ing In DEployment), a human-in-the-loop online post-training system. TIDE introduces a provenance-conditioned supervised learning framework that updates the policy online from human interventions and policy-generated rollouts collected during deployment, without a learned critic, reward model, or action scorer. Across four real-world bimanual manipulation tasks, TIDE improves milestone-normalized task progress and binary full-task completion over the warm-start policy and matched additional offline demonstrations, and performs comparably to the fixed-policy collection baseline while making updated checkpoints available during deployment. Each task-specific online post-training session, from the start of deployment rollouts until the final checkpoint is available, completes in less than one hour while learning and checkpoint handling run asynchronously.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Modern robot manipulation increasingly relies on large pretrained robot foundation models [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]. A common post-training paradigm adapts these pretrained priors to a target task using offline human teleoperation data [1], [7], producing an initially deployable policy. How-ever, real-robot failures often arise in off-demonstration states induced by the policy itself, including contact-rich interactions, recovery situations, and precision-critical con-figurations. Covering such rare but deployment-critical states by simply collecting additional offline teleoperation episodes is inherently data-inefficient [8], [9], [7]. Human-in-the-loop learning offers a natural mechanism for covering these policy-induced states, as human operators can intervene during failures or challenging recovery behav-iors. However, existing approaches often treat deployment primarily as a provider of expert action labels for supervised learning: human-intervention segments are used as demon-strations, while policy-generated segments are discarded or treated as unlabeled data [8], [10], [11], [12]. This limits data efficiency, especially when most deployment experi-ence is generated by the policy itself. Moreover, offline-arXiv:2610.29264v1 [cs.RO] 21 Oct 2026 only post-training methods [10], [11], [13], [12], [14] typ-ically follow a collect-train-deploy cycle, preventing them from immediately incorporating deployment experience and thereby slowing adaptation under distribution shift. Online reinforcement learning leverages both human interventions and policy-generated rollouts, bringing policy updates closer to deployment [15], [16]. However, these methods often rely on value functions or critics trained from sparse episode-level success-or-failure feedback. Such weak supervision makes it difficult to stably evaluate high-dimensional action chunks, increasing both training complexity and instability. Other systems reduce this burden by updating only small policy modules or by shifting annotation away from real-robot rollouts [15], [17], [18]. While effective in constrained set-tings, these design choices limit the potential of online post-training to improve large robot foundation models across diverse manipulation tasks. Existing methods therefore lack a mechanism for promptly incorporating deployment-time intervention and execution signals without deferring learning to a subsequent offline training round or relying on difficult-to-estimate value functions. Our central insight is that, once a pretrained policy is warm-started on a target task, deployment need not be a data-collection stage before the next offline retraining round. It can instead serve as a continual learning process that pro-duces structured training signals. Human interventions and policy executions reveal what the current policy can accom-plish and which state regions still require repair. Deployment data should therefore be organized as provenance-structured online experience rather than flattened into additional expert demonstrations. Building on this insight, we introduce TIDE (Training In DEployment), a human-in-the-loop online post-training sys-tem for pretrained manipulation policies. As shown in Fig. 1, TIDE places the robot, the human operator, and the learner in a single deployment loop: the robot executes action chunks generated by the current policy, the operator intervenes only when necessary, and the system records the executed action at each step together with its provenance, i.e., whether it was produced by the human or the policy. Provenance records execution source only; it is not a reward, correctness label, or quality score. The learner continuously updates all trainable policy modules using both offline task data and the growing online dataset, and returns updated checkpoints to the robot at episode boundaries. Rather than treating mixed online episodes as homogeneous expert demonstrations, TIDE re-structures them into pure-provenance chunks. These labels enable provenance-conditioned supervised training without a separate reward model, critic, or action scorer. We validate TIDE on four real-robot manipulation tasks spanning precision contact, deformable-object manipulation, constrained placement, and recovery from policy-induced states. Across these tasks, TIDE improves no-takeover task performance over the warm-start policy and matched addi-tional offline demonstrations. It performs comparably to the Fixed Policy baseline while making updates available during deployment. The observed intervention ratio also decreases over deployment, indicating lower human-supervision burden without serving as an autonomous-performance measure. This paper makes three main contributions: (1) TIDE ,a human-in-the-loop online post-training system that keeps real-robot execution, human takeover, learning, and policy redeployment in one session; (2) provenance-conditioned joint optimization of all trainable policy modules, turning mixed human-policy deployment data into structured source supervision; and (3) real-robot evidence across four manipu-lation tasks showing improved no-takeover task performance and a declining observed intervention ratio. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Robot foundation models for manipulation 

Modern manipulation policies are increasingly built on large pretrained robot foundation models that map obser-vations and language instructions to actions [19], [4], [2], [3], [1], [20], [21]. RT-1 and RT-2 [19], [4], OpenVLA [2], and Octo [3] established this recipe for broad instruction-conditioned manipulation, while recent generalist and hu-manoid systems extend it across embodiments and physical settings [20], [21]. Progress has also come from more capable action interfaces, including flow-matching action experts over continuous action chunks [1], [14], [22], [23], and efficient action tokenization [24], [25]. In parallel, models that couple action generation with future visual state pre-diction use video-generation priors to improve robustness to unfamiliar skills and embodiments [5], [6], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]. These advances rely on increasingly large cross-embodiment datasets for robot learning [32], [33], [34], [35]. Yet deployed policies remain bounded by training-distribution coverage: small errors compound into out-of-distribution states, and precision-critical subtasks can resist improvement even after many additional teleoperation episodes [9], [7]. This limitation motivates post-training procedures that refine pretrained policies through interaction with the deployment environment. 

B. Human-in-the-loop post-training 

Human-in-the-loop learning extends imitation learning by allowing a human to intervene on states visited by the current policy, directly addressing covariate shift and compounding errors [8], [36], [10]. Subsequent systems improve when interventions are triggered, how mixed demonstration and intervention data are weighted, what corrective data are collected, or how disruptive takeover is [11], [13], [37], [9], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]. Related methods extract different learning signals from interventions: RLIF treats intervention signals as rewards for off-policy reinforce-ment learning, MILE models the intervention process and uses both intervention and non-intervention feedback, and Sirius reweights deployment data with approximated human trust for weighted behavioral cloning [45], [46], [13]. Many interactive imitation-learning systems still update models in batches. Recent systems move policy improvement closer to de-ployment, but each keeps only part of the desired closed loop. TIDE targets a complementary setting in which real-robot deployment remains in the data loop, the video backbone, cross-modal adapter, and action expert are jointly updated, and supervision is organized by action-source provenance rather than learned values or trajectory-level reward labels. Before deployment, we warm-start all trainable policy modules on offline human teleoper-ation data for the target task. This warm-started policy provides an executable initial-ization, but deployment can still expose states that are not covered by offline demonstrations. TIDE turns these deployment-time states into training signals within the same session. We first describe the training-in-deployment runtime, which integrates robot execution, provenance recording, online learning, and checkpoint handoff in a single loop (Section III-A). We then explain how execution records are converted into provenance-labeled chunks for provenance-conditioned training (Section III-B). An overview of the pipeline is shown in Fig. 2. 

A. Training-in-deployment system 

The training-in-deployment system couples a real-robot client, an inference-and-recording server, and an online learner within a single deployment session. The client ex-ecutes actions from either the policy or the human operator and streams executed-action records to the server. The server handles policy inference, stores the incoming records, and makes them available to the learner. The learner updates all trainable policy modules online and returns new policy checkpoints at episode boundaries. 

a) Robot client: The robot client is responsible for low-latency real-robot control. At each control step, it collects observations, requests action chunks from the server, and ex-ecutes policy actions unless the operator intervenes. future actions are refreshed in the background. This design reduces inference-induced stalls and smooths transitions be-tween consecutive action chunks. During takeover, VR delta control allows the operator to command incremental motions around the current robot state and return control to the policy without resetting the episode. Each handoff clears queued policy chunks to prevent stale actions from being executed. The client streams executed actions and intervention flags asynchronously to the server, keeping data recording off the critical control path. 

b) Inference and recording server: The inference-and-recording server mediates between real-robot control and on-line training. It centralizes GPU-based policy inference and writes online episodes to a shared dataset, while leaving low-latency control to the robot client. For each client query, the server runs the active checkpoint on the current observation and returns an action chunk. In parallel, it appends execution records and manages episode commit or discard. At episode end, the human operator manually confirms task completion. Before upload, the server commits only confirmed-successful episodes; failed, timed-out, aborted or stopped, reset-before-completion, re-recorded, stale, and unrecovered episodes are discarded. To ensure rollout consistency, the server adopts learner-produced checkpoints only at episode boundaries, so that each rollout is associated with a single policy generation. 

c) Online learner: The online learner continuously incorporates deployment experience into the pretrained pol-icy during the same deployment session. As committed records arrive, it reads from the growing online dataset and jointly updates the video diffusion backbone, cross-modal adapter/Q-former, action expert, and source embeddings with offline task data; the pretrained text encoder and video VAE remain frozen. The learner allocates 0.2 optimizer steps per newly committed control frame and publishes a checkpoint every five committed episodes. The server prefetches new weights and adopts them only at the next episode boundary. This mechanism returns model improvements to the robot during deployment while ensuring that each rollout is gen-erated by a single policy generation. 

B. Provenance-conditioned learning a) Provenance-labeled chunks: The execution loop de-scribed above produces a continuous stream of mixed de-ployment episodes containing both human interventions and policy executions. To convert these episodes into supervision, TIDE assigns provenance labels at the action-chunk level rather than treating an entire episode as a single demonstra-tion. Human-executed chunks provide supervision for failure recovery and difficult states, while policy-executed chunks provide additional learning signal from behaviors that the current policy can already perform. Chunk-level provenance labels allow TIDE to use both sources of deployment data without flattening mixed episodes into homogeneous expert demonstrations. The provenance labels are derived directly from signals recorded during execution. At each control step, TIDE stores the action sent to the robot and whether that action was produced by the human operator or by the policy. Prove-nance encodes action-source identity only, not action quality. Episode success is used only to decide whether an episode enters the online training dataset; it is not a model condition, reward, provenance label, or training target. d) Baselines: Every method starts from the same task-specific warm-start checkpoint. The offline-demo baseline uses newly collected teleoperation demonstrations matched to the amount of online data, then trains for the same number of optimizer steps as TIDE. The fixed-policy collection base-line (Fixed Policy) holds the warm-start policy fixed during HIL collection matched to TIDE by duration, then trains after collection using the same objective, buffer configuration, fil-tering rules, and total optimizer-update budget. TIDE instead trains and redeploys checkpoints during collection. 

e) Implementation details: The reported world-action policy has 3B parameters and is trained on eight H100 GPUs. Each online optimizer step takes approximately 0.4 s on this setup. Online training jointly updates the video diffusion backbone, cross-modal adapter/Q-former, action expert, and source embeddings, while the text encoder and video VAE remain frozen. The policy predicts a 50-step, 14-dimensional bimanual joint-relative action chunk. Each predicted offset is added to the current joint state before the controller receives the resulting absolute joint target. Online samples have a 5:1 weight relative to offline samples ( α = 5 ), equivalent to an online-buffer selection probability of 5/6; this preliminarily tuned setting is fixed across all reported tasks and variants. The learner takes 0.2 optimizer steps per newly committed control frame and publishes a checkpoint every five commit-ted episodes; the server prefetches and adopts it at the next episode boundary. For each task, the continuous wall-clock interval from the start of online HIL rollouts until the final checkpoint is deployable is less than one hour. This interval includes asynchronous data processing, training, checkpoint handling, robot execution, human takeover, and episode resets, but excludes initial setup and final autonomous evalu-ation. Online training runs concurrently with data collection and episode resets, and its wall-clock cost is included in this reported interval. For Bit Insertion, collection comprised 41 committed episodes and 38,839 control frames (21.6 minutes at 30 Hz): 1,432 human-controlled frames (3.69%) and 37,407 policy-controlled frames.

We presented TIDE, a human-in-the-loop online post-training system that turns real-robot deployment into a learn-ing process for pretrained manipulation policies. TIDE places robot execution, human takeover, online data formation, joint optimization of all trainable policy modules, and checkpoint handoff in one deployment session. Its provenance-labeled chunks distinguish human-intervention from policy-executed behavior by source, enabling supervised online training with-out learned critics, trajectory-level reward labels, or action scorers. Across four real-world tasks, TIDE achieves higher milestone-normalized task progress and binary completion than the warm-start and additional-offline-data baselines.
