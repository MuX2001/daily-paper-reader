## Abstract 

We present OpenTac, a novel sensor and a full-stack ecosystem for tactile skins that are highly customizable, scalable, and readily deployed in downstream robot control and learning applications. Our core contribution is twofold. First, we develop a scalable resistive tactile skin with a structured deformable overlay that converts tangential loading into spatially varying pressure. Differential responses across neighboring ‘taxels’ support decoding of local shear vectors, and the common-mode response encodes normal force. Second, we provide a highly customizable sensor design tool that is able to automatically generate fabrication files for these tactile skins from 2D outlines and developable 3D surfaces, including from abstract descriptions such as text (e.g., “ design a sensor for a size 9 shoe insole ”). We develop an ecosystem around the OpenTac sensor, including the CAD design tool, a sensor simulator, cross-sensor transfer, and fast/adaptive data acquisition. We demonstrate the broad applicability of the OpenTac-CAD sensor design tool by designing and fabricating more than 30 sensors across a variety of robot embodiments. We characterize the three-dimensional tactile fields produced by OpenTac with a commercial, high-precision force/torque sensor. We demonstrate the broad applicability of 3D tactile sensing across contact-rich feedback control and manipulation policy learning on four real-world robot embodiments. Additionally, adaptive acquisition enables kilohertz-rate partial readouts, exceeding full-array raster scanning on the same sensor by 19 .2× at 16 sampled taxels per update for a 1024-taxel skin. We provide all hardware, software, and result videos are at opentac.github.io

## 1 Introduction 

As robots expand to perform tasks in more general, var-ied, and dynamic environments, their ability to manipulate quickly, reliably, and safely will increasingly depend on di-rectly sensing and reacting to contact—that is, on tactile sensing. However, sensors must first be customized to fit the end-effector or surface of interest. As a result, research on the use of tactile sensing is fundamentally limited by customizability: researchers must either adapt their work to sensors manufactured in fixed geometries, or they must possess sufficient design and fabrication expertise to develop custom sensors in-house. These constraints are particularly limiting when instrumenting custom-designed robot geome-tries or larger, irregular surfaces. In this paper, we focus on conformal tactile skins , as they integrate distributed sensing elements into thin, flexible sur-faces that can be wrapped around robot geometries, enabling fully sensorized robot bodies. However, existing tactile skins, such as FlexiTac [1], provide only normal-force (1D) esti-mates and require manual redesign for each new geometry. We present a sensor design that can be easily customized to fit many robot geometries, scaled in sensing area or reso-lution, and used to estimate normal and shear forces (3D) over local groups of sensing elements. Several efforts have partially addressed sensor customizability and made tactile skins more capable and accessible for the robot control and learning communities [1–3]. AnySkin pro-vides replaceable sensing surfaces but relies on a predefined electronic layout [2], while eFlesh automates the design of a deformable sensor body but relies on separately supplied magnetometer electronics [3]. In addition to the challenges of customizing the entire sensor, existing sensors are sam-pled at rates ranging between 30 and 100 Hz. Such raster scanning-based acquisition can limit the ability to resolve and respond to contacts lasting only a few milliseconds. Be-yond hardware, changes in sensor geometry must also be accommodated by the software used to acquire, simulate, and process tactile observations. To address these limitations, we introduce OpenTac , an open sensor design environment and software ecosystem for customizable, scalable tactile sensing (Fig. 1). OpenTac supports the design of low-cost piezoresistive tactile skins that estimate both local normal and shear forces with millimeter-scale spatial resolution over planar and developable surfaces. Our sensor combines a flexible printed circuit board and a piezoresistive film with a structured elastomeric overlay that redistributes lateral pressure across neighboring sensing elements, allowing local shear to be decoded while retaining normal-force information. The sensing layout, electrode geometry, and mechanical overlay can be customized to the surface geometry and force range of interest. To support this degree of customizability, OpenTac includes a full-stack ecosystem of design, simulation, and interoperabil-ity tools. OpenTac-CAD is a design tool that automatically generates sensor fabrication files from a desired geometry. Users can provide a 2D outline, select a developable surface on a 3D robot model, or specify an abstract verbal description, such as “ design a star-shaped sensor that fits inside a circle with a diameter 5 cm .” In addition, OpenTac-CAD produces a machine-readable manifest file describing the sensor’s ge-ometry, electrical addressing, and mechanical configuration. 

OpenTac-Sim provides tools to simulate the sensors and sensor-object interactions for robot manipulation tasks across any sensor design described by the manifest file. OpenTac-Transfer enables cross-sensor transfer by mapping calibrated tactile observations across sensor layouts using their physical coordinates and local surface frames. These components allow sensors with different shapes, resolutions, and electrical layouts to share acquisition and processing tools and provide a common interface for downstream control and learning. Scaling tactile coverage also increases the number of mea-surements required to read the sensor. To address this, we also provide OpenTac-Xampling , an adaptive acquisition method which operates across different sensor outlines using adaptive partial scans. For example, on a 32 × 32 skin, adaptive acquisition of sparse contact events reaches 1286 Hz, more than a 19 × increase over full-array scans (67 Hz) achieved by conventional acquisition [4].

We evaluate all components of the OpenTac ecosystem. We use OpenTac-CAD to generate fabrication files for more than 30 sensor layouts (including robot fingers, grippers, palms, and feet), fabricate the corresponding sensors, and deploy them on four real robot embodiments. We further evaluate the 3D force estimates against a commercial force/torque sensor. Finally, we demonstrate that our sensor’s normal and shear force estimates enable contact-rich feedback control and manipulation policy learning. To summarize, our main contributions are: • OpenTac: a full-stack ecosystem for automated sensor design, fabrication, simulation, cross-sensor observation mapping, and kilohertz-rate adaptive acquisition. • A customizable tactile skin that estimates normal and shear forces using a simple, scalable construction. • Evaluations spanning over 30 fabricated layouts, sensor characterization, high-speed sensing, and real-world robot control and learning, demonstrating the utility of the re-sulting tactile observations for contact-rich manipulation. All OpenTac hardware, software, and firmware is available under permissive licenses, enabling replication, modification, and redistribution for research and commercial use. 

## 2 Related Work 

Tactile sensing hardware typically involves optical, mag-netic, capacitive, and/or resistive transduction to recover contact. GelSight provides high-resolution optical contact sensing [5], while uSkin integrates distributed three-axis force elements [10]. GelSlim uses marker displacements and inverse finite-element modeling to estimate dense contact forces [14], while Yan et al. use a magnetized film to decouple normal force and one shear component [15]. Mechanical struc-tures can also encode shear into differential pressure: Sun et al. place a bump over four piezoresistive cells and infer load di-rection from their relative responses [13]. OpenTac builds on this principle using a single-FPCB (flexible PCB) sensing ar-chitecture designed for automated customization and shared readout design that specifies a unified cross-sensor interface. Early work on customizing and scaling tactile skins used modular or severable elements to cover complex robot sur-faces [16, 17], while later systems such as the iCub skin, RobotSweater, and conformal tactile textiles target large-area conformable coverage [12, 18, 19]. ReSkin and AnySkin de-couple the replaceable magnetic interface from the electron-ics [2, 11]. eFlesh automates magnetic microstructures [3], and FlexiTac provides an open flexible pressure array [1]. Computational design tools have also produced personalized FPCB tactile gloves [20] and surface-conforming capacitive sensors for existing 3D objects [21]. Lim et al. use laser-patterned flexible circuits to integrate capacitive pressure and bending sensors with temperature and proximity modules in customizable multimodal skins [22]. OpenTac instead starts from robot geometry and jointly generates the sensing layout, routing, connectorization, mechanical structure, and associated metadata, enabling robot-specific tactile skins. 

Tactile simulation and manipulation. TACTO [23] and Taxim [24] focus on simulating the imaging process inherent in vision-based tactile sensors. DiffTactile [25], TacEx [26], and Taccel [27] additionally implement sensor surface de-formation mechanics for robot manipulation. HydroShear further models shear and stick-slip effects for sim-to-real learning [28]. Tactile feedback has been used in fine ma-nipulation and shear-based control [29, 30]. Different to prior work, OpenTac-Sim implements a simulation frame-work specifically for OpenTac sensors using the compiled sensor geometry and bump layout to generate normal and tangential deformation observations.

## 3 The OpenTac Ecosystem 

OpenTac introduces a full-stack ecosystem for piezoresistive tactile skins in robot control and learning applications. The OpenTac tactile sensor comprises a structured deformable overlay, which redistributes tangential loading across a small grid of underlying taxels. This enables the estimation of both normal and shear forces over the piezoresistive film. All OpenTac components use a shared sensor description: the 

manifest . The ecosystem comprises four distinct modules: 1. OpenTac-CAD, which designs a sensor given a 2D outline or a developable 3D shape, producing the fabrication files and the manifest; 2. OpenTac-Sim, a simulation toolkit built on MuJoCo to simulate sensor readings; 3. OpenTac-Transfer, a module to enable cross-sensor ge-ometry transfer; and 4. OpenTac-Xampling, an adaptive readout module enabling kilohertz-rate sampling for sparse contact acquisition. Together, these enable OpenTac to be directly deployed across highly versatile traditional, learning-based, and inter-active robotics applications. 

3.1 OpenTac: Normal and Shear Sensor design 

We choose piezoresistive sensing for its low material cost, simple readout circuitry, and compatibility with standard flexible-PCB (fPCB) fabrication. The OpenTac sensor stack (Fig. 3B) comprises three primary components: a single fPCB (bottom), a piezoresistive film (middle), and a printed mechanical cover (top). At each row–column intersection on the fPCB, interdigitated copper fingers form one taxel. Any applied pressure on the sensor decreases the local resistance on the piezoresistive film and is converted by a readout circuit into an output voltage to estimate normal force. The top surface of the sensor is a deformable bump strip, based on [37], which is where sensor-object interactions occur. Each bump sits atop a small window (e.g., 2 × 2) of taxels. For three-axis force sensing, each elastomeric bump covers a local group of taxels, with the number and arrangement of taxels determined by the sensor geometry. For a 2×2

3.2 OpenTac-CAD: Automatic sensor design 

To deploy OpenTac sensors on a new robot, surface, or end-effector, we need to first custom-design a sensor that fits the surface. With existing sensors, customization is challeng-ing, necessitating expertise across electronic circuit design, electrode spacing, cable routing, mechanical design and mod-eling, and more. To address this, and to enable high degree of customizability, we build OpenTac-CAD. Given a 2D or 3D shape, our compiler deterministically generates the board ge-ometry. Users can customize sensing regions, taxel footprint, pitch, electrode and clearance rules, cable, and connector. 

Sensor outline extraction. OpenTac-CAD supports sensor design for a user-drawn 2D input region, a 2D outline im-ported from a vector drawing (DXF), or a 3D mesh (STL) where users specify the surface(s) to be sensorized. In the case of a 3D mesh, surfaces are first unfolded to 2D trian-gle by triangle across shared edges. Since each triangle’s flat shape follows exactly from its edge lengths, developable surfaces such as cylinders, cones, and faceted parts flatten without distortion, and doubly curved selections that cannot be flattened are detected and reported. We additionally support an agentic outline extraction mode, where a large language model processes an input text prompt and designs a 2D outline. Extracted outlines from this step are passed along to the next step for wiring. 

Taxel size, pitch, and placement. The default size across most OpenTac sensors is 4 mm taxels at 4.2 mm centre-to-centre spacing (also referred to as the pitch ). In our tests, we fabricated sensors ranging from 4 mm to 1 mm taxel sizes. Reducing the pitch increases the number of taxels within a given area and provides finer spatial sampling; increasing taxel size increases the signal measured. Sensor width and height can be independently configured if needed. Once taxel size and pitch are determined, we populate taxels all along the area to be sensorized, accounting for any additional free space required to route the wires originating from the taxels. We also allow users to specify additional design constraints, such as additional boundary space on the sensor edge, or the length and shape of the sensor tail (i.e., the side of the sensor where the wires are all routed to, and connected to the readout board). 

Readout boards. We provide two readout board designs: a 60-pin board for sensors with up to 30 rows and 30 columns, and a compact 30-pin pair for up to 15 rows and 15 columns. Both use the same firmware codebase and USB interface, with the sensor-specific layout supplied through its manifest. One half of the connector drives up to 32 lines through shift registers; the other senses up to 32 lines through a multiplexer and an inverting amplifier that holds the sensed line at virtual ground to suppress crosstalk. The compiler assigns one electrode set to each connector half and allocates pins outward from the center. This enables skins of varying sizes to be plugged into the same readout board. 

Contour-following routing. Rows and columns lie on op-posite faces of the fPCB, so routing each face is a planar single-layer problem. Every net follows a span of the sensor-array contour, split into hops around concavities. Spans on a face must be nested or disjoint, so unavoidable crossings are detected a priori. Routes are processed from the shortest to the longest span. Each route follows a clearance-offset contour around the array and previously routed copper, avoid-ing an expensive routing search. Every route is checked for clearance before acceptance. This estimates the required routing band for each sensor outline. 

Sensor Manifest The manifest describes the sensor’s me-chanical and electronic construction, and enables interop-erability across all tools in the OpenTac ecosystem. For every taxel i, it stores the physical center pi, row–column scan indices (ri, c i), and footprint parameters, together with the sensing boundary, manufactured outline, connector pin map, and the recipe that generated the board. Bump strips additionally store each taxel’s mechanical group b(i). Sepa-rating the geometric information from raster scan ordering is important for irregular skins, because the wires are optimized for routing, and that order could be different from that of the physical coordinates. 3.3 OpenTac-Sim: Simulating OpenTac sensors 

OpenTac-Sim provides a set of accurate simulation methods for OpenTac sensors. The bump-covered sensors that mea-sure both normal and shear forces are simulated using an FEM model, while smooth sensor skins that only measure nor-mal force are simulated using a reduced foam-contact model. Sensor geometry and output formats are directly instantiated from the sensor manifest, enabling OpenTac-Sim to support the simulation of sensors of arbitrary shapes and resolutions. 

Sensor model : The manifest file describes sensor geometry and is used in the initialization of multiple tactile pads . Each pad is converted into a tetrahedral mesh and is simulated as a linear-elastic FEM body in MuJoCo, with its base pinned onto the sensor base plate (or a robot gripper, as applicable). Upon physical contact with an object, the tactile pads deform based on the contact geometry and the load (Fig. 5A). We retain the original surface mesh for collision evaluation. At each bump, we compute normal and shear forces in the local tactile pad frame and interpolate them to taxel resolution. 

Robot and task simulation : We support the simulation of multiple robots from MuJoCo menagerie. New robots and tasks can be easily instantiated. The current release of the simulator only supports stable simulation of sensor-object interactions with rigid objects. 

Tactile readout : To simulate the effects that the sensor stack and circuitry would have on taxel readings, we also model interaction between the tactile pads, the base, and the electrode footprints. Starting from the FEM state, we compute the elastic force exerted by each tactile pad on the base, map it onto the electrode footprints, and apply our normal and shear force estimation method from Eq. 1. We run an automated calibration step per sensor to remove loads originating from the pad’s own scaled mass under gravity and arm acceleration. 

Smooth sensor simulation : The simulation also supports smooth sensor skins using a reduced foam-contact model. We model the foam as a Winkler layer, p = k(δ − z)+, solve 

δ to match the applied normal force, smooth the resulting pressure field with a Gaussian of width σ, and integrate it over the manifest taxels before applying the target calibration.

3.5 OpenTac-Xampling: Adaptive Acquisition 

Piezoresistive sensor arrays are commonly read via raster scanning . We read taxels left-to-right and top-to-bottom, by energizing one row and column at a time. For N taxels, this raster strategy costs N samples even when contact occupies only a small region. This results in two unintended con-squences. First, readout rates slow down with increase in the area or resolution of a tactile skin. Second, fast contacts that typically occur in under 10 milliseconds go undetected by the sensor. To mitigate these, OpenTac-Xampling assigns a bud-get of M ≪ N samples per frame, using the binary-quadtree adaptive subsampling of [33]. We allocate one quarter of this per-frame sampling budget to search over the full mask, and the remainder to follow active regions detected in the previous frame. This leverages the idea that contacts tend to be sparse, local, and temporally coherent. The approach works on any kind of sensor layout (rectangular, concave, curved-part, etc.). In the worst-case (i.e., when every single taxel experiences contact), this gracefully degrades to have identical complexity as raster scanning.

## 4 Experimental Evaluation 

We experimentally evaluate every component of the OpenTac ecosystem, including the compiler, sensor characterization, acquisition, transfer, and applicability to contact-rich control and manipulation policy learning. The key experimental findings are that: 1. OpenTac-CAD auto-generates designs that result in higher taxel coverage, without any design violations 2. OpenTac sensors provide accurate shear and normal force estimates, measured against reference commercial 3-axis force sensors 3. OpenTac-Sim faithfully simulates the sensors, as quanti-fied by aligned real-and-simulated sensor data 4. OpenTac computes accurate shear for downstream contact-rich control and manipulation policies 5. OpenTac-Transfer enables transferring normal forces across sensor designs 6. OpenTac-Xampling reads out at kilohertz-rates, measur-ing fast contacts that raster scanning methods miss

4.2 Shear, material, and geometry Characterization 

We conduct multiple studies to characterize the sensor and answer two questions: 1. How do each of the design choices (piezoresistive material, electrode geometry) impact force response? 2. How accurate are the normal and shear force estimates from OpenTac compared to reference 3-axis force sensors? The experiments all involve pressing a square sensor array pressed against a load cell, while varying one design variable at a time. The results are shown in Fig. 7 a–c. 

Choice of piezoresistive film : We compare 3 commercially available piezoresistive films – Chibitronics, LINQSTAT, and Yunjia. Each film exhibits different sensitivity and saturation behavior. At 2.0 mm pitch and 0.2 mm gap, Chibitronics saturates at low force, LINQSTAT does not reach the satura-tion criterion through 60 N, and Yunjia produces the lowest ADC response over the tested force range. 

Electrode geometry : Electrode gap has a modest effect on response, whereas larger pitch increases the per-taxel signal. Taxel pitch therefore affects both spatial resolution and sensitivity. For instance, 2 mm taxel pitch with a Chibitronics piezoresistive has a 60 N force range, but increasing the taxel pitch to 4 mm saturates the sensor earlier (at about 20 N). 

Force estimation accuracy : We conduct this experiment with our default configuration, which is a 4×4 bump strip array using a LINQSTAT piezoresistive sheet with a 4.0 mm taxel pitch and 0.2 mm electrode gap. The sensor is first calibrated with a reference force measured by the load cell. 4.3 Shear sensor simulation 

To validate whether the simulation faithfully models a real OpenTac shear sensor, we collected sensor measurements by pressing the bumps with a force-controlled UR5e robot end-effector. We vary the forces from 1 N and 5 N in increments of 0.5 N. We then repeat the experiment in simulation, where we press down a bump with exactly the same sequence of forces as we did in the real-world. We calibrate the real-world sensor so we can map the sensor readings to force units. We then compute two correlation plots, shown in Fig. 8. We notice that simulated sensor measurements are extremely well correlated with the mean value of real sensor data. Measurements from real sensor exhibit signal-dependent noise that generally increases with the force value. We also find a strong correlation between the contact depth and force across real-world and OpenTac. We tested the viability of the simulator for manipulation policy learning by training a peg insertion policy in the simulator, and show example qualitative results in Fig. 9.

4.8 Endurance (duty cycle) testing

## 5 Conclusion 

We have presented OpenTac, an open ecosystem spanning robot surface geometry, fabricated tactile skins, manifest-driven simulation, cross-sensor transfer, and high-speed ac-quisition. Several limitations remain. The flexible PCBs are not stretchable, and mesh-derived layouts require surfaces that can be flattened into developable patches. Piezoresistive sensing introduces hysteresis, drift, and temperature depen-dence. Adaptive acquisition trades spatial completeness for update rate; larger contact regions require larger sampling budgets. The simulated signals are deformation proxies, and transfer is evaluated on normal-contact shape rather than force accuracy or sim-to-real policy performance. We release all hardware, firmware, software, and data.
