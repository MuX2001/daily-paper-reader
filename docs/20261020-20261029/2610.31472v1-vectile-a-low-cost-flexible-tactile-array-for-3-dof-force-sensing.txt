# VecTile: A Low-Cost Flexible Tactile Array for 3-DoF Force Sensing 

Jian Hou, Student Member, IEEE , Xin Zhou, Member, IEEE , and Adam J. Spiers, Member, IEEE 

Abstract — Tactile sensing provides robots with contact in-formation difficult to obtain from vision alone. Shear force is particularly important for understanding tangential contact, guiding corrective motions, and maintaining grasp stability, yet flexible and low-cost tactile arrays often measure only normal force. We introduce VecTile, a low-cost, 6 mm thin, flexible tactile array capable of three-axis force sensing. VecTile combines a piezoresistive layer with passive silicone nubs that convert normal and shear forces into distinguishable pressure patterns. The sensor can be fabricated in-house without spe-cialized equipment. The complete system costs less than 35 USD for a 4 ×4 array. We validate the sensing mechanism through finite element analysis and hardware experiments. In controlled single-taxel tests over 1–5 N normal force and up to 1.5 N shear force, VecTile achieves root-mean-square errors of 0.39 N and 0.16 N for normal and shear forces. A VecTile calibrated while flat retains accuracy when curved, while a five-minute manual calibration is sufficient for practical use. Two applications demonstrate its utility. First, shear readings are sensitive enough to stop a sharp scalpel from puncturing a balloon after contact. Second, the sensor substantially improves Diffusion Policy-guided power-plug insertion. All designs and code will be released upon paper acceptance. Project website: https://jiannnh.github.io/VecTile/. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Tactile sensing gives robots a sense of touch during manipulation. It reveals force, friction, stiffness, and other properties that are difficult to infer from vision alone [1], [2]. This information is particularly important for grasp-stability estimation [3], [4], object recognition [5], [6], and in-hand manipulation [7], [8], [9]. During manipulation, the normal-force component cap-tures only part of the contact state. While the shear com-ponents reveal the direction and magnitude of tangential loading, supporting grasp-stability assessment, friction and slip reasoning, and contact regulation [10], [11]. Flexibility is also important because conformable tactile skins can cover non-planar and adaptive surfaces, including rounded fingers and soft grippers [12], [13]. Because direct contact exposes tactile sensors to wear and damage, low cost and repairability are important for practical deployment [14], [15]. These considerations motivate tactile skins that combine 3-degree-of-freedom (DoF) force sensing, flexibility, and low cost. Existing tactile architectures, however, trade among these properties due to fabrication and integration com-plexities [2]: Vision-based [11], [16], [17] and magnetic-based [10], [15] sensors can recover rich multi-axis contact information but are rigid, while thin capacitive [12] and piezoresistive [13] arrays more readily conform to curved

We introduce VecTile (Fig. 1), named as such due to its capability of reading a 3-DoF force vector at each tactile pixel (taxel). VecTile is a low-cost, flexible, 6 mm -thin, 4×4

tactile array (16 local 3-DoF taxels at a 10 mm pitch) that combines a piezoresistive array with passive silicone nubs. The complete sensor and readout board cost less than 35 USD (of which 25 USD is spent on an Arduino Nano that can be replaced with a lower-cost microcontroller and ADC circuit if needed). Raw frames stream at 343 Hz, and the measured end-to-end latency relative to a commercial force/torque (F/T) reference is approximately 25 ms . Most conveniently, this sensor can be easily produced in-house without the need for expensive equipment or tools. At its core is a combination of a standard flexible PCB (FPC), whose fabrication can be out-sourced at low cost (we used JLCPCB’s services) and silicone parts that can be easily produced via 3D printed molds (we used PLA molds from a Bambu Lab P1S) and hobby-grade vacuum chamber (less than 150 USD). After Section II outlining related work, Section III details the electronic design, the nub-based transduction mechanism validated via finite element analysis, as well as the fabrication process. Section IV experimentally evaluates various nub heights and quantifies their force-reconstruction accuracy on physical sensors, resulting in a design with 0.39 N normal RMSE and 0.16 N shear RMSE with a range of 1–5 N 

normal force and up to 1.5 N shear per taxel. We further assess performance differences between flat, convex, and concave configurations and demonstrate a practical calibra-tion process through short manual interactions against an F/T arXiv:2610.31472v1 [cs.RO] 22 Oct 2026 reference. Finally, we demonstrate the practical utility of VecTile on compliant gripper fingers in two downstream tasks (Sec-tion V). In the scalpel–balloon interaction, we show that the sensor’s shear force sensitivity allows a sharp scalpel to push against a fully blown balloon without rupturing it. A visuotactile plug-insertion task (Fig. 1) shows the sensor’s practicality via a learned manipulation policy under partially visual occlusion. II. R ELATED WORK 

Existing tactile sensors obtain multi-axis contact informa-tion using various forms of transduction hardware. Vision-based sensors such as GelSight [16] and DIGIT [17] use cam-eras to image the deformation of an elastomer surface, from which recent learning-based methods estimate a resultant 3-axis force [18]. However, their cameras, illumination, and op-tical paths require rigid housings, resulting in relatively bulky units with a limited sensing area. Magnetic sensors instead infer deformation from relative motion between magnets and magnetometers. eFlesh [10] and ReSkin [15] place magnetic skins over magnetometer boards to improve replaceability and customizability. However, the active electronics beneath the sensing surface complicate large-area integration and limit how freely the sensor can bend. Thin capacitive and piezoresistive matrices can form con-formable tactile skins, but simple implementations primarily measure normal pressure [12], [13]. CoinFT recovers a six-axis wrench from capacitive layer displacement, but remains a rigid, single sensing unit rather than a distributed skin [19]. Our VecTile design utilizes raised nubs as an alternative by mechanically converting shear into differential normal pressure. Prior works have also investigated this mechanism: Huh et al. created a rigid capacitive sensor for slip detection [20] and Yu et al. proposed a flexible piezoelectric nub array wrapped around finger sections of rubber gloves [21]. While both provide evidence for this mechanism’s high performance, neither has created a design that is particularly practical or cost effective in terms of in-house fabrication: the former requires spin coating of silicone on PCB, while the latter requires material coating, etching, and bonding. III. S ENSOR DESIGN 

A. Sensing Element and Readout Electronics 

Fig. 2(A) presents the sensing element, matrix architec-ture, and readout electronics. VecTile is a 4 × 4 taxel array with per-taxel three-axis force sensing, implemented on an FPC. Each round taxel comprises four piezoresistive quadrants (circular sectors) in a 2×2 arrangement. Within the quadrants sit electrode pairs: the outer ones are connected in rows (red), while the inner ones are connected in columns (blue), cre-ating an addressable 8 × 8 row–column matrix. The sensing mechanism relies on variable resistance: a pressure-sensitive silver-fiber fabric (TEK Silver Fiber model 75D, chosen for its local compliance to minimize cross-quadrant influence) is put on top of the FPC; compression lowers its resistance across each electrode pair. We read the 64 quadrants via the Arduino Nano ADC with the driven-column, sensed-row matrix scan illustrated in Fig. 2(A). A common flexible resistive architecture [1] places the sensing material between two patterned electrode sheets, with the current flowing perpendicular through the pressure-sensitive layer. On top of a simpler assembly, the main advantage is in the perfect electrode alignment without the need for precision layer positioning. Initially, we followed the simple-voltage-divider readout approach from [13], but experienced early saturation due to additional current paths when multiple nubs are loaded simultaneously. This issue was solved via an additional LM358 transimpedance amplifier, holding the non-activated columns at a 0 V virtual ground. A full list of electronic components can be found in the figure. For a selected quadrant (i, j ), the ideal output is given by relation VADC ,ij ≃ Vdrive Rref /R ij , where Rij is the pressure-dependent resistance of quadrant (i, j ), Vdrive is the selected-column voltage, and Rref is the transimpedance feedback resistance. Compression lowers Rij and therefore increases the ADC output. We implement Rref with a poten-tiometer, allowing the readout gain to accommodate resistive layers with different working resistances. For the present build, Rref = 10 kΩ ; the fixed resistors in the two-stage front end are also 10 kΩ .The Arduino ADC returns a 10-bit conversion for each quadrant, after which the firmware discards the two least-significant bits and transmits one 8-bit value per quadrant. Each 64-byte frame payload is transmitted at 2 Mbaud and a measured rate of approximately 343 Hz .

B. Nub-Lever Mechanism 1) Working Principle: Fig. 2(B) shows the assembled sensor stack and the nub-lever working principle. Each cast silicone nub covers four sensing quadrants and converts a three-axis contact force into four local normal-force signals. A normal force Fz compresses the nub and raises the four quadrant readings together. A shear force Fx or Fy tilts the nub and transfers normal load from the trailing side to the leading side. The mechanism therefore encodes normal force in the balanced response and shear force in the imbalance between opposing quadrant pairs. 

2) Finite Element Model: To quantify nub-induced pres-sure redistribution independently of piezoresistive transduc-tion and electrical readout, we developed a finite element model of a single nub under combined normal and shear loading (Fig. 3). The simulation assumes nominal in-plane symmetry and therefore considers only the positive Fx shear force case. We selected 3.0 mm as the maximum nub height to retain a thin sensor profile and simulated heights of 0.6,

1.0, 2.0, and 3.0 mm to quantify the effect of reducing nub height. The model uses Dragon Skin 30 and the fab-ricated nub geometry (near-incompressible Mooney–Rivlin model [22], C10 = 0 .198 MPa and C01 = 0 .049 MPa ;four 1 × 1 mm fixed corner patches on the cap underside; remaining underside in frictionless rigid plane; SOLID187 elements with mesh size min(0 .4 mm , h/ 2) ). For each of the four nub heights, we ramp Fz to a preload between 1

and 5 N and then ramp Fx to 1 N while holding Fz , giving 20 loading cases.

4) Simulated Effect of Nub Height: Because nub height changes both the compliance and lever arm, we compare the four heights under the same loading conditions in Fig. 3(B). Fig. 3(B.i) shows that taller nubs require more indentation to support the same normal force and are therefore more compliant. Despite this change in stiffness, Fig. 3(B.ii) shows that most of the applied normal force reaches the nub footprint for every height. At Fz = 5 N , Σ decreases from 5.00 N for the 0.6 mm nub to 4.44 N for the 

3.0 mm nub, while the remaining load is carried outside the footprint. In contrast, Fig. 3(B.iii) shows a strong increase in the differential response with nub height. The simulation therefore predicts that nub height primarily controls shear gain while preserving most of the normal force transmitted to the taxel footprint. This result motivates the experimental height sweep in Section IV-C. 

C. Sensor Fabrication 

The fabrication process involves only Smooth-On Dragon Skin 30 silicone molding, bonding via commercially avail-able glue, and simple assembly. No equipment is needed other than a 3D printer for molds (we used a Bambu Lab P1S for PLA molds), a degassing chamber (in our case a generic vacuum chamber under 150 USD), and readily available tools

such as a syringe, plastic clamps, and a lab scale. Fig. 4 shows the complete procedure. During molding, a 3D printed 0.4 mm -thick PLA dummy FPC approximates the combined thickness of the sensor FPC and resistive layer and reserves a matching insertion pocket. The real sensing stack then slides into this pocket, so the mold geometry aligns the FPC, resistive layer, and silicone. Two bonding materials are used to secure the stack while preserving its local compliance. Because silicone adheres poorly to the FPC, cyanoacrylate glue dots bond the resistive layer to the FPC at the marked positions between sensing regions. This glue is not used between the resistive layer and molded silicone layer because it locally hardens the silicone and constrains its deformation. Instead, a small batch of Dragon Skin 30 is injected through the mold openings to bond the resistive layer to the silicone layer. Both bonding materials remain outside the electrode areas, leaving the sensing interfaces locally compliant for pressure redistribution. Because the process adds no rigid backing and confines bonding to small regions, the assembled sensing tile remains flexible. IV. S ENSOR CHARACTERIZATION 

We characterize the physical sensor performance, and test whether the mechanically separable response predicted by the simulation remains observable after resistive transduction and electronic readout. We first characterize the resistive layer, then evaluate how nub height and curved mounting affect the raw response and decoded three-axis accuracy. 

A. Transduction of the Resistive Layer 

We first characterized the resistive layer separately. As shown in Fig. 5, the FPC and resistive fabric were mounted directly on an ATI Mini40 F/T sensor without the silicone layer. A guide plate aligned the loading pin with each taxel. The pin covered the four quadrants of one taxel, so the applied normal force and summed counts were divided by four to report the response on a per-quadrant basis. We applied 20 manual loading cycles to each of the 16 taxels. Fig. 5 shows that the ADC response becomes progres-sively less sensitive as the quadrant force approaches 1.25 N .This is not a hard force limit, but additional force above this region produces little change in the reading and is therefore more difficult to resolve accurately. Under the four-quadrant normalization used in the plot, 1.25 N per quadrant corresponds to approximately 5 N per taxel. We therefore selected 1–5 N as the normal-force range for each nub for the characterization of the whole sensor.

D. Practical Calibration & Evaluation 

After selecting the 3.0 mm design, we evaluated whether the sensor could be calibrated using a short manual data collection lasting only about a handful of minutes, and whether this calibration can be useful in less controlled, real-life contact interactions. We mounted VecTile on the ATI Mini40, as shown in Fig. 7(B), and performed 10 fast manual loading interactions on each nub, with a median duration of 0.55 s per interaction. During each interaction, the operator first pressed normally, then moved the contact in a random circular trajectory. The decoder was fitted on data collected at 12.5 Hz, leading to around 1,400 data points in total. Then, nubs were randomly pressed and moved again, leading to 240 testing data points. The manually calibrated model achieved RMSEs of 0.43 N 

for normal force and 0.22 N for shear force per taxel. Fig. 7(B) shows a real-time comparison between the VecTile estimates and the F/T reference. The cross-correlation gave a median VecTile-to-F/T lag of 25 ms , including sensor response, USB acquisition, and quadratic decoding. In addition, we investigated multi-nub force prediction following the manual calibration. The operator randomly pressed down and applied shear forces 65 times with the palm and fingers across portions of the sensor, with 624 frames recorded. A median of 8 nubs were contacted for each press, with 7 of these presses contacting all 16 nubs. Loads up to 15 .54 N and 5.83 N were recorded by the F/T sensor. VecTile predictions are simply taken as sums of per-taxel normal and shear forces. A normal RMSE of 0.71 N 

and a shear RMSE of 0.43 N were achieved. V. S ENSOR DEMONSTRATION 

To further demonstrate the practicality of VecTile, we integrated it into two closed-loop robotic manipulation tasks. Task A showcases shear sensitivity by pressing a sharp scalpel against a fully inflated balloon, and retracting once contact is detected without bursting the balloon. Task B evaluates VecTile in a contact-rich plug insertion task using a visuotactile Diffusion Policy [23]. For both tasks, the sensor used the manual fast-calibration method described in the previous section. Demonstration videos are provided as supplementary material. We designed a pair of compliant Fin-Ray type fingers for a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper mounted on the UR5e, as shown in Fig. 8. The fingers were printed from generic Amazon Basics 95A TPU using a Prusa MK3S printer. We attached VecTile arrays with 3.0 mm nubs to each finger. Their flexibility conforms to the bendable finger surfaces. 

A. Shear-Aware Scalpel–Balloon Reflex 

In an initial trial that deliberately burst the balloon, the gripper held a scalpel and moved it toward an inflated balloon at 5 mm /s. The estimated shear-force change, summed over the 16 taxels of one fingertip along the loading direction, reached approximately 0.5 N before rupture (Fig. 8(A.i)). We then set a per-taxel shear threshold of 0.1 N and conducted the reflex experiment (Fig. 8(A.ii)). The robot arm retracted when the change in shear-force magnitude at any taxel exceeded this threshold. Across 20 repeated trials, scalpel contact triggered retraction before puncture in every instance. In a matched normal-only condition using the same motion and threshold, the normal-force change did not reach the threshold before the balloon burst. 

B. Visuotactile Plug Insertion 

We selected plug insertion to evaluate VecTile in contact-rich manipulation, Fig. 8(B.i) shows the setup. The task began with the UK 3-pin plug held by the gripper and required the robot to align and insert it into a socket. A wrist camera (Intel RealSense D435i) and a fixed scene camera (Maxhub UC W20) provided two views.

We collected 84 demonstrations by teleoperating the robot with an Xbox controller at six socket locations spanning an approximately 100 × 30 mm region. Each location con-tributed 7 direct (no corrective adjustment needed) and 7 corrective (direct insertion failed, followed by small adjust-ments) insertion trajectories. Independent ResNet-18 [24] encoders mapped each RGB view to a 512-dimensional feature. Each tactile observation comprised 96 baseline-subtracted channels ( 2 fingers × 16 

taxels × 3 axes), which we projected onto 16 principal components (PCs) fitted using the training data. Following the vision-conditioned Diffusion Policy architecture [23], we combined the visual features with the tactile PCs, end-effector pose, and gripper width. The policy predicted action chunks containing Cartesian pose and gripper commands, which were executed at 10 Hz . We trained each policy for 60 epochs and evaluated three conditions: (i) vision only; (ii) vision with normal-only tactile input (using PCA fitted on the 32 normal channels); and (iii) vision with the full 3-DoF tactile input. For each of the three conditions, we conducted 25 rollouts at socket locations unseen during training but within the spatial range covered by the training data. A rollout was considered successful if the plug was seated and its indicator light illuminated within 60 s .Success improved monotonically across the three con-ditions (Table III), while the shear readings helped with correction rather than first-attempt alignment. Fig. 8(B.ii) shows a recorded, typical contact sequence during a successful rollout with full 3-DoF tactile sensing, learned solely from the demonstrations. In most cases, a direct insertion failed, leading to a finding hole step (1). The recorded rotational shear pattern was consistent with the longer earth pin contacting first and causing the plug to experience pivotal force. During this step, the learned policy led to small movements being made, until the earth pin is par-tially engaged with the socket hole. Then, an alignment step (2) prepared the plug for insertion. At the beginning of this step, the concentrated normal and shear forces on the right finger were consistent with asymmetric contact, providing directional information for adjustment. The shear response decreased during the final descent (3) as the aligned plug entered the socket. Once fully seated, the shear field opposed the insertion direction, signaling the policy to stop (4). The tactile response returned toward baseline after release (5). These signals captured contact transitions that were occluded from the cameras and provided state information absent from the normal-only condition. Neither VecTile showed visible damage throughout these contact-rich experiments, despite a material cost below 4 USD per sensing surface.

VI. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced VecTile, a flexible 4 × 4 tactile array that recovers three-axis force at each taxel using passive silicone nubs to convert shear into a measurable pressure difference across four piezoresistive quadrants. The design requires no specialized fabrication equipment beyond a 3D printer and a hobby-grade vacuum chamber, and costs under 35 USD per unit (under 15 USD with a cheaper microcontroller). Finite element analysis showed that normal and shear loading remain mechanically separable and that nub height primarily controls shear gain; hardware experiments confirmed this, with the 3.0 mm design reaching 0.39 N normal and 0.16 N shear RMSE over 1–5 N normal and up to 1.5 N shear. A de-coder fitted in a flat configuration transferred to R = 85 mm convex and concave mounts with an average RMSE increase of 0.06 N, and a handful of minutes manual calibration against an F/T reference proved sufficient for practical use. Two demonstrations showed the value of the shear channel: a shear-triggered reflex stopped a scalpel before puncturing an inflated balloon in 20 of 20 trials, where normal force alone did not, and adding 3-DoF tactile input to a Diffusion Policy raised plug-insertion success from 48% (vision only) and 64% (normal force only) to 88%. Several limitations remain. The resistive layer loses sensi-tivity above roughly 5 N per taxel, bounding the force range, and we did not yet characterize hysteresis, drift, or long-term durability. Future work will therefore examine whether other readily available elastomers or alternative nub geometries can extend this range and sharpen the shear response, as well as quantify time-dependent effects systematically. Furthermore, the general transducer mechanism and fabrication procedure allow for easy form factor extension. We have already started designing a VecTile version that can be easily cut into a desired footprint via scissors. Finally, we plan to benchmark VecTile against commercial tactile sensors and to test whether its readings can be mapped into sensor-agnostic tactile representations such as those proposed in [25], [26].
