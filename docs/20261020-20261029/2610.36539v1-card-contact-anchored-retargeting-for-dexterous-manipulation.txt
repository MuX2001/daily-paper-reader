Abstract
Humans are dexterous, but traditional data collection
paradigms such as teleoperation, kinesthetic teaching, and
wearable exoskeletons force humans to act through the robot
embodiment, bottlenecking their dexterity. Retargeting can
avoid this. However, existing methods purely translate kine-
matics and ignore the real-world contact dynamics essen-
tial for task success. We present CARD: Contact-Anchored
Retargeting for Dexterous Manipulation, a simulator and
object-free framework that retargets human demonstrations
to robot actions by explicitly preserving contact force targets.
CARD unifies human data sources including video, motion
capture, and tactile gloves via a shared hand–contact rep-
resentation and generates explicit contact force targets in
addition to kinematics. To maintain these contact targets

1. Introduction
Human demonstrations are the most scalable source of data
for dexterous manipulation, yet nearly every way we collect
them today, whether teleoperation [10, 77, 89], wearable
exoskeletons [11, 79, 87], or kinesthetic teaching [8, 63],
forces humans to act through a robot embodiment, limiting
dexterity to what the interface allows. Retargeting natural
human motion removes this bottleneck. However, existing
methods [22, 56] treat retargeting as a kinematic problem of
mapping fingertip positions or joint angles. Further, retarget-
ing often requires scene reconstruction followed by heavy
optimization in simulation, and even recent contact-centric
formulations [33, 66, 84] still presuppose an object model or
a simulator. For manipulation with contact, reconstructing
kinematics alone is not enough: millimeters of embodiment
mismatch decide whether a replayed trajectory hovers, slips
over, or crushes the object.
We present CARD, a retargeting framework that treats con-
tact force targets as a first-class citizen. Instead of relying
on object geometry, CARD uses per-link contact force targets
extracted from a human demonstration, comprising a cen-
ter of pressure, a push direction, and a force, to solve for
robot configurations that place semantically matched robot
links on every contact force target inside the demonstrated
friction cone. CARD produces contact force targets alongside
the joint trajectory without relying on object information,
therefore it needs no simulator, object mesh, or object pose
estimate. Consequently, rigid, articulated, and deformable
objects need no special handling.
CARD is agnostic to the demonstration source: any modal-
ity containing hand pose and contact can be used.
To
make heterogeneous sources interchangeable, we introduce
a unified hand–contact representation that turns monocular
video (through Vid-HACO, our video contact model), tactile
gloves, and marker-based motion capture into a common
interface: a MANO mesh sequence with one contact force
target per hand region. Because every contact force target is
bound to the human hand surface, retargeting to a new robot
requires only a semantic map from hand regions to robot
bodies.
On hardware, CARD pairs retargeting with a tactile
tracking controller, an integral admittance loop over joint
impedance that maintains the demonstrated contact forces
during replay. Together they enable open-loop replay of
dexterous human demonstrations on multiple robot embodi-
ments mounted on a 7-DoF arm. Here open-loop means no
object pose estimate and no learned policy. The only feed-
back is the fingertip force loop that maintains the demon-
strated contact forces. We prioritize open-loop replay to
demonstrate the retargeting quality of CARD over policy learn-
ing, since a closed-loop policy confounds retargeting fidelity
with other issues such as learning error, partial observability
and the like.
Concretely, our contributions are:
• Contact-Anchored Retargeting: A simulator and object-
free formulation that optimizes contact reach, friction-cone
alignment, force weighting, and contact stability per frame
with a fast GPU sampling optimizer.
• Unified Hand–Contact Representation: A shared in-
terface that maps video (through Vid-HACO, our video
hand-contact model), tactile gloves, and motion capture
into contact force targets with whole-clip force synthesis
for sources that measure no force.
• Tactile Tracking Controller: A real-world force tracking
controller with feedforward force that replays contact-rich
tasks open-loop faithfully on physical hardware.
• Cross-Embodiment Generalization: Cross-embodiment
retargeting through a semantic map, shown on four robot
hands from a 22-DoF dexterous hand down to a parallel-
jaw gripper.
Across video, glove, and motion-capture sources, CARD
achieves state-of-the-art contact fidelity, matching demon-
strated contact on 43–78% of contact frames where kine-
matic retargeting reaches under 10%, while retargeting meth-
ods needing object information [18] only reach 22.5%. Fur-
ther, our trajectories transfer to the real world through open-
loop replay, where the tactile tracker raises success from 4
to 25 of 30 trials over position-only replay.

Kinematic retargeting.

an object reference. Neither had released code at the time
of submission, so we cannot compare against them. CARD
keeps the optimization view but optimizes contact locations,
push directions, and forces.
Contact-based retargeting. Contact Transfer [32] and its
sequel [33] transfer contact areas instead of joint angles.
Concurrent work anchors retargeting on contact structure,
under the same name [84] or in the object frame [66], or on
demonstrated forces [2, 78]. Each needs an object model, a
simulator, or a learned tracker. CARD needs none, and adds a
push direction and a force to every contact.
Dynamic retargeting.
A complementary line transfers
demonstrations through simulation with the object in the
loop [9, 14, 34, 39, 40, 42, 43], optimizes robot and object
trajectories jointly [82], or rewards contact wrenches com-
puted on the object [90]. These methods capture contact
dynamics in simulation but need an object model and a sim-
ulator per task. CARD needs neither, since its contact force
targets are obtained directly in the real world.
Learning from human data. Many pipelines recover hand
and object pose from video or wearable capture devices and
retarget kinematically for imitation or RL [19, 24, 25, 44, 50,
55, 64, 74]. Concurrent ones preserve contact, but anchored
on a reconstructed object [45, 49, 59, 68]. Hardware inter-
faces [11, 17, 79, 89] make collection cheaper but tie the
human to a device built per robot. Human contact and force
encode grasp function [5, 6, 69, 70] and improve contact-
rich learning [1, 12, 26, 38], yet retargeting rarely preserves
them, which is the gap CARD closes.
Dexterous hand controllers. Tactile and force feedback
improve multifingered control, from contact-event grasp con-
trollers [60] and tactile-driven policies [21, 27] to tactile
servoing and grasp-force regulation [35, 36, 73]. These con-
trollers regulate forces chosen by a policy or a heuristic.
CARD outputs tactile targets from a demonstration, so a robot
hand can replay it open-loop.
Contact and force from vision. Dense hand contact has
been predicted from hand–object geometry [20] and from
images [30], and concurrently from video together with pres-
sure [29, 47] or force [28]. Forces have also been fitted to
the tracked object’s wrench [52, 53]. CARD consumes any
contact source and synthesizes forces from grasp balance
without an object model.
3. Method
Given a demonstration with hand pose and contact, CARD
(Fig. 2) produces a robot trajectory that reproduces its con-
tact force targets, not only its kinematics. Per frame we
solve for the hand pose q = (t, R, θ) ∈R3 × SO(3) × RN:
wrist position, wrist rotation as an axis–angle vector, and the
N joint angles (N=22 for the SHARPA Wave), without a
physics simulator or object model as input. Every contact
force target is bound to the hand surface, so deformable and
articulated objects can be handled without additional burden.
Hats (ˆ) denote unit vectors, q⋆denotes the optimal solution
of a frame, q−denotes the previous frame’s solution.
3.1. Unified Hand–Contact Representation
Every data source is reduced to three components (Fig. 11):
a MANO [61] hand mesh sequence, a vocabulary of seman-
tic links (3 regions per finger, 15 finger regions plus the
palm region, see Appendix D for details) shared by every
source and every robot, and at most one contact force target
per link and frame, denoted by Cf = (cf, ˆdf, Ff), where f
is the finger link and F the full robot link set. The center
of pressure cf ∈R3 is constrained onto link surface f, ˆdf
denotes a unit push direction into the object, and Ff ≥0
denotes the force magnitude. Video does not provide con-
tact force, so we synthesize it from the contact predictions.
Source-specific processing into the shared representation is
detailed in Appendix A.
3.2. Vid-HACO: Contact from Monocular Video
For monocular video, we introduce Vid-HACO, a video-
based hand–object contact estimation model that predicts
contact probabilities for all 778 MANO vertices, enabling
video demonstrations to be converted into our unified
hand–contact representation. Given an 8-frame clip end-
ing at frame t, Vid-HACO uses hand crops obtained from
HaWoR [88], a world-frame extension of HaMeR [51]. A
video-pretrained V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-L encoder [48] and a DPT
decoder [57] extract multi-scale features. Two prediction
heads estimate vertex-level contact logits and a frame-level
binary contact indicator, respectively. Temporal context
helps distinguish contact transitions during approach, grasp,
and release.
Following HACO [30], we use a 10 mm distance thresh-
old to label vertices as contacting the touched surface and
set the probability threshold to 0.52 for contact prediction.
We train on 1.55M frames from 12 datasets spanning ego-
centric, third-person, and web views, holding out 10% of
each dataset for validation. We fine-tune the encoder end
to end with HACO’s class-balanced vertex loss [13] and
an additional binary cross-entropy loss on the frame head,
retaining contact-free frames during training. Dataset and
implementation details appear in Appendix B.

Style, smoothness, collision. When a finger is unconstrained
by contact force targets, Estyle encourages the robot hand
to mimic human motion. Specifically, Estyle pulls each
robot pad toward its corresponding MANO fingertip, aligns
the pad’s belly axis with the MANO surface normal, and
aligns individual finger segment orientations with the hu-
man pose. Esmooth, on the other hand, applies a quadratic
penalty on joint motion relative to the previous frame to
maintain temporal continuity. Finally, Ecollision uses a soft
hinge barrier to penalize hand self-collisions and object pene-
trations. Inter-finger clearance uses capsule-to-capsule gaps.
Object penetration queries pad samples against an object
signed distance field (SDF) or, without a mesh (video, glove,
GRAB), against a pseudo-object SDF built from the convex
hull of the clip’s contact force targets cf in the wrist frame
(Fig. 12, Appendix D). Sampling-based optimization. Be-
cause the objective in Eq. (1) is non-smooth, we minimize it
using zeroth-order optimization—specifically, an annealed
Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) [54, 75] whose noise sched-
ule follows the diffusion-style annealing of DIAL-MPC [80].
Each frame is warm-started with the geometric initialization
qinit. At each of the K = 16 iterations, N = 1024 candidate
configurations are sampled around the mean, clipped to the
feasible set Q, and evaluated in a single parallelized pass.
The mean is then updated using a softmax weighting over the
top 5% lowest-cost candidates while noise is progressively
annealed. A zero-phase Savitzky–Golay filter (15 frames)
smooths the result. The solver runs at 10 FPS on one RTX
5090.

4. Experiments
We evaluate CARD across five aspects: contact fidelity against
kinematic baselines (Sec. 4.1), force preservation in open-
loop hardware replay (Sec. 4.2), transfer across embodiments
(Sec. 4.3), contact estimation from video (Sec. 4.4), and the
performance of a physics-based retargeting variant, CARD–
physics (Sec. 4.5). We also provide additional ablations and
contact analyses: Appendices B.2, E and H.
4.1. Contact fidelity against kinematic retargeting
Data. We evaluate on two datasets: clips captured in house
(Fig. 3: 20 casual phone videos, 18 tactile glove with VIVE
tracker clips, and 48 motion-capture with tactile glove clips)
and 100 public GRAB clips [70] with ground-truth object
meshes. Every method is retargeted to the 22-DoF SHARPA
Wave.
Metrics. A contact is considered established when a contact
force target lies within 2 mm of the assigned link surface
and the nearest pad sample is on the palmar face. Contact
rate measures the fraction of anchor-frames with established
contact. Correct-region rate further requires contact on the
correct region according to the demonstration, while in-cone
rate additionally requires the pad normal to align within 45◦
of the demonstrated push direction. Gap is the median dis-
tance from the contact force target to the pad and penetration
the median deepest incursion into the object (Appendix G).
Baselines.
We compare against the kinematic retarget-
ing baselines: DexPilot [22] and the two objectives of
AnyTeleop [56], key-vector (AnyTeleop-K) and fingertip-
position (AnyTeleop-P). We also report ReGrind [18] (w/ Ob-
ject), a contact-preserving retargeting method that requires
the object mesh, and CARD’s geometric initialization (init
only). TopoRetarget [76] and Morphometric Imitation [62]
have not released code at submission time and are therefore
not compared.
Results.
Table 1 compares contact fidelity across both
datasets. Kinematic baselines establish the demonstrated
contact on only 3–10% of anchor-frames, with a median
gap of 10–23 mm. In contrast, CARD establishes contact
on 61% of anchor-frames on video, 78% on the glove and
motion-capture clips, and 43% on GRAB, while reducing
the gap to 1.4–3.6 mm. Importantly, nearly all established
contacts occur on the correct finger, and over half of anchor-
frames also satisfy the in-cone constraint. On GRAB, where
ground-truth object meshes allow us to measure penetration,
CARD reaches 43% contact against 22.5% for ReGrind, which
takes the object mesh as input, and also produces the least
object penetration (7.96 mm) (Fig. 4). We also find that con-
tact fidelity correlates with contact signal quality: CARD can
achieve 89% contact on the tactile glove clips, where contact
is measured, 76% on motion capture, 61% on video, where it
is inferred, and 43% on GRAB, where contact is calculated
from geometric proximity (Appendix H).
4.2. Open-loop replay on hardware
To directly evaluate the fidelity of CARD’s retargeted actions,
we replay the trajectories open-loop on real hardware, elimi-
nating confounding factors such as policy learning. Under
this setting, task success depends directly on the faithful re-
production of task-critical contacts and forces. Fig. 7 shows
sequential snapshots of our hardware replays, including pick-
ing up a deformable card from a deck and rotating an artic-
ulated Rubik’s Cube with a single finger. All tasks use our
tactile tracking controller to preserve contact forces. Full
videos are available on our project website.
Tactile tracker ablation. We replay identical CARD trajec-
tories on the SHARPA Wave with and without the tactile
tracker on three real-world tasks, 10 trials each (Fig. 6).
Position-only replay succeeds on 1/10 hammer lifts, 3/10 cup
lifts, and 0/10 mustard dispenses: the hammer and cup slip
out during the lift while the bottle is lifted but not squeezed.
With the tactile tracker the same trajectories succeed on 8/10,
10/10, and 7/10 respectively. Reproducing the joint motion
alone does not reproduce the intended contact forces.

5. Conclusion
We presented CARD, a simulator- and object-free retar-
geting framework that preserves task-critical contacts and
forces across diverse human demonstration sources and
robot embodiments. Combined with a tactile tracking con-
troller, CARD enables faithful open-loop hardware replay
across rigid, articulated, and deformable objects. Our results
demonstrate substantial gains in contact fidelity and task suc-
cess, establishing contact preservation as a scalable interface
for transferring human dexterity to robots.
Limitations and future work.
Retargeting quality is
bounded by the input demonstration, and open-loop replay
cannot recover from a large object displacement. We will use
CARD-generated data to train closed-loop sensorimotor poli-
cies that close this gap, and to scale human demonstrations
into contact-preserving robot data.

Demonstrations
Data sources. Vision: A monocular phone video is tracked
in the world frame with HaWoR [88], while per-vertex hand–
object contact is predicted by Vid-HACO (Secs. 3.2 and 4.4).
Hand segmentation and metric scale are obtained using off-
the-shelf models [58, 81]. Tactile glove: A 744-taxel pres-
sure grid provides measured contact and pressure, with each
taxel registered to a personalized MANO mesh, and a wrist-
mounted tracker provides hand motion. Motion capture
with tactile glove: The same tactile glove is worn under
OptiTrack markers, so the glove supplies the contact and
pressure while the markers provide drift-free wrist motion,
together with a tracked object mesh used only for evaluation.
GRAB [70] and ARCTIC [16] are incorporated through their
native annotations and loaders.
From sensor measurements to contact force targets. All
three sources are converted into the same contact force tar-

link, a set of contacting MANO vertices v with a weight

counts equally. GRAB provides contact from hand–object
proximity in place of pressure. The center of pressure cf fol-
lows in three steps. (i) The contacting vertices are expressed
in the link’s 2-D pad chart, with the bone as the x axis
and the pad normal as z, and reduced to the weighted cen-

The initializer is deterministic, so a steady source gives
a steady start, and it costs under a millisecond per frame.
It enters the solve only as half of the sampler’s warm start.
None of the cost groups is evaluated inside it, and the style
group scores the same quantities—pad apex, pad normal,
link direction—in task space during the solve. Figure 9
shows the initializer beside the solved pose. We also tested
the converse designs: a per-finger least squares on the style

metric. The initializer’s joint copy used as the free-finger
reward in place of the style group cannot place fingertips
and loses contact rate. The division of labour between a
direction-exact initializer and a task-space style reward is
therefore kept.

Cross-embodiment semantic maps. A semantic map lists,

Friction identification.
The feedforward torque only
reaches the fingertip if joint friction is known, so we iden-

sated as a per-joint, per-direction Coulomb-plus-viscous
model with a stiction breakaway threshold, fit from constant-
velocity sweeps and breakaway ramps in simulation on the
hand’s own model and validated against its ground-truth fric-
tion parameters. It is evaluated on commanded rather than
measured velocity.
Arm. The arm tracks the retargeted wrist by differential

H. Contact fidelity by demonstration source
Table 8 splits the clips of Tab. 1 by how the contact signal
was obtained: measured taxel pressure on the glove, contact
inferred by Vid-HACO on video, proximity-thresholded con-
tact on our motion capture, and GRAB, whose contact comes
from the ground-truth meshes. CARD follows the quality of
the contact signal: it is strongest where contact is measured
(89.1% contact, 52.2% in-cone, 1.5 mm gap on the glove),
then on tracked motion capture (76.1%), weaker on video
(61.4%), where Vid-HACO infers the contact and a wrong
label places the robot pad on empty space, and weakest on
GRAB (43.1%), whose proximity labels carry no pressure
and no true center of pressure. The kinematic baselines stay
at 1–10% on every source and move the other way, best on
video (7.6–9.7%) and worst on the glove (1.3–1.8%), which
is what methods that track keypoints should do: they gain
nothing from a better contact signal. The geometric initial-
izer, CARD’s own deterministic warm start, already exceeds
every baseline on every source (12.2–43.2%): a human-
isomorphic pose places centers of pressure inside the link
far more reliably than fitting scaled fingertip targets does.
I. CARD–physics
Setup. SPIDER [71] retargets in two stages. A kinematic
stage fits the robot fingertips to the MANO keypoints by
inverse kinematics and synthesizes contact targets from a
replay of those keypoints; a physics stage rolls the hand and
the object forward in MuJoCo with a sampling-based model-
predictive controller that tracks the kinematic reference and
rewards contact. CARD-physics keeps the physics stage and
its budget (256 samples and 8 iterations per 2 s segment,
early-abort gate off so that every rollout covers the whole
demonstration) and changes three things. (1) The sampler is
initialized with, and tracks, the CARD trajectory of Sec. 3.3 in
place of the fingertip-IK reference; the object track and the
resampling, padding, and smoothing of SPIDER’s kinematic
stage are kept, so both arms follow the identical object ref-
erence. (2) SPIDER’s fingertip contact term is replaced by
our contact objective Econtact(q) of Eq. (3): the squared dis-
tance of each contacting pad to its contact force target enters
the reward with weight 10 and the 45◦friction-cone term on
the demonstrated push with weight 2, relative to SPIDER’s
tracking terms; the anti-slip term is omitted. (3) The contact
force targets come from our pipeline (Appendix A) with the
geometric contact rule used for GRAB, a contact probability
of 1 −d/9 mm at hand–object distance d, thresholded at 0.5.
SPIDER needs the object mesh and pose track to run at all,
so the anchors add no information the baseline lacks.

Scoring. SPIDER’s own criterion, a final object pose within
10 cm and 90◦of the reference, is met by rollouts that never
touch an object that returns near its start, so we score each
run post hoc from its trajectory. A run succeeds when it
covers the whole demonstration, the object travels at least
half the demonstrated distance and, if the demonstration
lifts it by 5 cm or more, rises by at least 3 cm and half the
demonstrated lift, and the peak object position error stays
below 15 cm. A task is solved when at least one seed suc-
ceeds; runs with invalid physics do not count. Table 3 reports
task success. Per run, CARD-physics succeeds on 51% of the
motion-capture runs against 39% for SPIDER and on 43%
of the GRAB runs against 10%, with median peak object
errors of 106 vs. 138 mm and 120 vs. 274 mm. The rollouts
show the mechanism: the IK reference forms thumb–finger
opposition only after the object has started to move, so the
sampler must find the grasp on its own, whereas the CARD
reference encloses the object at the demonstrated contact
onset and the sampler only has to squeeze.
