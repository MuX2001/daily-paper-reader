# Some quartic control results for scalar-input systems 

## Karine Beauchard ∗

## , Fr´ ed´ eric Marbach †

## August 31, 2026 

Abstract 

We investigate the role of quartic terms in the small-time local controllability of scalar-input systems. First, we prove a new sufficient condition for controllability, which exploits simultaneously more good quartic Lie brackets than previous results. Second, we identify a family of quartic obstructions to controllability, relying on Lie brackets whose coordinates of the second kind are positive-definite functionals of the control. Third, we show that the complementarity of these results can be seen as an answer to Kawski’s 1987 open problem concerning the construction of a Hall basis which somehow separates good and bad quartic brackets. We give examples and remarks illustrating some of the intricacies of these notions.

## 1.1 Scalar-input control-affine systems 

In this article, we consider a scalar-input affine control system ˙x(t) = f0(x(t)) + u(t)f1(x(t)) (1.1) where the state x(t) belongs to Rd (d ≥ 1), the control is a scalar input u(t) ∈ R, f0 and f1 are vector fields on Rd, real-analytic on a neighborhood of 0, such that f0(0) = 0. These are standing assumptions throughout the paper. Nevertheless, we expect that the analyticity assumption can be removed using the arguments of [8, Section 11]. For each T > 0 and u ∈ L1((0 , T ); R), the Cauchy problem (1.1) with initial condition x(0) = 0 admits a unique maximal absolutely continuous solution, denoted by x(·; u). We will consider small enough controls and small enough times so that this solution is defined up to time T .

## 1.5 Organization of the two parts of the paper 

To facilitate understanding, our paper is split into two parts. 

• Part I concerns the case of smooth-STLC. In this case, the classification of good and bad brackets is satisfying, the statements and proofs are rather straightforward, and one can focus on the key ideas. We describe the corresponding results below. 

• Part II concerns the case of rough-STLC, which in particular includes the usual historical 

L∞-STLC notion. In this case, the classification of good and bad brackets is less stringent, the statements are more intricate, and the proofs more technical. The description of our results in this case is postponed to Section 7, including our main obstruction result of Theorem 7.1.

## 2.1 Hall sets 

In this section, we recall the notion of Hall sets and Hall bases. For more details on these bases of 

L(X), we refer to [4, 13], [35, Chapter 4] or [41, Chapter 1]. These bases are particularly useful for control theory as they are linked with Lazard’s elimination process [28] and Sussmann’s infinite product expansion [39] (see [5, Section 2.5]). 

Definition 2.1 (Length, left and right factors) . For b ∈ Br( X), |b| denotes the length of b. If 

|b| > 1, b can be written in a unique way as b = ( b1, b 2), with b1, b 2 ∈ Br( X). We use the notations 

λ(b) = b1 and μ(b) = b2, which define maps λ, μ : Br( X) \ X → Br( X).

Definition 2.2 (Hall set) . A Hall set is a subset B of Br( X), totally ordered by a relation < and such that 

• X ⊂ B ,

• for b = ( b1, b 2) ∈ Br( X), b ∈ B iff b1, b 2 ∈ B , b1 < b 2 and either b2 ∈ X or λ(b2) ≤ b1,

• for every b1, b 2 ∈ B such that (b1, b 2) ∈ B , one has b1 < (b1, b 2).

The main interest of Hall sets is that their images by e yield algebraic bases of L(X), called Hall bases, as proved in [41, Corollary 1.1, Proposition 1.1 and Theorem 1.1]. 

Theorem 2.3 (Viennot) . Let B ⊂ Br( X) be a Hall set. Then e(B) is a basis of L(X).

Definition 2.4 (Support) . Let B be a Hall set of Br( X) and a ∈ L (X). For b ∈ B , we denote by 

⟨a, b ⟩B the coefficient along e(b) in the decomposition of a on the basis e(B). We define 

supp B(a) := {b ∈ B ; ⟨a, b ⟩B̸ = 0 } . (2.1) 

If A ⊂ L (X), we denote by supp B(A) := ∪a∈A supp B(a). We drop the subscripts B when there is no possible confusion on which basis is used. 

The following structural property follows from the classical recursive rewriting algorithm within Hall bases, described for example in [4, Section 2.1] or [36, Section 9]. 

Lemma 2.5. Let B be a Hall set of Br( X) and b1 < b 2 ∈ B . Then, either (b1, b 2) ∈ B , or any 

b ∈ supp B[b1, b 2] satisfies λ(b) > b 1. In both cases, each b ∈ supp B[b1, b 2] satisfies λ(b) ≥ b1.Proof. This is item (2.2) of [4, Theorem 2.1].

# 7 Main results 

We describe the main results of Part II. In this part, one fixes a priori some regularity index m ∈ J−1, ∞J quantifying the expected smallness of the control, and looks for sufficient or necessary conditions for W m, ∞-STLC. The case m = 0 (so controls small in L∞) was the most commonly studied in the literature (see e.g. [15, Definition 3.2] or STLC ε in [22]). It is quite natural when one has in mind to transfer the results concerning the control-affine system (1.1) to systems of the form ˙ x = f (x, u ). The case m = −1 is heuristically close to the historical setting (as in [40]) where one fixes a neighborhood e.g. U = [ −1, 1] of 0 of admissible values for u(t), and then lets T → 0. It is equivalent to the small-state STLC for scalar-input systems (see [6, Section 8.2]). More generally, studying obstructions for a fixed regularity index can be relevant for applications where the control’s regularity depends on physical constraints (speed, acceleration, jerk, ...) or when the functional framework is determined by the well-posedness theory of the considered partial differential equation (see the survey [2]). Our main result for rough-STLC is the necessary condition Theorem 7.1 involving the bad Lie bracket Qj,k,k , which is the counterpart of Theorem 1.16 in the smooth-STLC case. It involves a threshold M , of which we prove the optimality, as well as a geometric assumption. We present in Section 7.2 examples illustrating that, without this geometric assumption, the “bad” Lie bracket 

Qj,k,k can yield a controllable direction. These results hint that the classification problem discussed in Section 5 might be considerably more intricate in the case of rough-STLC than smooth-STLC.

## 9.1 Paradoxical results? 

The example of system (7.2) shows that, when k ≥ 2j, the “bad” Lie bracket Qj,k,k can actually be used to obtain controllability at low regularity, thanks to the presence of fWj (0) at a very particular position within the system. Let us try to explain this apparent paradox. As explained in Section 4.1, the quartic obstructions we prove rely on the coercivity of the coordinate of the second kind ξb(t, u ) of the concerned “bad” Lie bracket b ∈ B ⋆. As stated in Theorem 2.17, the coordinate involved in the expansion of the state x(t; u) is not ξb(t, u ), but the one of the pseudo-first kind ηb(t, u ). In many situations, we are able to bound the difference between 

ξb(t, u ) and ηb(t, u ) thanks to closed-loop estimates (see e.g. Proposition 8.8 in combination with Proposition 8.13) and this difference turns out to be negligible for sufficiently regular controls, using interpolation inequalities. Unfortunately, it is known that there are situations where one cannot ignore the difference between these two coordinates (see [8, Section 4.5]). The examples studied in this section fall in this context. More precisely, the presence of fWj (0) saturates the mentioned closed-loop estimates, and the lack of regularity of the control prevents being able to use interpolation theory to ne-glect the difference. This somehow justifies why, despite the coercivity of ξb(t, u ), one can obtain controllability in the directions ±fb(0), therefore relying on the fact that ηb(t, u ) is onto.
