Abstract 

This paper presents a method for computing inner polytopic approximations of admissible sets for continuous-time linear control systems subject to affine state constraints. Building upon barrier theory and the explicit solution of linear systems, a structured sampling procedure is developed to generate boundary samples of the admissible set directly, thereby avoiding the discretization of large regions of the state space required by many viability and reachability methods. The proposed framework computes admissible sets for the individual state constraints separately and combines the resulting inner approximations by inter-section, avoiding the explicit treatment of simultaneous active constraints. The boundary samples are converted into a half-space representation, and runtime complexity analyses are derived for both the sampling and polytope reconstruc-tion algorithms. An extension of the framework to Hammerstein systems is also presented. The proposed approach is demonstrated on a triple integrator and a higher-dimensional mass-spring-damper chain, illustrating the geometric construction of the admissible set approximation and demonstrating scalability to higher-dimensional systems.

# 1 Introduction 

Admissible sets play a central role in constrained control, since they describe the set of initial conditions for which the state constraints can be satisfied for all future times under a suitable input. Hence, their computation are of interest in constrained stabilization, safety verification, and optimization-based control. However, the con-struction of admissible sets remains computationally challenging, in particular for high-dimensional systems. The difficulty stems from the fact that the admissible set is defined by an infinite-time constraint satisfaction property and its boundary is generally a non-trivial geometric object. In literature, several classes of methods have been developed to approximate such sets. Grid-based viability and reachability methods, including Hamilton-Jacobi meth-ods, typically rely on discretization of the state space and the computation of an implicit representation of the set on this grid [1–3]. While these methods are power-ful and broadly applicable, their computational cost increases rapidly with the state dimension. Moreover, refinement usually requires recomputation on a finer grid, and the resulting grid-based representation is not always directly suitable for constrained control or optimization unless combined with a set description as proposed in [2]. Another important class of approaches is based on set propagation and reachabil-ity analysis. For instance, reachable sets may be propagated through time with help of support functions [4], or through the construction of ellipsoidal inner approximations [5]. Such methods provide rigorous geometric approximations, but they require the repeated propagation of sets or geometric objects whose complexity depends strongly on the chosen set representation. Similarly, zonotope-based approaches propagate or scale structured set representations to obtain invariant or reachable-set approxima-tions [6, 7]. Hereby, the geometry of zonotopic approximations is determined by the selected generators and the central symmetry property of zonotopes, which might limit their ability to approximate non-symmetric admissible set boundaries. An alternative viewpoint is provided by barrier theory. It provides a charac-terization of the boundary of the admissible set based on trajectories satisfying necessary conditions similar to Pontryagin’s maximum principle. Accordingly, the method directly targets the relevant part of the state space, namely the admissi-ble set boundary. Classical barrier-based methods can compute individual boundary trajectories [8]. However, they do not directly yield a computationally usable set rep-resentation, and obtaining a dense description of the admissible set boundary may require the integration of a large number of barrier trajectories. Furthermore, in higher dimensions, an explicit treatment of intersections and stopping points of barrier trajectories becomes increasingly difficult [9]. This paper combines the geometric boundary characterization provided by barrier theory with a structured sampling strategy for linear control systems. Instead of inte-grating individual barrier trajectories, the proposed sampling algorithm propagates 2a reference trajectory together with the associated affine subspace representing the points of ultimate tangentiality, enabling the efficient generation of boundary samples with prescribed sampling distances. The resulting samples are converted into poly-topic inner approximations of the admissible set in half-space representation, which are directly compatible with constrained control and optimization problems. Furthermore, the proposed framework introduces a decomposition with respect to the individual state constraints. Rather than explicitly treating intersections between barrier trajectories associated with multiple simultaneously active constraints, admis-sible sets are computed for the individual constraints and subsequently combined by intersecting their polytopic inner approximations. This substantially simplifies the construction of admissible sets for systems with multiple state constraints. Finally, run-time complexity analyses are derived for both the structured sampling and polytope reconstruction algorithms. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 formulates the admis-sible set problem, specializes the barrier-theoretic characterization to constrained linear systems and establishes structural properties of the resulting admissible sets. The section concludes with an extension to Hammerstein systems. Section 3 develops the proposed structured sampling algorithm and derives its computational complex-ity. Section 4 presents the construction of polytopic inner approximations in half-space representation from the obtained samples together with the corresponding complex-ity analysis. Numerical results for high-dimensional constrained systems are presented in Section 5, illustrating the proposed algorithms and the computational benefit of exploiting the barrier-induced boundary structure. Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper.

This section summarizes the barrier theory framework for linear control systems and establishes structural properties of the admissible set, which form the basis for the structured sampling procedure developed later in this work to reduce the com-putational effort associated with individual barrier trajectories and to enable the construction of computationally tractable polytopic inner approximations.

# 2.3 Structural Properties of the Admissible Set 

The barrier-theoretic characterization from the previous subsection can be applied directly to systems subject to multiple state constraints by considering all active con-straints simultaneously. However, in this setting, barrier trajectories associated with different constraints may intersect, leading to so-called stopping points [9], where the backward integration must be terminated. Determining such stopping points is, in general, nontrivial and depends strongly on the underlying system dynamics. 6To avoid this difficulty, we instead consider the admissible sets associated with the individual state constraints separately and construct the admissible set corre-sponding to multiple constraints through set intersections. This decomposition also provides an important geometric advantage: the convexity of the individual admissi-ble sets guarantees that convex combinations of boundary samples remain inside the corresponding admissible set. This property later enables the construction of inner polytopic approximations from sampled boundary points. # 3 Structured Barrier Sampling 

While barrier trajectories characterize the boundary of the admissible set, obtaining a computationally usable representation of this boundary remains challenging, particu-larly in higher-dimensional systems. This section develops a structured procedure for efficiently sampling the boundary of linear control systems. A key idea is to compute the admissible set associated with each state constraint separately. Since the admissible set is given by the intersection of the individual admis-sible sets, the full admissible set can be approximated by intersecting the corresponding polytopic inner approximations. This avoids the need to determine stopping points [9] associated with multiple simultaneously active constraints. The structured organization of the boundary samples also allows for a detailed computational complexity analysis of both the sampling procedure and the subsequent polytope construction, which is presented in the following sections.

Remark 4 For comparison, we briefly discuss the computational complexity of several other established set-based methods for computing admissible sets. Methods based on Hamilton-Jacobi equations [1, 3, 14] typically require a discretization of the state space and therefore suffer from the well-known curse of dimensionality, leading to an exponential growth of computational effort with the state dimension. Sampling-based admissible-set approximations, such as the method proposed in [2], avoid full state-space discretizations but require the solution of a feasibility problem for each sam-pled direction and time interval. For a fixed number of time intervals, the complexity reported in [2] scales as 

O (N log( d)Φ( n)) ,

where N denotes the number of sampled directions, d the diameter of the admissible set, and Φ( n) the complexity of the underlying feasibility program. Consequently, the computational 

15 effort depends not only on the number of boundary samples but also on the complexity of the optimization problem solved at each step. Set-propagation approaches based on geometric set representations, e.g., zonotopes [7] or related set-valued techniques like support functions [4], typically perform a sequence of set operations at every propagation step. The complexity of these operations generally increases with both the state dimension and the complexity of the chosen set representation. The barrier theory [8], which forms the basis of the proposed method, computes individual trajectories that run along the boundary of the admissible set. The computational complexity of this approach is primarily determined by the number of barrier trajectories that are prop-agated. While only the ( n − 1)-dimensional barrier is sampled, covering this manifold with a prescribed resolution still requires an exponentially increasing number of barrier trajecto-ries as the state dimension increases. Note that the barrier-based sampling method allows each barrier trajectory to be propagated individually, until it reaches its stopping point [9]. Consequently, the required propagation time may differ between trajectories and is not tied to a single global time horizon. In practical implementations, however, the propagation is terminated after a prescribed finite time horizon if no stopping point has been reached. 

# 4 Construction of Polytopic Inner Approximation 

With the structured sampling procedure, we obtain a finite set of boundary sam-ples of the admissible set. For practical control applications, however, a finite point cloud is typically not a convenient representation. Therefore, in this section, we con-struct a polytopic inner approximation in a halfspace representation from the sampled boundary points, which is suitable for constrained control and optimization problems. 

# 4.1 Polytope Preliminaries 

We briefly recall the notions from polytope theory required for the construction of the proposed inner approximation, see, e.g., [15] for a comprehensive overview. A polytope P ⊂ Rn admits two equivalent representations, see, e.g., [16]. The first is the halfspace ( H-)representation. 

Definition 1 (H-Polytope) A set P ⊂ Rn is called an H-polytope if it can be written as a bounded intersection of finitely many closed halfspaces. Equivalently, there exists a J ∈ N, a matrix AP ∈ RJ×n and a vector bP ∈ RJ such that 

P := {x ∈ Rn | AP x ≤ bP }.

Equivalently, a polytope can be represented in vertex ( V-)representation. 

Definition 2 (V-Polytope) A set P ⊂ Rn is called a V-polytope if it is the convex hull of a finite set of points S ⊂ Rn, i.e., 

P := conv( S).

In the proposed approach, samples generated on the boundary of the admissible set are collected in a finite set Si ⊆ ∂Ai. The corresponding polytopic approximation 16 ˜Ai of Ai is then obtained as ˜Ai = conv( Si).

# 4.3 Runtime Complexity of Conversion Algorithm 

To analyze the computational complexity of Algorithm 2, we use the same assumptions on classical dense linear algebra operations from [12, Chapter 1 ,5] and [13] as in Section 3.3. Let N denote the number of samples in S, n the state dimension and J

the number of facets of the resulting polytope. In Step 1, to verify dim(aff( S)) = n, one typically computes the rank of the matrix [x2 − x1, x 3 − x1, . . . , x N − x1] ∈ Rn×(N −1) .

Computing the rank via SVD or QR decomposition requires O(n2N ) operations. The computational complexity of Algorithm 2 is dominated by the convex hull computation in Step 2. Since the input points S lie on the boundary of the admissible set, all points are potentially vertices of the convex hull. Consequently, the QuickHull algorithm exhibits its worst-case behavior, which scales as O(N 2) for two- or three-dimensional state spaces [17]. For higher dimensions, the complexity grows with the number of facets J of the polytope, which can scale as O(N ⌊n/ 2⌋) according to the Upper Bound Theorem. The computation of the centroid in Step 3 requires O(nN ) operations. The loop in Step 4 runs over all J facets. Computing a facet normal requires solving a linear system or nullspace problem of size n, which has complexity O(n3). The offset computation as well as the orientation test both require a dot product with cost O(n). Thus, Step 4 has an overall complexity of O(Jn 3). # 6 Conclusion 

This paper addressed the computationally efficient construction of inner polytopic approximations of admissible sets for linear control systems subject to affine state con-straints. Building upon the barrier-theoretic characterization of admissible sets in [8], a structured sampling framework was developed to generate boundary samples directly from the explicit solution of linear control systems. The proposed framework further decomposes the admissible set computation into individual state constraints, allowing the corresponding inner polytopic approxima-tions to be constructed separately and subsequently combined by intersection. This avoids the explicit treatment of simultaneous barrier intersections while preserving the barrier-theoretic characterization of the admissible set. The boundary samples are converted into half-space representations using the QuickHull algorithm, and run-time complexity analyses were derived for both the structured sampling and polytope 29 reconstruction algorithms. While the sampling procedure exhibits polynomial com-plexity in the state dimension and the number of time steps, the computational cost of the polytope reconstruction is dominated by the convex hull computation and therefore depends strongly on both the number of samples and the state-space dimen-sion. Finally, an extension of the proposed framework to Hammerstein systems was presented. The proposed method was demonstrated on two examples. For a triple integrator subject to six affine state constraints, the structured sampling procedure and the construction of the corresponding inner polytopic approximation were illustrated in detail. A higher-dimensional mass-spring-damper chain was then used to demonstrate the scalability of the proposed algorithms. Possible future applications include the extension of the proposed framework to nonlinear model predictive control. In particular, the computational efficiency of the structured sampling algorithm may enable the online computation of admissible set approximations based on local linearizations of nonlinear systems. Important questions in this context include quantifying the approximation quality, identifying conditions that guarantee the preservation of the inner approximation property, and determining the time horizons over which admissible sets computed from local linearizations remain valid for the underlying nonlinear dynamics.
