METRIC NON-COLLAPSE IN LEARNED WORLD MODELS FOR CONTROL: APPROXIMATION THEORY, FINITE-SAMPLE GEOMETRIC GUARANTEES, AND DETERMINISTIC PLANNING TRANSFER 

ALAIN BENSOUSSAN, MINH-NHAT PHUNG, AND MINH-BINH TRAN 

Abstract. We develop a three-part mathematical theory for metrically faithful learned world models for nonlinear deterministic control systems. First, on the approximation-theoretic side, we construct smooth exact latent realizations and verify the required finite-capacity C1,1 approximation property for norm-constrained tensor-product B-spline classes with capacity-independent regularity budgets. Sec-ond, on the finite-sample geometric side, we introduce an encoder-only local–global metric hinge whose directional and separated-pair terms prevent infinitesimal collapse and global folding. Although the prediction loss is observation-based, evaluation of this penalty uses state-metric supervision through observable-state distances and tangent directions. Under a regular observable-factor assumption, lower-Ahlfors coverage, and uniform C1,1 budgets, provided the explicit finite-sample deviation lies below the metric-margin threshold, every approximate empirical minimizer above a computable one-sided regular-ization threshold is pointwise co-Lipschitz and satisfies a uniform approximate controlled-semiconjugacy estimate, with approximation, statistical, and training-optimization errors kept separate. The L2-to-L∞

exponent used in this step is sharp under Lipschitz regularity. Third, for deterministic planning trans-fer, metric non-collapse induces compatible Lipschitz latent costs, while semiconjugacy gives uniform trajectory and finite-horizon cost bounds and an optimizer-transfer guarantee; learned latent cost heads enter through explicit compatibility errors. Numerical experiments use bounded latent ranges, archived fixed metric samples, a finite-capacity coefficient-box spline proxy motivated by the theorem, and la-tent model-predictive control on a controlled pendulum. They show that small prediction residuals can coexist with collapsed representations and poor control. The distinctive theoretical contribution is an end-to-end finite-sample implication: an approximately optimized empirical metric objective yields a pointwise co-Lipschitz encoder, a uniform controlled-semiconjugacy certificate, and an explicit deter-ministic optimizer-transfer bound for the same learned model. Together, the results position metric non-collapse as a verifiable geometric mechanism behind reliable latent planning, with experiments il-lustrating the mechanism under controlled finite-sample conditions.

A learned latent world model is useful for control only if it does more than fit one-step data on average. It must retain the state-space distinctions that matter under interventions, and its latent transition must remain uniformly compatible with the controlled dynamics. Otherwise a planner may exploit folds, overlaps, or poorly resolved regions of the representation even when the empirical prediction loss is small. The central question of this paper is therefore control-theoretic: under what conditions does an empirically trained latent model become a metrically faithful approximate realization of a nonlinear deterministic control system, with quantitative guarantees for downstream planning? The model-based reinforcement-learning viewpoint that learned dynamics can support planning and policy improvement goes back to Dyna and underlies modern world-model methods for visual con-trol [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Methods such as PlaNet and Dreamer encode high-dimensional observations into compact latent variables and learn the dynamics in latent space. Joint-embedding predictive architec-tures (JEPA) adopt a related but distinct principle: instead of reconstructing every detail of the next observation, the model predicts its representation [6]. This principle has led to I-JEPA for images [7], V-JEPA for video [8], and V-JEPA 2, which combines video pretraining with robot interaction data to construct an action-conditioned latent model for planning [9]. These developments motivate a precise mathematical problem: when does action-conditioned latent prediction produce both a non-collapsed representation and a latent transition that faithfully represents the underlying controlled system? prediction loss is observation-based, but the metric regularizer studied here uses additional training information. Observable-factor coordinates, or equivalent state distances and tangent directions, are used to evaluate its local and separated-pair terms. The framework is formulated for settings where observable-state metric information is available, including simulator-state, proprioceptive, and state-estimation regimes. Forward prediction alone cannot yield the representation property needed for deterministic control. The constant encoder Φ ≡ z0, together with F ≡ z0, has zero prediction loss. More subtly, positive latent variance or covariance does not prevent folding: a representation may have substantial global spread while identifying distinct states or losing arbitrarily much local metric resolution. For the worst-case deterministic planning guarantees pursued here, the relevant non-collapse condition is not merely distributional. It is a pointwise lower metric estimate of the form 

∥Φ( H(s)) − Φ( H(s′)) ∥Z ≥ c∗|s − s′|, s, s ′ ∈ S, 

which makes the learned representation quantitatively injective on the observable state factor. To enforce this geometry, we introduce an encoder-only local–global metric hinge. Its local directional component penalizes infinitesimal collapse of D(Φ ◦ H), while its separated-pair component penalizes overlap of states that are a prescribed distance apart in the observable-state metric. The penalty is one-sided: it vanishes after the desired local and global margins are reached. It therefore does not require a decoder, inverse model, or reconstruction objective. The two components have antecedents in conditioning and lower co-Lipschitz regularization; the contribution here is the finite-sample control theorem obtained from their joint enforcement. The central novelty is the direction and completeness of the implication from learning to con-trol. Existing work separately provides finite-sample JEPA generalization and planning-regret bounds, identifiability results for structured world models, planning-aligned latent objectives, and analyses of prediction–control mismatch. Our result addresses a different theorem-level question: when does the approximately optimized empirical training objective itself force the learned representation and transi-tion map to become a quantitatively faithful deterministic control model? More precisely, the analysis proves the chain finite-sample empirical local–global metric control =⇒ pointwise co-Lipschitzness =⇒ uniform controlled semiconjugacy =⇒ worst-case finite-horizon optimizer transfer .

Among the neighboring results discussed in Section 2, we are not aware of one that starts from an approximately optimized empirical local–global metric objective and proves this entire chain for non-linear deterministic controlled dynamics. Thus the result is neither only an anti-collapse statement nor only a planning-regret statement: it is a finite-sample realization theorem specifying when a learned latent model becomes a metrically faithful approximate control model. The same geometric constant produced by the representation theorem enters the downstream Lipschitz-cost and planning bounds, while approximation, statistical, training-optimization, cost-head, and planner errors remain explicit and separate. Relation to existing non-collapse and abstraction mechanisms 

This section isolates the precise theorem-level novelty relative to the closest existing work. Collapse prevention, state abstraction, lower co-Lipschitz regularization, latent dynamics, and planning-aware representation learning all have clear precedents. The key novelty of the paper is the proved implication from an approximately optimized finite-sample metric-regularized objective to pointwise non-collapse, then to uniform nonlinear controlled semiconjugacy, and finally to a deterministic worst-case optimizer-transfer guarantee, with an explicit admissible regularization range and the approximation, statistical, optimization, cost-head, and planner errors kept separate. 

Stop-gradient and architectural asymmetry: Some typical methods are BYOL [10] and Sim-Siam [11]. These methods are highly effective empirically, but their objective structure does not directly impose a lower co-Lipschitz metric bound. Even when total collapse is avoided, the representation may still identify or fold states that have different future consequences. 

Redundancy reduction and covariance spread: Some typical methods are Barlow Twins [12] and VICReg [13]. These methods impose decorrelation, variance, or covariance-spread conditions on the latent distribution. Such conditions control the second moments of Φ( H(s)). They can exclude the constant encoder, but they are distributional rather than pointwise. In particular, a representation may have large variance while being non-injective. 

Bisimulation, abstraction, and DeepMDP: State abstraction and bisimulation [14, 15, 16] study equivalence relations that preserve relevant aspects of future prediction, transitions, and rewards. Bisimulation metrics [17, 18] quantify approximate state equivalence and connect model similarity to value similarity. DeepMDP [19] gives a learned latent-space version via reward and latent-transition prediction losses. DBC [20, 21] directly fits the bisimulation metric into the latent space. Later works analyze embedding-collapse and embedding-explosion pathologies, including the uninformative-reward regime. We focus on whether the learned encoder is metrically faithful on the quotient in the pointwise lower co-Lipschitz sense. 

Bi-Lipschitz encoders and injectivity regularization: BLAE [22] identifies encoder non-injectivity as a core bottleneck and proposes a separation criterion with a bi-Lipschitz relaxation. DKL 6 ALAIN BENSOUSSAN, MINH-NHAT PHUNG, AND MINH-BINH TRAN 

[23] applies related techniques including spectral normalization and frequency-analysis-based justifica-tions for distance-preserving ResNets. The present paper adds an action-conditioned forward objective, a deterministic controlled-semiconjugacy estimate, an explicit finite-sample analysis, and an admissible regularization range. 

Recent quantitative JEPA and controlled-world-model theory: Cui et al. [24] formulate JEPA pretraining as conditional spectral graph learning and derive a finite-sample connection between JEPA risk and downstream planning regret. Klindt, LeCun, and Balestriero [25] prove linear and approximate identifiability for LeJEPA under stationary additive-noise latent dynamics and connect orthogonal identifiability to optimal latent planning. Zhang et al. [26] establish joint representation– transition identifiability for controlled world models under structured Gaussian latent and behavior-policy assumptions, including approximate-identification bounds. These results make broad claims such as “the first finite-sample JEPA theory connected to planning” untenable; our claim is stronger in a different direction: finite-sample empirical metric regularization is converted into pointwise geometric and uniform deterministic-control certificates. 

Planning-aligned latent geometry and prediction–control mismatch: Several recent works make the planning role of latent geometry explicit. Value-Guided JEPA [27] shapes representation distance using a goal-conditioned reaching value; RC-aux [28] supplements local predictive training with multi-horizon and budget-conditioned reachability supervision; Temporal-Distance JEPA [29] co-designs a temporal-progress signal with latent MPC; ProWorld [30] imposes progress-aware hyperbolic geometry; and ActSWM [31] targets action-sensitive transition separation. Bhand and Joshi [32] study how multi-horizon consistency changes latent transition geometry, while Wang [33] derives structure-dependent rollout-horizon certificates for equivariant world models. You et al. [34] show that data-averaged prediction error need not govern control because planning probes a different distribution, and Smolyanskiy [35] documents a related empirical decoupling between validation loss and closed-loop performance. The sharp distinction with the current work is the direction of implication: here the training objec-tive itself yields a pointwise lower metric certificate and a uniform controlled-semiconjugacy certificate, and those certificates are then propagated into a worst-case finite-horizon optimizer-transfer bound for nonlinear deterministic dynamics. To our knowledge, none of the works above proves this complete finite-sample objective ⇒ geometry ⇒ controlled dynamics ⇒ optimizer-transfer chain. Conversely, their value shaping, reachability supervision, stochastic identifiability, off-distribution analysis, equiv-ariance, and long-horizon architectural mechanisms address questions outside the scope of our theorem. 

JEPA-specific non-collapse and planning results: Yu et al. [36] prove that, in deterministic MDPs, if both the JEPA latent-transition consistency loss and an auxiliary regression loss reach zero, observations that are inequivalent with respect to the transition dynamics or auxiliary regression target must receive distinct latent representations. An idealized variant of TD-JEPA [37] avoids collapse under proper initialization and learns a low-rank factorization of long-term policy dynamics, while its predictor recovers successor features. VJEPA [38] establishes JEPA representations as sufficient information states for control. LeWorldModel [39] achieves end-to-end JEPA training with a Gaussian regularizer. Bai and Xiong’s Temporal-Distance JEPA [29] mines a directed temporal-progress cost from offline trajectories and co-designs that cost with latent model-predictive control; its contribution is empirical and plan-aware rather than a pointwise metric or semiconjugacy theorem. 3. Deterministic controlled systems and the observable quotient 

In this section, we mathematically formalize the deterministic system and the observable quotient. First, let the state space S ⊂ Rds be a compact convex Euclidean domain with nonempty interior, and let the action space A ⊂ Rda be a compact Euclidean domain. No smoothness of ∂S is imposed. Whenever derivatives up to order C1,1 are used, the relevant maps are assumed to admit C1,1 extensions to open Euclidean neighborhoods of their compact domains. The metric spaces ( S, d S ) and ( A, d A) are Euclidean, with 

dS (s, s ′) := |s − s′|, dA(a, a ′) := |a − a′|.

The state-action space M = S × A is equipped with the usual product metric 

dM (( s, a ), (s′, a ′)) := dS (s, s ′) + dA(a, a ′).

The dimension of the state-action space is denoted by dm := ds + da.The true deterministic dynamics and observation map are 

st+1 = G(st, a t), xt = H(st),

where 

G : S × A → S, H : S → X. 

Here X is a separable Banach space, typically Rdx with dx ≫ ds. We assume throughout that G and 

H are Lipschitz. In the metric-regularized results, Lemma 7.3 and those that follow, the neighborhood extensions stipulated above are assumed to be of class C1,1.If H is not injective, no representation built from observations can recover more than the information contained in controlled observation histories. The correct object is therefore an observable quotient.

Why deterministic uniform semiconjugacy is the right downstream object 

A reasonable reader may grant that Theorem 7.15 is a clean quantitative statement and still ask: 

why this object? Most existing latent-model results provide value-error bounds in expectation (Asadi– Misra–Littman [44]), bisimulation-distance bounds (DeepMDP [19], DBC [20]), or L2-prediction-loss guarantees. Theorem 7.15 instead delivers a pointwise lower co-Lipschitz constant c∗ and a uniform supremum-norm semiconjugacy modulus ηW,n,δ (εtrain ), with the training optimization error tracked separately from the downstream planning error. This section argues that this particular conclusion is not a convenience of the proof but the object actually needed by deterministic planning, MPC, reacha-bility, and Lyapunov-based safety analysis. We make the argument in four steps: (i) the deterministic simulation lemma converts η into a uniform trajectory and cost bound with a transparent geometric constant; (ii) the representation theorem tracks the independent empirical-training error εtrain , whereas the planning theorem tracks the latent-solver error ξplan ; (iii) without additional regularity or reacha-bility assumptions, an Lp-mean residual bound does not give the same guarantee, because the planner’s argmin is selected adversarially with respect to the model error; (iv) the lower co-Lipschitz constant 

c∗ makes the original state costs well-defined and quantitatively Lipschitz on the learned latent image, while a separate corollary handles practically learned latent cost heads with errors εℓ and εg.9.1. Deterministic simulation and uniform planning transfer. Theorem 7.15 already includes the resulting finite-sample planning guarantee. We now isolate its deterministic ingredients in modular form. The simulation estimate below is the deterministic analogue of the Kearns–Singh simulation lemma [45] and of the Lipschitz model-based bound of Asadi–Misra–Littman [44].

The constant c∗ and modulus ηW,n,δ (εtrain ) of Theorem 7.15 are not arbitrary: to-gether they yield (i) a uniform finite-horizon cost-transfer estimate and a deterministic latent-optimizer suboptimality bound for compatible Lipschitz costs, (ii) the resolution of state recovery from latent codes ( §9.3), (iii) Lyapunov and practical-stability estimates after transfer, and (iv) latent-space Haus-dorff approximations of forward-reachable sets; a state-space pullback would require an additional decoder or projection assumption. Under the assumptions considered here, these worst-case conclu-sions are not controlled by an L2 prediction bound or a global variance/covariance-spread bound alone. The constants c∗ and ηW,n,δ (εtrain ) are explicit, and the planning guarantee keeps the empirical-training tolerance εtrain separate from the latent-solver tolerance ξplan . Corollary 9.4 further quantifies learned-cost errors through εℓ and εg. The guarantee holds whenever the regularization strength belongs to the admissible range of Theorem 7.15. 10. Results. Two conclusions follow, and the second corrects a reading that an unbounded implementation would have supported. First, in the ordinary initialization regime the picture is the expected one. Pure prediction collapses in 10 of 12 seeds, with a sampled lower chord ratio at the resolution of the grid. The proposed local– global hinge is noninjective in 0 of 12 seeds, with median bcmin = 0 .90, bcmax = 1 .31 and geometric condition ratio bκgeom = 1 .48: the improvement in the lower diagnostic is not bought by inflating the upper one. Covariance spread fails in 1 seed and, more tellingly, attains a median bcmin = 0 .25, roughly a third of the hinge value, so even its successful runs retain substantially less pointwise resolution. The local-only penalty fails in 1 seed and the global-only penalty in 0. Second, and less comfortably, the folded-basin stress test separates the objectives by how often optimization fails , not by the conditioning of the runs that succeed. The plain local–global hinge escapes the fold in 3 of 12 seeds and remains noninjective in 9. Conditioning on that outcome is essential, because the two groups are qualitatively different objects. The 3 successful runs are well conditioned: median bcmin = 0 .86, bcmax = 1 .27, bκgeom = 1 .47. The 9 failed runs carry the extreme upper geometry: median bcmax = 20 .78 and bκgeom = 7 .87 × 10 6, with bcmin = 2 .59 × 10 −6. An unconditional median over all 12 runs mixes the two and is not interpretable. Adding the upper directional term changes the frequency and the severity of the failure mode rather than the conditioning of the successes. Failures fall from 9 to 3 of 12; the successful runs are conditioned essentially as before ( bκgeom = 1 .78 against 1 .47); and the runs that still fail are far less violent ( bcmax =2.55 against 20 .78). So the upper penalty is not curing scale inflation in the runs that work — those were never inflated. It is making the bad optimization basin both rarer and milder. The folded-basin stress test separates successful and failed optimization outcomes. Under a common compact range, the hinge’s advantage in this regime is modest, and the two-sided audit provides the ability to tell the two outcomes apart at all: a one-sided lower diagnostic reports only that some runs have small bcmin , without revealing that those same runs are wildly expansive elsewhere. That distinction is precisely what the theorem’s uniform C1,1 budget is for. The ( bcmin , bcmax ) scatter in Figure 1 shows this directly: the runs that clear the lower margin by inflation sit high and to the right, and only the upper-controlled arm concentrates in the lower-right region that represents faithful resolution. The intended conclusion is therefore the narrower one. Under a fixed latent range and comparable upper geometry, covariance spread retains noninjectivity or severe local compression far more often than the local–global hinge, and the local-only and global-only penalties fail in distinguishable ways; but the hinge is not uniformly dominant, and without upper control it can clear a one-sided margin in a way that a two-sided audit rejects. 10.4. Discussion. The three experiments test complementary aspects of the theory, and their conclu-sions should be read at the calibration set out in (H1)–(H4). Experiment A establishes the obstruction analytically, in both the scalar and the full-rank covariance forms, and then shows what happens when the obstruction is placed in front of trained networks under a common latent range. The two-sided reporting is what makes the trained comparison meaningful: a one-sided diagnostic can be improved by scale inflation alone, and the ( bcmin , bcmax ) scatter of Figure 1 distinguishes the two. Experiment B is the finite-capacity proxy most directly motivated by the finite-sample theorem: it uses fixed samples, a norm-constrained class small enough for near-global search diagnostics, and an explicit evaluation of selected threshold ingredients. The theorem’s sufficient condition is conservative at these sample sizes, mainly because of the uniform covering estimate, so the plug-in bound is interpreted as a qualitative calibration mechanism rather than a quantitative prescription for λ.Experiment C tests whether the geometry the theory cares about is the geometry that predicts planning reliability on the true system. This experiment tests the practical relevance of the theorem by asking whether the learned latent geometry predicts planning reliability on the true system: all outcomes, including failures, are reported, and if covariance regularization plans as well as the proposed hinge on this benchmark, the conclusion is that the hinge supplies a sufficient deterministic guarantee and a sharper folding diagnostic, while relative empirical performance remains benchmark-dependent. Finally, the trained models are empirical approximations, not certified global minimizers, and the theorem’s optimization-error and uniform-budget hypotheses are not certified by these runs. Each reported b·eval supremum is over a stated finite evaluation set, with adversarial refinement from its worst points, not over the continuum. 11. Conclusion 

Forward action-conditioned JEPA prediction must be supplemented with geometric information to identify a control-relevant latent representation: the constant encoder is a zero-loss solution, and global covariance, variance, or spectral-spread constraints do not rule out non-injective foldings. The local– global metric hinge studied here targets this obstruction directly. It is encoder-only in architecture, but its evaluation uses observable-factor metric supervision through state coordinates, distances, and tangent directions. Under a regular Euclidean observable-factor assumption, lower Ahlfors coverage, uniform C1,1 bud-gets, and finite-capacity approximation, the representation theorem applies to εtrain -approximate em-pirical minimizers. For every regularization strength in an explicit one-sided admissible range, it yields a pointwise lower co-Lipschitz constant c∗ and a uniform deterministic semiconjugacy modulus

The tensor-product B-spline construction verifies the required uniform C1,1 approximation property for a concrete finite-dimensional class, and the sharpness theorem shows that the exponent 1 /(dm + 2) is intrinsic to an L2-plus-Lipschitz interpolation argument. For LF = 1, the undiscounted running-cost contribution has order ηT 2/c ∗; for LF < 1, the terminal rollout discrepancy is uniformly bounded in the horizon and the accumulated running-cost error grows at most linearly. Learned latent cost heads are covered by the additional explicit term 2( T ε ℓ + εg). The numerical results support a correspondingly calibrated conclusion. Experiment A verifies the exact covariance and folding obstructions and shows that one-sided lower diagnostics must be accompa-nied by upper geometric control. Experiment B is a deterministic finite-capacity coefficient-box spline proxy motivated by the comparison theorem; it does not instantiate the uniform-capacity C1,1 class of Proposition 7.6, and its plug-in finite-sample threshold remains far from being satisfied at the tested sample sizes. Experiment C shows that the smallest latent prediction residual can occur for a col-lapsed representation with substantially poorer closed-loop control. The combined geometry/residual diagnostic separates collapse from non-collapse in the tested setting, but the experiments do not es-tablish a reliable ordering among covariance spread, the local–global hinge, and the two-sided baseline. Thus the numerical evidence supports the necessity and diagnostic relevance of metric non-collapse, not numerical verification of the full theorem or universal empirical superiority of the proposed regularizer. Recent work now supplies finite-sample JEPA planning-regret theory, structured identifiability re-sults, planning-aligned latent geometries, reachability and action-sensitivity objectives, and analyses or certificates of long-horizon prediction–control mismatch [24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 34, 33, 31, 30]. The con-tribution of the present paper is precise: an approximately optimized finite-sample local–global metric objective is shown to force pointwise co-Lipschitz geometry, which yields a uniform nonlinear controlled semiconjugacy and then a deterministic worst-case finite-horizon optimizer-transfer guarantee. The dis-tinctive result is therefore the complete objective-to-geometry-to-dynamics-to-control implication, not a broad priority claim about the importance of latent geometry for planning.
