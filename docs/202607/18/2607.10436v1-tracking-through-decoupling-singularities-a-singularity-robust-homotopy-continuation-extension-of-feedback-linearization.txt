# Tracking Through Decoupling Singularities: A Singularity-Robust Homotopy-Continuation Extension of Feedback Linearization 

## Alex Borisevich July 14, 2026 

Abstract 

Input–output feedback linearization fails at decoupling singularities, where the decoupling matrix loses rank, the relative degree is lost, and the linearizing control becomes unbounded. This paper develops a singularity-robust trajectory-tracking controller for square nonlinear control-affine systems that tracks through isolated decoupling singularities with bounded control. The method recasts tracking as real-time arc-length homotopy continuation, equivalently a continuous-time Newton/Davidenko flow, and replaces the inverse decoupling matrix by the least-norm Moore–Penrose solution of an augmented matrix A = [Λ | b], where b is the homotopy direction. A transversality condition wT b̸ = 0 , with w in the left null space of the decoupling matrix, keeps the augmented matrix full row rank through a generic rank-one loss. The resulting flow agrees with feedback linearization away from the singular set, tracks with O(1 /k ) error, and re-locks after each crossing. The theory also characterizes the reflection-versus-branch-crossing dichotomy at Whitney folds and relates the reflection case to a Filippov sliding mode. Extensions cover dynamic relative-degree-one minimum-phase systems and arbitrary relative degree via filtered-error reduction. Simulations include a redundant 2-DOF manipulator, relative-degree-one and relative-degree-two plants, and a dual-active-bridge series-resonant DC/DC converter, where the method performs bounded inversion across buck/boost and resonance singularities while preserving zero-voltage soft switching. 

Keywords: feedback linearization; loss of relative degree; decoupling matrix; decoupling singularity; nonlinear trajectory tracking; homotopy continuation; arc-length continuation; continuous-time Newton (Davidenko) method; augmented Jacobian; least-norm inverse; transversality; singularity-robust control; damped least squares; singularity-robust inverse kinematics; kinematic singularity; redundant manipula-tors; resolved-rate control; Type-2 parallel-mechanism singularity crossing; Filippov sliding mode; Whit-ney fold; monodromy; minimum phase; series resonant converter; dual-active-bridge; DC–DC converter; zero-voltage switching (ZVS); soft switching. 

# 1 Introduction 

Feedback linearization is the standard route to tracking control for nonlinear systems with a well-defined relative degree [1, 2, 3]. For a square control-affine plant of relative degree one it inverts the decoupling 1arXiv:2607.10436v1 [eess.SY] 11 Jul 2026 matrix Λ( x) = Dh (x) g(x) to cancel the nonlinearity and impose linear output dynamics. The construction is, however, intrinsically partial : the linearizing law u = Λ −1(v − a) is defined only off the decoupling singularity (equivalently, the locus of relative-degree loss) D = {det Λ( x) = 0 }, and the commanded control grows without bound as the trajectory approaches D. The same obstruction appears kinematically as the manipulator-Jacobian singularity of resolved-rate control, and dynamically as the Type-2 (parallel) singularity of closed-chain mechanisms. This paper develops a controller that tracks a reference through isolated decoupling singularities with bounded effort, using one formula and no case analysis. The idea is to embed the tracking problem in an arc-length homotopy continuation and to replace the partial inverse Λ−1 by the least-norm (Moore– Penrose) solution of the augmented matrix A = [Λ | b], where b is the homotopy direction. Augmenting 

Λ with the extra column b repairs an isolated rank drop precisely when a transversality condition holds, and the resulting flow crosses the singularity at finite velocity while retaining the accuracy of feedback linearization elsewhere.

Singularity-robust inverse kinematics. The closest antecedent is the damped-least-squares (DLS), or singularity-robust, inverse of the manipulator Jacobian introduced by Nakamura and Hanafusa [4] and Wampler [5], and refined into task-priority and variable-damping schemes by Chiaverini and co-workers [6, 7]; see [8] for a review. DLS replaces J−1 by JT (JJ T + η2I)−1, trading tracking accuracy for boundedness in a neighbourhood of the singularity, with the damping η scheduled on the smallest singular value. Our construction differs in three respects. First, boundedness comes from the geometry of the augmented map A = [Λ | b] — the extra homotopy column, not an added damping term — and is exact rather than approximate at the singular instant. Second, away from the singularity the controller equals feedback linearization up to O(1 /k ) (Proposition 6), so unlike DLS it introduces no accuracy penalty in the regular region. Third, the augmentation supplies a checkable transversality criterion (Definition 1) that decides in advance whether a given singularity is crossable, and a clean characterization of what the extra degree of freedom does (reflection versus crossing), which damping does not provide. The price is that the method addresses isolated decoupling singularities along the path rather than dense singular regions. Beyond uniform damping, refinements include selectively damped least squares, which adapts the damping per singular direction [21], and hierarchical / task-priority formulations solved as (inequality-constrained) quadratic programs [22, 23]; more recently, predictive redundancy-resolution schemes attain singularity-free tracking by embedding the kinematics in a receding-horizon program [24]. All of these avoid 

or regularize the singularity — keeping the trajectory away from it or damping the response near it — whereas the present method drives the output through an isolated decoupling singularity while coinciding with the exact inverse away from it. 

Continuation and continuous-time Newton methods. Writing the root-finding flow ˙x = −[Df ]−1 ˙y

as a differential equation is the continuous Newton (Davidenko) method [9], and following a deformed solution curve is the subject of predictor–corrector and arc-length continuation [10]. In numerical algebraic geometry, generic-parameter homotopies avoid the discriminant locus with probability one (the “ γ-trick”), meeting singularities only at isolated points [11]. Our controller is an arc-length continuation in which the homotopy parameter is driven by real time and the augmentation plays exactly the discriminant-avoidance role, but with two control-specific additions: the transversality analysis of the augmented Jacobian at a genuine rank drop, and the locking feedback that regulates the parameter error e = t − μ to O(1 /k ).When a path does approach a singular endpoint, numerical algebraic geometry handles it with endgames 

— power-series or Cauchy-integral extrapolation — and with certified predictor–corrector tracking [30, 31]. Our transversality condition plays the complementary role of keeping the flow off the discriminant in the first place, so that a single least-norm step suffices at the crossing and no endgame is required. 

Continuation in control. Continuation has been used in control most prominently in Ohtsuka’s Con-tinuation/GMRES method for nonlinear model-predictive control [13], where the KKT system is tracked in time by a continuation flow. The present homotopy-for-tracking construction and its setpoint pre-decessor were surveyed by the author [14]; that note develops the “auxiliary controller as a drift term” idea for regulation but leaves the trajectory-tracking theory, the transversality/crossing analysis, and the 3singularity guarantees incomplete. The present paper completes and supersedes that line: it gives the augmented-Jacobian kernel lemma, the bounded-crossing theorem, the reflection/crossing dichotomy, and the dynamic and higher-relative-degree generalizations that were missing there. 

Crossing Type-2 singularities of parallel mechanisms. For closed-chain mechanisms it is known that a Type-2 (parallel) singularity can be crossed if the applied wrench is reciprocal to the twist along the uncontrollable direction — a dynamic criterion developed by Arakelian, Briot and co-workers, first with pre-planned optimal trajectories and later with workspace-enlarging and virtual-constraint controllers [16, 17, 18]. Our transversality condition wT b̸ = 0 is the continuation-flow counterpart of that reciprocity criterion: the homotopy direction b must have a nonzero component along the uncontrollable mode w.The difference is that our criterion arises from a purely kinematic rank argument on the augmented map, requires no inverse dynamic model, and comes with an explicit tracking-error guarantee rather than only a feasibility condition. This line has since moved from pre-planned optimal trajectories to controllers that cross without an off-line trajectory, through virtual-constraint and multi-model computed-torque designs [17, 28], while a parallel body of work instead avoids Type-2 configurations, detecting the responsible limbs via the angle between output twist screws [29]. The present contribution is on the crossing side but purely kinematic, and the same transversality test carries over verbatim to serial decoupling singularities and to the dynamic control-affine setting of Section 8. 

Control through loss of relative degree. Within nonlinear control proper, the decoupling singularity 

det Λ = 0 is precisely the loss of (vector) relative degree, at which exact input–output linearization is undefined. Two classical responses are approximate input–output linearization, which discards the ill-defined part of the dynamics in a neighbourhood of the singularity [25], and switching between control laws valid on either side of it [26]. More recent work retains a prioritized normal form so that a singular sub-block of the input-gain matrix does not propagate to higher-priority channels [27]. The continuation controller is a third route: rather than approximating, switching, or re-prioritizing, it augments the decoupling matrix with the homotopy direction and takes the least-norm solution, which remains bounded at the singularity under transversality and reduces to exact feedback linearization away from it (Proposition 6). 

Feedback linearization and flatness. The method sits inside the input–output linearization frame-work [1, 2, 3]: it presupposes a well-defined relative degree and minimum-phase zero dynamics, and does not manufacture either where absent. In particular it is orthogonal to dynamic feedback linearization and flatness [19], which enlarge the linearizable class by dynamic extension; the continuation idea wraps a (pos-sibly extended) system and repairs its decoupling singularities, a distinct obstruction from non-flatness. The relation is made precise in Section 9: the controller is a singularity-robust extension of feedback linearization, identical to it off D and bounded on it. 43 Problem setup and feedback linearization

Remark 3 (Why n ≥ 2 is needed to see the effect) . In the scalar toy example ( n = 1 ) a rank-(n − 1) point means Df = 0 , the left null vector is w = 1 , and transversality reads f (x0) − y∗̸ = 0 , which holds whenever the target differs from the anchor. The null space ker Df = R is the whole line, so there is no separate uncontrollable submanifold and the “wandering” motion of item 1 is invisible. The genuine difficulty — bounded but off-task motion along ker Df during the crossing — first appears for n ≥ 2, e.g. the 2-DOF arm, where ker Df is a proper subspace.

Constraint stabilization. All runs integrate the continuation flow (48) in real time with a fixed-step RK4 scheme, with one numerical safeguard worth stating. The flow only conserves the homo-topy constraint: it enforces ˙H = 0 , so that H ≡ 0 holds identically along the exact trajectory once

But {H = 0 } is a marginally stable invariant with no restoring action, so the local error the integrator commits at the stiff singular crossings accumulates as a slow drift off the manifold, corrupting the output identity f (x) − y∗ = e b . 10.2.4 Choice of orientation: when to spend the null-space DOF on a crossing 

The two experiments make it clear that the orientation of ˆn is not a free stylistic choice but the selection of which solution branch of the tracking problem to follow. Because the kernel of A is one-dimensional, the single available degree of freedom must be spent either on advancing (and locking) the homotopy parameter 34 μ or on steering the configuration through the null space of Df to change branch; at a singularity these two uses are mutually exclusive (Remark 2). We summarize when each is appropriate. 

Default: ρ ≥ 0 (reflection). The orientation of Theorem 1 is the greedy, minimal-reconfiguration policy: it keeps μ monotone and the feedback −kρ e dissipative, so the tracking error stays bounded and re-locks. Whenever the current branch admits a continuation as t advances — which is generic, since a redundant inverse (e.g. both elbow assemblies of the arm) realizes the same output y∗(t) on either side of the singularity — this choice is not merely acceptable but preferable: it preserves the tracking guarantee and never reconfigures the mechanism. In this regime no additional information is required; the controller simply stays on its branch. 

Forced crossing: the branch dies. The ρ ≥ 0 policy is feasible only while the branch being tracked continues to exist. If the reference drives the flow to a genuine turning point in real time — a point where the solution on the current branch terminates rather than merely grazing the singularity — then maintaining ρ ≥ 0 would require ˙μ < 0, which the orientation forbids; the flow stalls or the velocity diverges, as in Proposition 1. Here a crossing is not a choice but a necessity, and by Remark 2 it can be executed with bounded effort only along a crossing-compatible reference ( wT b bounded away from zero). A practical detector follows directly from the error dynamics (49): if |e| begins to grow despite the ρ ≥ 0

orientation, the branch is dying and a crossing is being forced. 

Discretionary crossing: a secondary criterion. When both reflection and crossing are feasible — the typical redundant case — the output reference y∗(t) and the constraint H = 0 contain no information favouring one branch over the other, since both track the output exactly. Any preference for a working-mode change must therefore originate outside the tracking objective, from a secondary criterion such as joint limits, obstacle or self-collision avoidance, manipulability, actuator effort, or an explicitly commanded posture. This exposes the price of the greedy default: ρ ≥ 0 is locally optimal but not globally, and by never reconfiguring it may steer the system toward a constraint that an earlier, deliberate crossing would have avoided. These three cases suggest a two-level controller. The inner continuation flow (48) realizes the primary tracking objective and, under the ρ ≥ 0 orientation, comes with the boundedness and re-locking guarantees of Theorem 1. A supervisory layer monitors branch viability (through the growth of |e|) and any secondary criterion, and only when one of these dictates does it override the orientation to spend the null-space degree of freedom on a crossing — ideally after shaping the reference to be crossing-compatible so that the reversal is smooth rather than violent. Formalizing this supervisor, and the second-degree-of-freedom augmentation that would let μ-locking and branch selection proceed simultaneously, is left for future work.

## 11.6 Discussion 

The converter exhibits, in a single plant, both faces of Lemma 1: a transversal crossing at the buck/boost fold and a transversal reflection at the resonance reach-boundary, mirroring respectively the relative-degree-two crossing of Section 10.4 and the maximum-reach reflection of the arm. The factorization 

det J = ( ∂σ/∂q )( ∂W/∂ω ) shows the two singular mechanisms are independent — the fold is a property of the (q, β ) → (σ, δ ) sub-map, resonance a property of the ω → W channel — and the augmented construc-tion repairs each without special-casing. Two engineering lessons follow directly from the theory. First, transversality is a reference-shaping condition: anchoring the homotopy away from the mode boundary keeps wT b̸ = 0 , and violating it reproduces the Proposition 1 failure. Second, the ρ ≥ 0 orientation encodes the physically safe operating choice — stay above resonance, on the incoming mode — so the boundedness guarantee of Theorem 1 and the correct engineering behaviour coincide. Finally, this is the static-inversion application (Sections 5–7): the controller wraps the converter’s steady-state characteristic and outperforms the closed-form and feedback-linearizing inverses at both sin-gularities. Extending it to a genuine dynamic averaged converter model — where the relative-degree-one theory of Section 8 applies with Λ = Dh g , and where minimum-phaseness of the output-filter and load zero dynamics must be certified (and can fail in deep boost) — is the natural next step. 

# 12 Future directions 

Three extensions stand out. The first two are largely worked out and near-term; the third is a conjecture that we state carefully, as it concerns the ultimate scope of the approach. A single design quantity ties them together: the number p of continuation parameters, which sets the dimension of the null space of the augmented Jacobian A = [Λ | b1 · · · bp] ∈ Rm×(m+p) and is spent jointly on outer-loop control and on transversal repair of singularities. 45 12.1 Arbitrary auxiliary controller in the null space 

The proportional locking law γ = k e is only the simplest choice. The null-space coefficient γ is a virtual control for the scalar locking-error subsystem 

˙e = 1 − d(x, t ) − ρ(x, t ) γ, (77) so any stabilizing compensator may be substituted. Integral action, γ = kpe + ki

R e, drives e → 0 exactly and removes the O(1 /k ) bias of Theorem 1, giving asymptotically exact tracking away from singularities; lead–lag, H∞, adaptive, or nonlinear designs shape the transient and reject disturbances in the drift 

d and gain ρ. This recovers, in the present tracking setting, the “auxiliary controller as a drift term” construction of the antecedent setpoint work, and it exposes a clean separation of concerns: singularity handling is intrinsic (through the augmentation and the authority ρ), while outer-loop performance is a free design in γ. The one structural limit is that ρ → 0 at the singular instant caps every auxiliary controller identically there — the bounded excursion of Theorem 1(3) is set by the geometry, not by the outer loop. 

## 12.2 Multi-parameter homotopy and higher-corank singularities 

Introducing p > 1 continuation parameters enlarges the augmented Jacobian to A = [Λ | b1 · · · bp] with a p-dimensional kernel. At a decoupling singularity of corank r (rank Λ = m − r), A regains full rank m

provided the added directions b1, . . . , b p span the r missing directions; generically p ≥ r suffices. Thus p

parameters transversally repair singularities of corank up to p, strictly enlarging the admissible singular set beyond the corank-one case treated here, while the same p-dimensional null space supplies the freedom for the auxiliary control of Section 12.1. There is a precise precedent in numerical algebraic geometry: a generic parameter homotopy avoids the discriminant (singular) locus with probability one, meeting it only at isolated points, the added parameters lifting the solution path off the singular set (the “gamma trick”). The parameter μ and anchor b play exactly this role here, and multi-parameter homotopy is the systematic version. A complementary direction concerns higher-order (non-fold) degeneracies that remain corank one but violate the Whitney nondegeneracy (50) — the cusp Y = ξ3 and its successors in the Thom–Boardman hierarchy. There the local model is no longer quadratic, so the reflection/crossing dichotomy of Theorem 2 acquires additional branches; characterizing the continuation flow through such points, and validating it on a Whitney cusp, is left to future work. 

## 12.3 Beyond a fixed morphism: flowing between linearizations (conjecture) 

Feedback linearization is a global object: it requires a linearizing morphism — a diffeomorphism together with static or dynamic feedback — whose existence is an integrability condition (involutivity in the static case, flatness in the dynamic case). The continuation controller, by contrast, requires only pointwise, path-local regularity of the augmented map along the trajectory, rank[Λ | b] = m, which is strictly weaker. We therefore conjecture that the homotopy viewpoint — a continuous deformation between linearizing 46 morphisms, rather than commitment to a single one — can regulate references and operating regions for which no global linearizing morphism exists, provided a regular augmented solution path exists in a suitably prolonged (jet) space. Concretely, building the homotopy on a dynamic extension that augments u

and its derivatives would let continuation replace exact inversion within the flatness/dynamic-linearization machinery, with the pointwise rank condition taking the place of global integrability. Two boundaries must be respected, and they delimit the conjecture. First, the approach does nothing for the non-minimum-phase obstruction: it regulates the output along a path but does not stabilize unstable zero dynamics. Second, it cannot manufacture controllability : transversal (removable) rank drops are repairable, but a genuine loss of control authority in a required direction is not. Within these limits, characterizing exactly which non-feedback-linearizable systems admit a regular augmented path — the true “width” of the class reachable by flowing between morphisms — is the central open problem this work points to. 

# 13 Conclusion 

We presented a homotopy-continuation controller that tracks references through isolated decoupling singu-larities of square, relative-degree-one systems, and, via the filtered-error reduction, of any uniform relative degree. Replacing the partial inverse Λ−1 by the least-norm solution of the augmented map A = [Λ | b]

turns a rank-(m−1) singularity into a regular point of A whenever the transversality condition wT b̸ = 0 

holds; the resulting flow crosses with bounded control and O(1 /k ) tracking error and re-locks afterwards (Theorem 1), and its reflection-versus-crossing behaviour at a fold is fixed by the kernel orientation, the two lifts differing by the Z/2 monodromy of the fold cover (Theorem 2). Two statements delimit the contribution. The method does not enlarge the structural class of regulable systems: it presupposes a well-defined relative degree, requires minimum phase for internal boundedness, and depends on the model through the same Lie derivatives as feedback linearization — indeed, off the singular set it is feedback linearization up to O(1 /k ) (Proposition 6). What it does enlarge is the admissible operating envelope of a given system: it regulates along trajectories that traverse decoupling singularities at which both static and dynamic feedback linearization produce unbounded control, the dynamic-systems analogue of what damped least squares provides over the plain Jacobian inverse. This was demonstrated on the arm, the dynamic relative-degree one and two plants, and the resonant converter, with feedback-linearization demand reaching 10 5–10 7 while the continuation control stayed bounded. The gain is genuine but bounded: at the singular instant the uncontrollable direction w (wT Λ = 0 ) is uncontrollable for any controller, so the method fails gracefully, with a bounded and recoverable error, rather than diverging. Lifting these limits — multi-parameter augmentation for higher-corank singularities, and the open question of which non-feedback-linearizable systems admit a regular augmented path — is the programme set out in Section 12.
